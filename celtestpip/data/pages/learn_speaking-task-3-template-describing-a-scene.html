Practise Speaking Task 3

CELPIP Speaking Task 3 (Describing a Scene) in short: you get 30 seconds to prepare and 60 seconds to describe a picture to someone who cannot see it. Start with one general statement, then describe a few things well: what the people look like, what they are doing and how they seem to feel. CELPIP's own advice is to be selective, so you do not have to cover the whole picture.

CELPIP Speaking Task 3: Describing a Scene + Full 60-Second Answer

The full CELPIP Speaking Course is free: CELPIP Speaking Course · More lessons on the CELTESTPIP YouTube channel

How long is CELPIP Speaking Task 3?

You get 30 seconds to prepare and 60 seconds to speak. The clock starts on its own and there is no pause button, which is why the first sentence has to be automatic rather than invented on the spot.

CELPIP's 2026 Speaking Pro study pack lists the preparation and speaking time for every task.

Only Tasks 1 and 7 get 90 seconds. Everything else is a minute. If you have been practising Task 3 with a 90-second timer because a study group told you to, you have been rehearsing an answer that runs 50 percent long, and on test day the recording will cut you off mid-sentence.

Sixty seconds is roughly 130 to 150 spoken words at a comfortable pace. That is four or five sentences of real content, not ten.

What does the Task 3 screen ask you to do?

In Task 3 you look at an illustration and talk about what is going on in it. The instruction on screen asks you to describe some of what is happening, and it tells you the person you are talking to cannot see the picture.

Two ideas in that instruction do a lot of work. The word some is permission to be selective. The fact that your listener cannot see the picture is the reason your spatial language matters at all: if you say "he is over there", your listener has nothing to attach that to.

So nobody is going to ask you to describe every object in the frame.

What is the difference between a Level 8 and a Level 10 Task 3 answer?

Our example, not a CELPIP sample. The two answers below were written by CELTESTPIP to show the gap. CELPIP did not rate them, and the levels are our teachers' estimate. The picture: a public library on a weekday afternoon. Both answers show roughly the first 30 seconds.

Around Level 8 (our example): "This picture shows a library. There are a lot of people inside. At the front there is a woman working at the desk, and she is helping a man. On the left there are some children, they are reading books on the floor. On the right side there is a man using a computer. There are many shelves with books in the back."

Around Level 10 (our example): "This is a public library on what looks like a quiet weekday afternoon. In the foreground, a librarian is scanning a stack of books at the checkout desk for an elderly man, who seems happy to chat with her. On the left, three young children are sitting cross-legged on a round rug, and one of them is turning the pages of a picture book while the others lean in to see. On the right, a young man in a hoodie is typing quickly on his laptop. He looks as if he is racing a deadline."

What changed between the two:

  • The opening. The higher answer sets the place and the mood in one line. The lower one only names the place.
  • Detail. The lower answer names a person and moves on: "a man using a computer". The higher one adds what he is doing and how he seems.
  • Words. "Checkout desk", "cross-legged" and "scanning" instead of "the desk", "the floor" and "working".
  • Sentences. The lower answer repeats "there is" and "there are". The higher one mixes in "who seems", "while" and "as if".

Notice that neither answer covers the whole picture. The stronger one picks a few things and says more about each, which is exactly what the zone templates below train.

Goal

Describe a picture in detail to a person who cannot see it. You must systematically cover the Who, Where, and What while demonstrating precise spatial vocabulary and accurate grammar.

What CELPIP actually rates on Task 3

CELPIP Speaking is rated on four dimensions: Content/Coherence, Vocabulary, Listenability and Task Fulfillment. Those four names come from CELPIP's own Speaking Performance Standards, and they apply to all eight tasks, Task 3 included.

01Content/Coherence

What it means on a picture task

Whether your description holds together. Did you move through the picture in an order a listener can follow, or jump around?

02Vocabulary

What it means on a picture task

Whether you named things precisely. Barn beats building. Chopping beats doing.

03Listenability

What it means on a picture task

Whether you are easy to listen to. Pace, pausing, clear pronunciation, and sentences that do not collapse halfway through.

04Task Fulfillment

What it means on a picture task

Whether you actually did the task, which here means describing what is happening, not narrating your opinion of it.

Three things worth knowing about this list, because most CELPIP prep sites get them wrong:

There is no points formula, and CELPIP publishes no weighting per dimension. A rater listens to the whole response and places it on a scale.

There is no separate Grammar score. Grammar is not one of the four dimensions: CELPIP counts grammar, and how varied your sentences are, as part of Listenability. That does not make grammar irrelevant, because broken tenses make you harder to follow, and that lands on Listenability first. But nobody is scoring your present continuous in isolation.

"Coherence and Cohesion", "Lexical Resource" and "Fluency and Pronunciation" are IELTS terms. If a template promises to fix your "Lexical Resource" on CELPIP, it was written for a different test.

If you want the fuller picture of how the four dimensions turn into a level, we cover it on the CELPIP Speaking score chart.

What do raters look at under each dimension?

Here is what each dimension covers, in our own words, and what it means on Task 3:

What strong Task 3 answers do well

These are the three habits that separate a Task 3 answer that lands from one that wanders. They are not the official criteria, they are what the criteria reward.

  • Structure (Spatial Flow)

    They want a logical path. You cannot jump randomly from left to right. You must move like a camera: Background → Foreground or Left → Right.

  • Grammar (Accuracy)

    They are listening for Present Continuous for actions (is running) and State Verbs with the "S" (he seems).

  • Vocabulary (Precision)

    You need specific nouns (barn, harvesting, chopping) rather than general ones (house, doing, cutting). Note: This vocabulary depends entirely on the specific picture, so you should practice on our website to cover all possible scenarios.

Step 1: The Opening (The Starter Phrase)

Goal: Begin clearly by identifying the setting and the general atmosphere. Do not just list items immediately.

Grammar Booster: Articles (A vs. The)

Since the examiner cannot see the image, you must introduce new items with "A" (indefinite). Once you have mentioned them, you can use "The" (definite).

Rule

Start with A. Continue with The.

Example

"I see a man. The man is working hard."

Structure

  1. 1The Setting

    Where is this? (A park, a office, a farm).

  2. 2The Atmosphere

    What is the "vibe"? (Busy, calm, chaotic, wintery).

Step 2: Organization (Spatial Indicators)

Goal: Move through the picture logically using "Signpost Words" so the listener doesn't get lost.

Grammar: Present Continuous vs. State Verbs

You must alternate between describing actions and describing impressions.

TypeGrammarUsageExample
ActionPresent ContinuousWhat is happening right now."The man is chopping wood."
Impression3rd Person SingularHow the person/object appears."He seems focused."

Grammar Alert: The "S" for 3rd Person

Even native speakers sometimes forget this. If the subject is He, She, or It, you MUST add an "S" to the state verb.

Correct

"The woman looks happy."

Incorrect

"The woman look happy."

Zone 1: The Center / Foreground

Start with the most obvious element.

Structure

  1. 1Spatial Marker

    "In the center of the picture..." / "In the foreground..."

  2. 2Action (Present Continuous)

    "Two women are riding brown horses along a dirt path."

  3. 3Impression (State Verb)

    "They seem quite relaxed as they enjoy their ride."

Zone 2: The Left Side

Move your "camera" to the left.

Structure

  1. 1Spatial Marker

    "On the left side..."

  2. 2Action (Present Continuous)

    "Two men are kneeling in a field and planting crops."

  3. 3Impression (State Verb)

    "The work looks physically demanding."

Zone 3: The Right Side

Move your "camera" to the right.

Structure

  1. 1Spatial Marker

    "On the right side..."

  2. 2Action 1 (Singular)

    "A man is chopping logs with an axe near a large red barn."

  3. 3Action 2 (Singular)

    "Behind him, a woman is picking apples from a tall tree."

  4. 4Impression (State Verb)

    "The harvest appears to be very plentiful this year."

Zone 4: The Background

Finish with the distant details.

Structure

  1. 1Spatial Marker

    "In the background..."

  2. 2Description

    "I can see a large white farmhouse and some rolling green hills."

Step 3: The Closing

Goal: A simple summary sentence to finish the description.

Example

"Overall, it seems like a very productive day at the farm."

Do you have to describe everything in the picture?

No. CELPIP's own advice for this task is to pick some details and describe them well, not to cover the whole picture. That one piece of advice contradicts most of what you will read about this task online.

CELPIP's study pack gives five strategies for Describing a Scene. Here they are in our words, with where each one sits in the template on this page:

CELPIP's strategy, in our wordsWhere it is in the template
Open with one sentence about the scene as a whole.Step 1: the opening line, with the setting and the atmosphere
Then choose a few details and talk about them properly. Full coverage is not expected.Step 2: a few zones, each described properly
Help your listener see the scene in their head, including where things are.The spatial markers: in the foreground, on the left side, in the background
Choose words that paint the scene clearly.Precise nouns and verbs: "chopping", not "doing"
Talk about the people: how they look, what they are doing and how they seem to feel.The Action and Impression lines in every zone

Source: CELPIP Speaking Pro: Target 9+ Study Pack (2026).

Read that order again. General statement first, then a few details, described well. Nowhere does it ask for coverage.

This matters because the most common way people lose marks on Task 3 is a breathless inventory. Sixty seconds of "and there is a tree, and there is a bench, and there is a dog, and there is a bicycle" is a list, not a description, and a rater hears exactly that. It reads as thin on Content/Coherence and thin on Vocabulary at the same time, because listing forces you into the same four words over and over.

Three or four elements, each with an action and an impression, will always beat twelve elements named and abandoned. If you finish at 55 seconds having covered four things properly, you have done the task.

The one thing you should not do is invent. If you cannot tell whether the man is a teacher or a parent, say "he appears to be a teacher" and move on. CELPIP asks you to describe what is happening, and a small, clearly hedged inference is part of describing. A confident wrong fact is not.

Three more CELPIP Task 3 sample answers

Every answer below runs between 134 and 142 words, which is what 60 seconds sounds like at a comfortable pace. Read one aloud with a timer before you trust that number, because most people speak faster than they think under pressure and finish at 45 seconds with nothing left to say.

Each one follows the same four-zone path as the template above. The settings change. The shape does not.

1A bus stop on a rainy morning2A community centre on a weekend afternoon3A beach on a hot summer day

Sample 1: A bus stop on a rainy morning (142 words)

The picture shows a crowded bus stop on what looks like a cold, rainy morning in the city.

In the foreground, a young woman is standing under a bright yellow umbrella and checking her phone. She seems a little impatient, and she keeps glancing down the road.

On the left side, an older man in a grey raincoat is sitting on the bench with a shopping bag beside him. He looks tired, as though he has been waiting for a while.

On the right side, two students are sharing one umbrella and laughing about something. Their backpacks appear to be soaked through.

In the background, I can see a row of shops with their lights on, and a bus is approaching through the traffic.

Overall, it seems like an ordinary weekday morning, and everyone looks ready to get out of the rain.

Sample 2: A community centre on a weekend afternoon (138 words)

The picture I am looking at shows a busy community centre hall, and the atmosphere seems cheerful and a bit noisy.

In the foreground, a woman is setting out paper cups on a long folding table. She appears to be volunteering, because she is wearing a name tag.

On the left side, four children are painting at a low table covered with newspaper. One of them is holding up his picture, and he looks very proud of it.

On the right side, two men are stacking chairs against the wall. They seem to be clearing space for something, possibly a class later in the day.

In the background, I can see a large notice board covered in posters and a set of glass doors leading outside.

Overall, it looks like a well-used neighbourhood space on a busy Saturday.

Sample 3: A beach on a hot summer day (134 words)

The picture depicts a sandy beach on what appears to be a very hot summer afternoon.

In the foreground, a family is sitting under a large striped umbrella. The mother is reading a book, and a small boy beside her is digging in the sand with a plastic spade.

On the left side, two teenagers are playing volleyball near the water. They seem quite competitive, and one of them is jumping to hit the ball.

On the right side, an older couple is walking slowly along the shoreline. They look relaxed, and the woman is carrying her sandals in one hand.

In the background, I can see several small boats on the water and a line of green hills behind them.

Overall, everyone seems to be enjoying a warm, easy day by the sea.

If you want more phrasing options for the slots above, the Task 3 vocabulary and phrase list gives you alternatives for each one, and the park scene word bank further down this page gives you words for nature, people, animals and objects.

Universal Templates

Learn the shape of these lines, not the exact words. A sentence repeated word for word often sounds rehearsed, and it may not fit the picture on your screen.

For example, "This is a busy scene inside a [Place]. Everyone seems to be in a rush." would be wrong for the library picture in our Level 8 and Level 10 example above, because that room is quiet. Keep the move and change the words: "This is a public library, and it is fairly quiet. Most people are reading on their own."

Step 1: The Opening (The "Big Picture")

  • "The picture I am looking at shows a [Place: park/office/street] on a [Weather: sunny/rainy/snowy] day."
  • "This is a busy scene inside a [Place: cafe/airport/station]. Everyone seems to be in a rush."

Step 2: The "Action & Feeling" Formula

Learn this pattern and use it in every section of the image.

Pattern A (Singular Person)

  • "I see a [Person]. He/She is [Verb-ing]..."
    "...He/She looks [Adjective: happy/stressed/bored]."

Pattern B (Group of People)

  • "There are [Number] people who are [Verb-ing]..."
    "...They seem [Adjective: excited/focused/tired]."

Step 3: Spatial Transitions

  • "Moving to the left side, I can see..."
  • "Shifting to the right side, there is..."
  • "In the background, I notice..."
  • "In the foreground, right in front of me, there is..."

Where to find CELPIP Task 3 pictures to practise on

A template is useless until you have run it against pictures you have never seen before. That is the whole difficulty of Task 3: the structure is fixed, the vocabulary is not, and the vocabulary is what falls apart when the picture is unfamiliar.

CELPIP publishes a small number of sample illustrations in its free materials, and they are worth doing first because they are the closest thing to the real style. There are not many of them, and once you have described the same three pictures four times each, you are testing your memory rather than your English.

For practicing more, our Task 3 question bank holds exam-like picture prompts, covering everyday settings: markets, waiting rooms, parks, kitchens, building sites, classrooms, transport. Each one runs on the real 30-second and 60-second clocks, and your recording comes back with AI scoring on the four dimensions above, which is faster than waiting for a teacher to listen to it.

How to practise so it actually transfers:

  1. 01

    Do not look at the picture before the timer starts. Surprise is the skill you are training.

  2. 02

    Say the first sentence out loud within three seconds of the speaking clock starting. If you hesitate at the opening you will hesitate all the way through.

  3. 03

    Record yourself and listen back once. Count how many different nouns you used. Under eight in 60 seconds means you were listing, not describing.

  4. 04

    Do five different pictures rather than one picture five times. Same reason as above.

If you would rather work through the whole section in order, the CELPIP Speaking practice hub has all eight tasks in one place.

What words can you use to describe a park scene?

A park is one of the best pictures to practise on, because one image gives you people, animals, weather, objects and a lot of space to organise. The words below are grouped the way you would move through the picture. Pick two or three from each group and practise saying them in full sentences, not as a list.

Nature and scenery

foliage
Meaning: leaves and greenery on trees or plants
Example: The park is full of green foliage.
pond
Meaning: a small body of still water, smaller than a lake
Example: In the background, there's a pond where ducks are swimming.
overcast
Meaning: when clouds cover the sky and block the sun
Example: The sky is slightly overcast, so the light is soft.
meadow
Meaning: a field of grass and wildflowers
Example: To the left, there's a meadow full of wildflowers.

People and actions

jogging
Meaning: running at a slow, steady pace for exercise
Example: A woman is jogging along the path.
lounging
Meaning: relaxing in a comfortable, informal way
Example: Several people are lounging on picnic blankets.
gathering
Meaning: meeting or spending time together as a group
Example: A family is gathering near the playground.
sightseeing
Meaning: visiting a place to see its interesting features
Example: A group of tourists is sightseeing and taking photos of the fountain.

Animals and wildlife

perching
Meaning: a bird sitting or resting on something, often up high
Example: A bird is perching on a branch above the path.
fluttering
Meaning: quick, light movements of wings
Example: Butterflies are fluttering around the flower beds.
pecking
Meaning: a bird tapping or eating food with its beak
Example: Some ducks are pecking at food by the edge of the pond.

Objects and details

picnic basket
Meaning: a basket for carrying food and drinks to a picnic
Example: A family is unpacking a large picnic basket.
swing
Meaning: a seat that hangs from ropes or chains, usually in a playground
Example: Two children are having fun on the swings.
thermos
Meaning: a container that keeps drinks hot or cold
Example: One man is pouring coffee from a thermos.
gazebo
Meaning: a small, open-sided shelter in a park or garden
Example: In the distance, a group has gathered under a gazebo.

Position and space

in the foreground
Meaning: the part of the picture closest to you
Example: In the foreground, a man is sitting on a bench.
to the left
Meaning: on the left side of the picture
Example: To the left, some children are playing.
towards the back
Meaning: near the back of the picture, in the background
Example: Towards the back, families are having picnics on the grass.
alongside
Meaning: next to something, following its length
Example: A path runs alongside the pond.

A sample answer using these words (128 words)

This picture shows a busy park on a cool, overcast afternoon. In the foreground, a man is sitting on a bench reading a newspaper, while a woman is jogging along the path beside him. To the left, a family is gathering near the playground, and two children are having fun on the swings. One parent is pouring coffee from a thermos next to a large picnic basket. Towards the back, there's a small pond with ducks pecking at food by the edge, and a path runs alongside it. Birds are perching on the branches above, and the trees are full of green foliage. On the right, a few people are lounging on blankets near a white gazebo. Overall, it looks like a relaxed, peaceful day in the park.

Notice the order: the wide view first, then the foreground, the left, the back and the right, and a closing line. That is the same route as the template above, with the new words dropped into it. To practise, take any park picture and describe it out loud in 60 seconds.

Quick answers to the questions test takers ask us most.

01

How long is CELPIP Speaking Task 3?

You get 30 seconds to prepare and 60 seconds to speak. CELPIP's 2026 Speaking Pro study pack lists 30 and 60 for Task 3. Only Tasks 1 and 7 give you 90 seconds of speaking time, so a 60-second answer is the target here.

02

What does CELPIP Speaking Task 3 ask you to do?

You look at an illustration and talk about what is going on in it. The instruction on screen asks you to describe some of what is happening, and it tells you that the person you are speaking to cannot see the picture.

03

Do you have to describe everything in the picture?

No. CELPIP's own advice for this task is to pick a few details and describe them well, not to cover the whole picture. Three or four elements described properly, each with an action and an impression, score better than a rushed inventory of twelve.

04

What are CELPIP's key strategies for Speaking Task 3?

CELPIP's study pack gives five. In our words: open with one sentence about the whole scene, choose a few details and talk about them properly, help your listener see where things are, pick words that paint the scene clearly, and talk about the people (how they look, what they are doing and how they seem to feel).

05

What happens if my Task 3 answer gets cut off?

The recording stops at 60 seconds. Being cut off is not a disaster on its own. If your opening line and your best details are already said, your answer still does the task. Open with your general statement, describe your best details first, and do not rush to add a closing line.

06

What is CELPIP Speaking Task 3 scored on?

The same four dimensions as every scored Speaking task: Content/Coherence, Vocabulary, Listenability and Task Fulfillment. There is no points formula, and CELPIP publishes no weighting per dimension. There is no separate Grammar score on CELPIP Speaking: grammar is rated inside Listenability.

07

Is describing a scene Task 3 or Part 3?

It is Task 3. CELPIP's materials list eight Speaking tasks, and Describing a Scene is the third. Plenty of prep material and study groups say "part 3" instead, and for this task the two names point at the same thing. If you searched for "part 3", you are on the right page.

08

Where can I get CELPIP Task 3 pictures to practice with?
