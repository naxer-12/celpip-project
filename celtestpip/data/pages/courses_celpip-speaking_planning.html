Fast Planning for Tasks

What to write in your preparation time on each CELPIP Speaking task, how much time you actually get, and why one plan does not fit all eight.

Preparation time feels like time you are not speaking. It is the opposite. What eats your sixty seconds is not talking speed, it is stopping to decide what comes next.

Everything on this page exists to move those decisions into the quiet time before the microphone opens.

First: how much prep time do you actually get?

Most advice says thirty seconds. That is wrong for two tasks, and the difference is large.

TaskPrepSpeak
1 Giving Advice30s90s
2 Talking about a Personal Experience30s60s
3 Describing a Scene30s60s
4 Making Predictions30s60s
5 Comparing and Persuading, Part 160snone
5 Comparing and Persuading, Part 260s60s
6 Dealing with a Difficult Situation60s60s
7 Expressing Opinions30s90s
8 Describing an Unusual Situation30s60s

Two things to notice. Tasks 5 and 6 give you a full minute to prepare, which is twice what you get elsewhere, so plan to use it rather than sitting still. And Task 5 Part 1 has preparation time and no speaking time at all. You read, you compare, you choose, and then Part 2 begins. It is the strangest thing about the test and it surprises people who have never met it.

When prep time ends the screen moves on by itself, a prompt tells you to start, and recording begins. Nobody says go.

The four decisions, on every task

Whatever the task, you are deciding the same four things.

1. Who am I speaking to?

A friend, a family member, a service agent, a manager, or the public. This sets how formal you sound and how you open.

Five seconds. Do not overthink it.

2. What is the task actually asking?

Read the prompt twice, and look hard at the last sentence, because a second instruction usually hides there.

Some tasks ask you to do one thing. Some ask two. Describe what it looks like, and ask him if he would like to try making one is two jobs, not one. An answer that does the first and forgets the second has done half the task, and it will sound complete while doing it.

Count the instructions, not the sentences. Write the second one down if there is one, in two or three words, so you cannot forget it under pressure.

3. My first sentence

Write your actual opening line, not a note about it. This is the one full sentence worth writing, because the first five seconds are where people freeze.

4. My content plan

This is the part that changes by task, and it is where the old version of this page went wrong.

Two shapes, not one

Six of the eight tasks want reasons. Two want a description. Planning them the same way does not work.

Shape A: opening, supported points, wrap

Use for Tasks 1, 2, 4, 5 Part 2, 6 and 7.

You give a position, an experience or a recommendation, then support it. Two developed points is usually right for sixty seconds. Three is possible in ninety if each one stays real.

In your notes: the opening line, then two short cues, each with one concrete detail attached.

Pick your two points from different angles, or you have written one point twice. Time, cost, access, safety, fairness, reliability, comfort, and how it affects other people are eight different angles. Any two different rows will do.

Shape B: wide shot, then close-ups

Use for Tasks 3 and 8.

You are describing something. There are no reasons in this shape at all, and trying to force them in is what makes people describe a picture as though it were an argument.

Start with what the whole thing is, in one sentence. Then move through three or four parts in one direction, top to bottom or left to right, each part starting with a position word.

In your notes: what the whole thing looks like in three or four words, then the parts as numbered nouns.

Why the order matters. Your listener cannot see what you are describing, so they need the whole thing first, and only then the objects and where they sit in relation to one another. The strongest answers work in that order. Almost nobody teaches this.

Task 5 Part 1 is neither

You get sixty seconds and you do not speak. Use them to choose your option and to find your two strongest points, because Part 2 is where you have to persuade and it moves fast.

What your notes look like

Shape A, a 60-second task

Notepad
to: service agent
2 jobs? explain problem + ask for date

open: "I'm calling about the delivery that hasn't arrived."

1  waited 3 weeks - paid already
2  no tracking - called twice, no answer
wrap: ask for a date this week

Shape B, a 60-second task

Notepad
to: friend Alex
2 jobs? describe + ask if he'd try one

WIDE: piano = aquarium + plant pot

1 top - ferns
2 keys - plants between
3 front - fish, coral
4 right - sign

Nouns and fragments, never sentences. One full written sentence takes about eight seconds. You have thirty. The only sentence worth writing out is your opening line.

Number the parts. At around forty seconds many people lose their thread. The numbers are what bring you back.

Time maps

Shape A, 60 seconds

Seconds
0 to 10Opening. Your position, choice or main idea
10 to 30Point one, with one concrete detail
30 to 50Point two, from a different angle, with a detail
50 to 60Wrap. One line that answers the task

Shape A, 90 seconds

Seconds
0 to 12Opening
12 to 40Point one, developed further than you would at 60
40 to 68Point two
68 to 90Wrap, and a benefit or next step

Ninety seconds is not sixty with a faster mouth. It is room to develop, so add detail rather than a third point you cannot support.

Shape B, 60 seconds

Seconds
0 to 8Open the call. Their name, and why you are calling
8 to 18The wide shot. One sentence
18 to 48Three or four parts, each starting with a position word
48 to 55The second instruction, if the task has one
55 to 60Finish the call

If there is no second instruction, give those seven seconds to the parts.

Watch the progress bar. When it is nearly full, stop adding new points and use your wrap line.

Opening lines by task

Adapt these. Do not memorise them word for word, because a memorised opening sounds like one.

TaskAn opening that works
1 Giving Advice"I suggest two things. First…"
2 Personal Experience"Last spring at my old apartment, I had a problem with…"
3 Describing a Scene"This looks like a busy farmers market on a Saturday morning."
4 Making Predictions"Looking at the clouds and the empty stalls, I think they are about to pack up early."
5 Comparing, Part 2"I would go with the second option, mainly because…"
6 Difficult Situation"Hi, I'm calling about the booking I made for Friday. There's a problem and I'm hoping you can help."
7 Expressing Opinions"I think they should, and I will explain why."
8 Unusual Situation"Hey Sam, it's me. I'm at the community garden and I've found something you have to see."

Notice Tasks 3 and 8. Both open by saying what the whole thing is. Neither opens with a small detail, and neither asks for help.

Connectors, used lightly

You do not need one in every sentence.

Cause and result: because, so, as a result
Adding and ordering: also, first, second, then, finally
Examples: for example, for instance
Contrast: but, however, although

Four will carry almost everything you say: because, so, for example, but. Reaching for heavier ones under time pressure is where sentences fall apart.

Closings

Task typeA closing that fits
Advice"Try those two this week and I think it will feel much easier."
Personal experience"It worked out in the end, and I would do the same thing again."
Description (3 and 8)"Anyway, that's what I'm looking at. Let me know what you think."
Prediction"So I think that's what happens next."
Compare and persuade"That's why I'd go with that one."
Difficult situation"If that works, please let me know and I'll sort it out today."
Opinion"So overall, yes, I think it's worth doing."

Practice

1. Sort the eight (3 min). From memory, write which shape each task needs. Check yourself against the table above. Getting Task 3 and Task 8 into Shape B is the whole point of this page.

2. Count the instructions (5 min). Open four speaking questions and, for each one, write only how many things it asks you to do. Do not answer any of them. Most people find at least one with two instructions they had not noticed.

3. Prep only, five times (5 min). Set a 30-second timer, make the notes, and stop. Do not speak. You are training the decision, not the delivery.

4. Sixty seconds with a full minute of prep (2 min). Try a Task 6 question and use the whole sixty seconds of preparation. Most people have never used more than thirty and are surprised how much steadier the answer is.
