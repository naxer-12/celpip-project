Overview

CELPIP Writing Task 2 gives you one survey with two options. You choose one option and explain why you chose it. You write one response of about 150 to 200 words, and you have 26 minutes.

That is the whole task. There is no second question to answer, and there is no second response to write.

The video above walks through the same map this page explains, using the Food Delivery Access Survey, which is the first question on the Practice tab and is free to try. You can watch it and then write the same prompt yourself.

The basics

Time26 minutes
LengthAbout 150 to 200 words. Aim for 180.
What you writeOne response
What you decideOne option, chosen and defended

About the word count. Under about 130 words is too short to develop your reasons, and it costs you marks in Task Fulfillment. Over about 220 words, you start losing more from rushed grammar and repeated ideas than you gain from the extra content. 180 is a comfortable target that leaves you time to check your work.

What you will see on the screen

You will read a short scenario. Then you will see two options, usually labelled Option A and Option B, and an instruction that looks like this:

Choose the option that you prefer. Why do you prefer your choice? Explain the reasons for your choice. Write about 150 to 200 words.

There is a second, less common shape. Instead of Option A and Option B, the prompt asks whether you agree or disagree with a proposal. The task is the same: choose one position and defend it. Everything on this page works for both shapes. The video covers the second shape at 4:30.

There is no correct option. Nobody is checking whether you picked the better idea for the community. They are checking your English. Choose whichever option you can write about most easily, and choose it fast.

The 4-part map

Every survey response fits the same four parts. You do not need a memorised template, and a memorised template will hurt you. You need this shape.

1. Your choice, in the first line.
Say which option you prefer before you say anything else. The examiner should never have to read to the end to find out what you think.

2. Reason A, with a real example.
Give one reason, then make it concrete. A reason on its own is an opinion. A reason with a specific person, place, or number behind it is support.

3. Reason B, with a real example.
The same again, with a different reason. Two developed reasons beat four thin ones.

4. Your close, with a note about the other option.
Briefly acknowledge what someone choosing the other option would say, answer it, and return to your choice.

The video builds this map at 2:33.

The note about the other option is scored

This is the part most people leave out, and it is the part that separates a capped response from a high one.

Mentioning the other option shows the examiner that you understood the whole situation and still made a decision. That is critical thinking, and it sits inside the Content and Coherence criterion. It is not optional politeness.

Be careful with the difference here, because it is easy to get backwards:

This earns marksThis costs marks
Choosing one option, then briefly answering the strongest argument for the other oneNever choosing, or changing your mind halfway through
"Some neighbours worry about security. But that is fixable: check driver ID.""Both options have benefits and drawbacks."

One sentence is enough. Two at most. Then go back to your choice and finish.

More on this at 3:19.

How Task 2 is scored

Your response is scored on four criteria:

  • Content and Coherence. Are your ideas developed, and do they connect?
  • Vocabulary. Are your words accurate and suitable, not just long?
  • Readability. Grammar, sentence variety, punctuation, and paragraphing.
  • Task Fulfillment. Did you do what the prompt asked, completely?

All four are reported as a CELPIP level. Paragon does not publish how the four are weighted against each other, so the practical approach is to treat all four as things you cannot ignore.

The video covers the criteria at 1:48.

Rules Paragon publishes for this task

These come from the official study material, and several of them are the reason strong writers still lose marks.

Give reasons, not suggestions. The prompt asks why you prefer your option. It does not ask you to improve the plan. Writing "they should also add more parking" is answering a question nobody asked, and it takes space away from the reasons that are scored.

Do not write an academic essay. No thesis statement, no "In contemporary society," no formal essay opening. This is a survey response from a resident, an employee, or a customer. Write like one.

Never use point form. No bullet points, no numbered lists, no dashes down the page. Full sentences in paragraphs.

Do not change the given information. If the scenario says the pool closes at six, it closes at six. Inventing different facts to make your argument easier is marked as not fulfilling the task.

Use real places, people, and numbers. "An older neighbour on my floor" beats "some people." "Until nine on weekdays" beats "later." Specific detail is the cheapest way to raise your Content score.

Paraphrase the prompt, do not copy it. Reusing the scenario's exact sentences gives the examiner no evidence about your vocabulary.

End with a concluding statement. One sentence that returns to your choice. Do not stop mid-argument because time ran out.

Know your own repeat mistakes. Most people make the same three or four errors every time. Find yours, and check for those specifically in your last few minutes.

How to spend the 26 minutes

MinutesWhat you do
About 5Read the scenario. Pick your option. Write down two reasons and one concrete example for each.
About 17Write the response, following the four parts. Do not stop to edit.
About 4Check it. Read once for meaning, once for grammar, once for your own repeat mistakes.

Planning feels like time you cannot afford. It is the opposite. Five minutes of planning is what stops you from rewriting your second paragraph three times.

A full sample answer

The prompt: Food Delivery Access Survey. Your building manager is surveying residents. Option A is that delivery drivers leave orders at the front lobby desk. Option B is that drivers keep delivering to apartment doors.

A response that scores 10 or above (just under 170 words):

I support Option B: delivery drivers should keep bringing orders straight to residents' doors. Convenience matters here, but the bigger issue is that some tenants simply cannot manage a trip to the lobby every time they order.

First, door-to-door delivery protects the people who rely on it most. Elderly residents and parents with young children can't easily leave an apartment to collect a meal downstairs. On my floor, an older neighbour orders dinner most evenings because cooking is hard for her; asking her to walk down and back would take away that independence.

Second, keeping deliveries at the door avoids mix-ups and cold food. If every order piles up at one desk, meals sit out, get cold, and are easy to grab by mistake, and residents paid for warm, correct food.

Admittedly, some neighbours worry about security and evening noise. But that's fixable: check driver ID, or set delivery hours, without ending a service people depend on. For these reasons, the building should keep door-to-door delivery.

Where the four parts are:

PartIn the answer
Your choice, first line"I support Option B"
Reason A with an examplePeople who rely on it, plus the older neighbour on his floor
Reason B with an exampleMix-ups and cold food, plus what happens at one desk
Close with the note"Admittedly, some neighbours worry about security... But that's fixable"

Notice what is not in it. No long words. "Mix-ups," "rely on," and "depend on" are ordinary words used precisely. The examiner is not counting difficult vocabulary, and reaching for it is one of the fastest ways to lower your score. Common mistakes

Not choosing. Writing about both options, or changing position halfway through, is the most common reason a strong writer still misses CLB 9. Pick one in the first minute.

Reasons with nothing behind them. "It would be more convenient" is not a reason yet. Convenient for whom, doing what, at what time of day?

Suggestions instead of reasons. Improving the proposal is not the task.

Writing too much. Going well past 200 words usually means repeating an idea in different words, and every extra sentence is another chance to make a grammar error.

Copying the scenario's wording. It gives the examiner nothing to score.

No time left to check. A response with three careless errors in the last paragraph reads as a lower level than the same response checked once.

Before you submit

Six checks, and they take about four minutes:

  1. Is my choice clear in the first line?
  2. Do I have two reasons, each with something specific behind it?
  3. Did I mention the other option once, and answer it?
  4. Am I between 150 and 200 words?
  5. Are there paragraphs, and full sentences throughout?
  6. Did I check for the mistakes I know I repeat?

Practise this

Reading about the map will not move your score. Writing four or five responses and reading the feedback will.

Start with the Food Delivery Access Survey, which is free and is the prompt used in the video. Then work through the rest of the questions on the Practice tab, with the timer running. Every response you submit comes back with a score on all four criteria and specific feedback.

Frequently Asked Questions

Quick answers about Writing Task 2.

Should I pick one side or discuss both in CELPIP Writing Task 2?

Pick one side and defend it, then briefly answer the strongest argument for the other option before you close. Never choosing is the problem, not mentioning the other option. A short note like "some residents worry about noise, but delivery hours would fix that" shows the examiner you understood the whole situation and still made a decision, which is scored under Content and Coherence. What costs you marks is writing about both options equally, or changing your mind halfway through. Decide in the first minute, then spend the rest supporting your choice. You can try this on a free survey prompt with instant feedback.

How many words should a CELPIP Writing Task 2 response be?

Write one response of about 150 to 200 words, with 180 as a comfortable target. You have 26 minutes for that single response. Under about 130 words there is not enough room to develop two reasons, which costs you marks in Task Fulfillment. Over about 220 words you usually start repeating ideas and making rushed grammar errors, so the extra length works against you.

How is CELPIP Writing Task 2 different from Task 1?

Task 2 is an opinion response to a survey where you choose one option and justify it. Task 1 is an email that solves a specific problem for a named person. Task 1 gives you 27 minutes and, almost always, three bullet points you must all cover. Task 2 gives you 26 minutes and one choice you must defend. Both ask for about 150 to 200 words. The clearest difference is who you are writing to: Task 1 has a reader, and Task 2 has a form. See Task 1, Writing an Email for the other half.

How do I structure a CELPIP survey response?

State your choice in the first line, give two reasons that each have a concrete example, then close by briefly answering the other option and returning to your choice. That is four parts and it fits every survey prompt, both the Option A or B shape and the agree or disagree shape. Use ordinary connectors like "first," "second," and "for these reasons." Write in full sentences and paragraphs, never in point form. The Task 2 answer template shows the shape with full sample answers.

What is the most common mistake in CELPIP Writing Task 2?

Not committing to one option is the top reason strong writers still miss CLB 9. Writing about both sides equally, or switching position partway through, reads as not fulfilling the task no matter how good the English is. The next most common mistakes are giving reasons with no specific detail behind them, offering suggestions for improving the proposal instead of reasons for your choice, and writing an academic essay when the prompt asked for a survey response.

How is CELPIP Writing Task 2 scored?

Task 2 is scored on four criteria: Content and Coherence, Vocabulary, Readability, and Task Fulfillment. The result is reported as a CELPIP level. Paragon does not publish how the four criteria are weighted against each other, so the safest approach is to treat every one of them as something you cannot afford to ignore. In practice, the criterion people lose the most on is Task Fulfillment, because it is the one that penalises not choosing an option and not developing your reasons.

What topics come up in CELPIP Writing Task 2?

Everyday community and workplace decisions: building policies, city services, office arrangements, school and library programs, transport, and local facilities. You are never asked about specialist knowledge or current events. The scenario always gives you everything you need to form an opinion, which means you can write a strong answer about a topic you have never thought about before. The Practice tab on this page has 74 of them.

Can I practise CELPIP Writing Task 2 with a timer like the real test?

Yes. Every Task 2 question on CELTESTPIP runs a 26-minute countdown and a live word counter, the same limits as the real test. Full mock exams run Task 1 and Task 2 back to back so you practise the pacing of the whole Writing section, not just one task. Every response you submit is scored on all four criteria with specific feedback, so you can see which one is holding your level down. Start with a free timed survey prompt or a full mock exam.

How do I raise my CELPIP Writing Task 2 score to 9 or higher?

Hold one position, support each reason with a specific detail, answer the other option in one sentence, and stop reaching for difficult vocabulary. Most responses stuck at 7 are stuck for the same three reasons: the reasons are general rather than specific, the other option is never mentioned, and the writer has swapped ordinary accurate words for heavier ones that do not quite fit. Fixing the third one alone often moves a response up a level, because inaccurate word choice is scored under Vocabulary and it also damages Readability.
