What is the official format for CELPIP Writing Task 1?

Task 1 gives you 27 minutes to write one email of 150 to 200 words. The prompt describes an everyday situation, names the person you are writing to, and asks you to do around three things. Prometric, which owns and administers CELPIP, publishes the task, the timing, and the word range on its official test format page at celpip.ca, and lists the on-screen timer, word counter, and spell check among the test's built-in tools.

Element / What you get

Time
27 minutes
Length
150 to 200 words
Prompt
One scenario plus three or four bullet points
Format
Email: greeting, body paragraphs, sign-off. No To, From or Subject line unless the prompt asks for one.
Screen
Prompt on the left, typing box on the right, live word counter and countdown timer
Tools
A manual spelling checker. No auto-correct, no grammar check, no dictionary

That last row is where people lose marks they never see. The spelling checker underlines a misspelled word and offers you a short dropdown of alternatives, but it will not fix anything by itself, so a flagged word you never click stays wrong on submission. It also checks spelling only. Write "effect" when you meant "affect", or "quiet" when you meant "quite", and the screen stays clean while a rater reads a word-choice error. A real word in the wrong place never gets an underline. Budget the last five minutes to click every underlined word, then read the whole email once more for the grammar the software cannot see.

The word range is a guideline, not a hard cutoff. Paragon says you have a little room to go under or over the suggested limit, and that your score may suffer if you land significantly outside it. Treat "significantly" as the operative word. Aim for 175 to 200 words when time allows, and you will never have to think about the range again. Ignore the specific buffer figures that circulate on prep blogs, because Paragon does not publish one.

Task 1 is one half of the Writing section. Task 2, the survey response, follows it with its own clock. If you want the whole section mapped out, see the full Writing section format, and when you are ready to work against exam-style prompts, the CELPIP Writing practice test hub holds the full bank.

How is CELPIP Writing Task 1 scored?

Your email is rated on four dimensions, each scored against performance-level descriptors: Content/Coherence, Vocabulary, Readability, and Task Fulfillment. Trained raters score it against the published performance descriptors, and there is no raw score out of 38 for Writing, whatever you may have read elsewhere.

Here is what each dimension is actually looking at, and what you do about it.

01Content/Coherence

The number and quality of your ideas, and whether they sit in a logical order. This is where signpost phrases earn their keep. Phrases like To briefly review, In terms of, and Regarding act as bridges between paragraphs and tell the rater exactly where you are in the task. Develop each idea with a supporting detail instead of listing bare answers.

02Vocabulary

Range, precision, and tone. Avoid repeating the same word three times. Reach for the precise formal synonym: dissatisfaction rather than unhappy, inquire rather than ask, reimbursement rather than money back. Do not copy phrasing straight out of the prompt.

03Readability

Paragraphing, sentence structure, grammar, spelling, and punctuation all live here. Grammar is not a separate criterion on CELPIP. It is scored as part of how readable your email is. Varied sentence structure counts: mix short sentences with longer complex ones rather than writing eight simple sentences in a row.

04Task Fulfillment

Did you address all three bullet points, hold an appropriate tone for the recipient, and stay near the word range? Miss a bullet and this dimension caps your score no matter how elegant the writing is.

For the full breakdown of the rating scale, see how CELPIP Writing is scored. To see what each Writing level means for your immigration file, the CELPIP Writing score chart and CLB equivalency maps every level to a Canadian Language Benchmark (CLB).

Who should you address the email to, and how do you sign off?

Decide who you are writing to before you type a single word, because that one decision fixes your tone and your sign-off. Read the prompt twice and ask whether this person knows you, and whether the relationship is professional or personal.

The Rule of Pairs

In formal writing, your opening salutation must match your closing sign-off. Mismatching them is a Readability error that costs you nothing to avoid.

Skip the To, From and Subject lines unless the prompt specifically asks for them. Plenty of prep sites tell you to invent a subject line. The task gives you a response box, not an email client, so start at the greeting and spend those words on the three bullets instead.

Step 1 of 5

How do you write the opening paragraph?

State who you are and why you are writing, in two sentences. The rater should know your purpose before the end of your second line.

Grammar Booster: Present Perfect Tense

Use this to prove your history with the recipient. It shows that your relationship started in the past and is still true today.

Formula

Subject + Have/Has + Been + Noun/Adjective + For [Time]

Why?

"I am a tenant" is basic. "I have been a tenant for five years" demands respect.

The Template

  1. 1Context

    "My name is [Name], and I have been a [Resident/Customer] for [Duration]."

  2. 2Purpose

    "I am writing to [express a concern / complain / inquire] regarding [Topic]."

Step 2 of 5

How do you cover the first bullet point?

Give the background. Set the scene so the reader understands the context before you complain or ask for anything.

Grammar Booster: Simple Past and Passive Voice

When you describe a finished event or order details, use the Simple Past. Use the Passive Voice to sound formal and objective, because it puts the focus on the action rather than the person.

Active

I ordered the cake.

Passive (Better)

The order was placed on January 10th.

The Template

  1. 1Transition

    "To briefly review the situation, ..."

  2. 2Details

    [Describe what happened / the object / the date].

Step 3 of 5

How do you cover the second bullet point?

Explain the specific issues, hazards, or details. This paragraph is the substance of the email, so give it your best sentence work.

Grammar Booster: Subordinating Conjunctions

Do not write short, choppy sentences. Use conjunctions to connect the problem with the result.

Contrast

Although, While

Cause and effect

Since, Because, Consequently

The Template

  1. 1Zoom-In

    "In terms of [the hazard/problem], ..."

  2. 2Detail 1 (complex sentence)

    "The roots are uplifting the pavement, leading to a tripping hazard."

  3. 3Detail 2 (expander)

    "Furthermore, [additional detail]."

Step 4 of 5

How do you cover the third bullet point?

Give the solution, request, or suggestion the prompt asks for. Be specific enough that the reader could act on it tomorrow.

Grammar Booster: Modals of Suggestion and Necessity

Be polite but firm. Avoid "I want." Modals soften the request while keeping it strong.

Strong

Must, urge

Polite

Would suggest, could, ought to

The Template

  1. 1Transition

    "Regarding [the solution/request], ..."

  2. 2Proposal

    "I strongly urge you to [action]." or "I would suggest [action]."

  3. 3Alternative

    "Alternatively, [Plan B]."

Step 5 of 5

How do you close and sign a CELPIP email?

Close with one forward-looking sentence, then your sign-off and your full name. A missing name or sign-off is a formatting error that a rater cannot ignore.

The closing sentence is the last thing the rater reads, so make it about this email. Say your purpose again in different words and attach it to something specific: the action you want, a date, or how you can be reached. A sentence that would fit any email ever written ("I look forward to hearing from you soon") tells the rater nothing about your prompt, and a stock line pasted from memory is exactly the kind of content CELPIP's test taker agreement rules out. The policy wording is on our page about memorised templates and CELPIP test integrity.

The Template

  1. 1Closing frame

    "I look forward to hearing whether [the specific request] is possible by [date]." or "Thank you for [the specific action you are asking for], and [how or when you can be reached]."

  2. 2Sign-off

    [Matches your Rule of Pairs choice]

  3. 3Name

    [Your full name]

Before and after: the same closing, made specific

Each "after" answers at least two of the five Ws (who, what, when, where, why) from the prompt. That is the whole trick.

Before (fits any email)

After (fits only this email)

  • Before (fits any email):

    Thank you in advance for taking this matter into consideration.

    After (fits only this email):

    Thank you for looking into the oak tree before the autumn storms; I am in unit 1207 most evenings if you want to see the branches yourself.

    What changed:what, when, where, who

  • Before (fits any email):

    I look forward to hearing back from you soon.

    After (fits only this email):

    I look forward to hearing whether the Saturday section of ACC 210 has space before the first week of September.

    What changed:what, when

  • Before (fits any email):

    I hope this matter will receive your immediate attention.

    After (fits only this email):

    I would appreciate a reply before the next forecast snowfall so that I can arrange other clearing if needed.

    What changed:when, why

CELPIP Writing Task 1 Samples With Answers: Three More Worked Examples

The template above works on every Task 1 prompt, but it looks different depending on who you are writing to. These three samples run it through three different registers: a formal request to someone you know by name, a formal complaint to a stranger, and a semi-formal note to a neighbour. Each one names its word count, because knowing what 175 words feels like is half the battle.

2Requesting a schedule change (formal, named recipient)3Complaining about a service contract (formal, unknown recipient)4Proposing a shared repair (semi-formal, neighbour)

Sample 2: Requesting a schedule change (formal, named recipient)

Prompt

You are enrolled in a part-time accounting certificate at a community college. You need to move from the Tuesday evening section of one course to the Saturday morning section. Write an email to the program coordinator, Ms. Fraser. In your email:

  • Explain why you need to change sections.
  • Describe how you will catch up on the work you have missed.
  • Propose when the change should take effect.

Sample Answer, 176 words

Dear Ms. Fraser,

My name is Daniyar Toktassyn, and I have been enrolled in the accounting certificate since September. I am writing to request a transfer from the Tuesday evening section of ACC 210 to the Saturday morning section.

To briefly review my situation, my employer restructured our department last month and moved me onto a rotating evening shift. Since that change took effect, I have missed two Tuesday classes and I expect to miss several more if my schedule stays as it is.

In terms of catching up, I have already borrowed notes from a classmate and completed both missed assignments. I would be glad to meet the Saturday instructor beforehand so that I arrive at the same point as the rest of the group.

Regarding timing, I would suggest the transfer take effect from the first Saturday in September. Alternatively, if the section is full, I would appreciate being added to a waiting list.

I look forward to hearing whether the Saturday section has space before the first week of September.

Sincerely,
Daniyar Toktassyn

Why this scores well.

Task Fulfillment

all three bullets get their own paragraph and a concrete answer, including a fallback for the third.

Content/Coherence

the three signpost phrases carry the reader from reason to remedy to date.

Vocabulary

"restructured", "rotating evening shift", and "take effect" are precise rather than general.

Readability

the salutation "Dear Ms. Fraser" correctly pairs with "Sincerely", and no sentence runs past two clauses.

Sample 3: Complaining about a service contract (formal, unknown recipient)

Prompt

You paid a company in advance for driveway snow clearing for the whole winter season. The company has not cleared your driveway after several snowfalls. Write an email to the company's customer service department. In your email:

  • Describe the service you paid for and when.
  • Explain what has gone wrong and how it has affected you.
  • Say what you would like the company to do.

Sample Answer, 183 words

Dear Sir or Madam,

I am writing to express my dissatisfaction with a seasonal snow clearing contract I purchased from your company on October 14th for the address at 88 Larkspur Crescent.

To briefly review the situation, the contract guaranteed clearing within twelve hours of any snowfall over five centimetres. Since December, there have been four such snowfalls, and my driveway was cleared only once. I reported the problem by telephone on January 9th and was told a crew would be dispatched, but nobody arrived.

In terms of the impact, I was unable to reach my vehicle on two working mornings and had to arrange transport at my own expense. My elderly mother, who visits weekly, has been unable to reach the front door safely.

Regarding a resolution, I would request a refund of half the contract value, given that roughly half the season remains. Alternatively, I would accept a written commitment that clearing will resume within the guaranteed window.

I would appreciate a reply before the next forecast snowfall so that I can arrange other clearing if needed.

Yours Faithfully,
Priya Raghunathan

Why this scores well.

Task Fulfillment

every bullet is answered with a checkable detail, including a date, a depth in centimetres, and a specific remedy.

Vocabulary

"dissatisfaction", "dispatched", and "guaranteed window" hit the formal register without sounding forced.

Content/Coherence

the impact paragraph gives two distinct consequences, one practical and one personal, rather than repeating the same complaint.

Readability

"Dear Sir or Madam" pairs correctly with "Yours Faithfully".

Sample 4: Proposing a shared repair (semi-formal, neighbour)

Prompt

A windstorm damaged the fence between your backyard and your neighbour's. You would like to repair it and share the cost. Write an email to your neighbour, Elena. In your email:

  • Describe the damage to the fence.
  • Explain what repair you are proposing and how you suggest splitting the cost.
  • Suggest when the work could be done.

Sample Answer, 175 words

Dear Elena,

I hope you and the kids came through Saturday's storm all right. I am writing about the fence between our yards, which did not fare quite as well.

To briefly review the damage, three panels near the maple tree have come away from their posts, and one post itself is now leaning badly. One of the cross rails has splintered as well, though that looks like a cheap fix. The rest of the fence looks sound, so I do not think we need to replace the whole thing.

In terms of the repair, I asked a contractor on Kingsway to take a look and he quoted eight hundred dollars to reset the post and refit the panels. Since the fence sits on the property line, I would suggest we split the cost evenly, though I am happy to discuss it if that does not seem fair to you.

Regarding timing, he could start the week of the fifteenth. Let me know what suits you and I will book it.

Best regards,
Tomás Herrera

Why this scores well.

Task Fulfillment

the three bullets are covered and the cost split is stated as a number, not a vague "we could share it".

Vocabulary

the register drops to semi-formal ("came through", "what suits you") without sliding into slang.

Content/Coherence

the same three signpost phrases still structure the email, which shows the template survives a change of tone.

Readability

"Dear Elena" pairs with "Best regards", and the opening line does social work before the business starts, which is what a real semi-formal email does.

What does a Level 7, Level 9 and Level 10 CELPIP email look like?

The difference between Level 7 and Level 10 is rarely the idea. It is the detail, the control and the tone. Below is one Task 1 prompt answered three times, as our examples of a Level 7, a Level 9 and a Level 10 email. Under each one you'll see why it sits at that level, measured against the four things Prometric rates: Content/Coherence, Vocabulary, Readability and Task Fulfillment.

These are our own practice answers, written to match Prometric's published Writing level descriptors (May 2026) and compared with the rated sample responses (Levels 7, 9 and 12) in its Writing Pro: Target 9+ Study Pack. They are not official CELPIP responses, and no CELPIP rater scored them. Only the real test gives you a real level. CELPIP levels match CLB levels one to one, so a Level 9 here means CLB 9.

Prompt

You signed up and paid for a six-week evening cooking class at your local community centre. After two classes, the centre cancelled the course without any warning. Write an email to the centre's program manager. In your email:

  • Explain what happened.
  • Describe how the cancellation affected you.
  • Say what you would like the centre to do.

Level 7 example (157 words)

Dear Program Manager,

I am writing about the cooking class at your community centre. I registered for the six-week evening class and I paid $180. After only two classes, the centre cancelled the course and nobody informed us before.

This cancellation was very bad for me. I was very excited about this class because I wanted to learn how cook healthy food for my family. Also, I took the bus for 40 minutes to come to the third class, but the door was closed. I also asked my manager to change my work shift on Tuesdays so I can come to this class. I was very disappointed and I lost my time.

I would like the centre give me my money back for the classes that I did not take. Or maybe you can start the class again with other teacher. Please answer me soon because I want to know what will happen.

Thank you,

Matt Zare

The errors in this answer are deliberate.

Why this is a Level 7 answer.

Content/Coherence

every bullet is answered and each has a supporting detail, which is what a Level 7 email does: a clear main idea with some support behind it. But the details stay general: "very bad", "lost my time".

Vocabulary

common words, a few repeated many times ("very" three times, "class" or "classes" eight). At Level 7 the words are mostly everyday ones, with only a few that belong to the situation.

Readability

three clear paragraphs and mostly correct simple sentences. Errors appear, especially as the sentences get longer ("learn how cook", "would like the centre give me", "other teacher"), but the meaning always stays clear. That fits Level 7: longer sentences are attempted and mostly work, but not always.

Task Fulfillment

the request is there, but "the classes that I did not take" gives no number, and "answer me soon" sounds more demanding than polite.

Level 9 example (172 words)

Dear Program Manager,

I am writing to express my disappointment about the cancellation of the Tuesday evening cooking class at the Riverside Community Centre. I registered for the six-week course in August and paid the full fee of $180.

After only two sessions, the course was cancelled without any notice. When I arrived for the third class, the room was locked, and the staff at the front desk could not tell me why. Nobody had called or emailed the students.

This situation affected me in several ways. I had arranged for my neighbour to look after my children that evening, and I travelled 40 minutes by bus for nothing. Most importantly, I chose this course to learn healthy recipes for my family, and now I have no class to attend.

I would like the centre to refund the four sessions I did not receive, which is $120. If the course restarts with a new instructor, I would also be happy to join again.

I look forward to your reply.

Sincerely,

Matt Zare

Why this is a Level 9 answer.

Content/Coherence

each bullet now has specific, realistic details (the locked room, the neighbour, the exact refund). At Level 9, each main idea is backed by facts and details that fit it.

Vocabulary

accurate and natural ("without any notice", "the full fee"), though "class", "course" and "session" still carry most of the load.

Readability

well-organized paragraphs and no grammar errors. There are only a few transitions ("After", "Most importantly"), a common gap even in a strong email.

Task Fulfillment

all three bullets are covered, and the request is clear and polite.

What changed from Level 7: the numbers became exact ($120, four sessions), the vague feelings became real consequences, and the grammar errors are gone.

Level 10 example (182 words)

Dear Program Manager,

I am writing about the Tuesday evening cooking course at Riverside Community Centre, which I registered and paid for in August. After just two sessions, the course was cancelled, and I only found out when I arrived for the third class and found the room locked.

What frustrated me most was the lack of communication. Nobody phoned or emailed the students, and the front desk could offer no explanation. By then, I had already paid my neighbour to watch my children that evening and spent 40 minutes on the bus, only to turn around and go home. More importantly, I signed up specifically to learn affordable, healthy meals for my family, and that plan is now on hold.

To resolve this, I would appreciate a refund of $120 for the four sessions I missed. Alternatively, if the centre plans to relaunch the course with a new instructor, I would gladly accept a credit toward it instead.

Thank you for looking into this. I hope to hear from you by the end of next week.

Kind regards,

Matt Zare

Why this is a Level 10 answer.

Content/Coherence

the email doesn't just list details, it ranks them ("What frustrated me most", "More importantly"). At Level 10 the support is wider and better chosen, not just longer.

Vocabulary

precise choices do the work: "relaunch", "a credit toward it", "on hold". At Level 10, word choice is exact enough to make each detail and comparison clear.

Readability

the sentence types vary (a relative clause, "only to turn around", "What frustrated me most was"), and transitions link ideas within and between paragraphs. At Level 10, ideas are linked smoothly inside each paragraph and from one paragraph to the next.

Task Fulfillment

two solutions instead of one, a clear deadline, and a firm tone that stays polite.

What changed from Level 9: the same facts, now connected. The writer explains why each detail matters, offers a fallback, and sets a date.

Can you use a memorized template on CELPIP Writing Task 1?

As a skeleton, yes. As a script, no, and the reason is stronger than marks. Prometric states that memorised or copied content in a test response is subject to invalidation of the result, and every test taker signs a contract to produce spontaneous, original responses. The full policy, with the exact wording, is on our page about memorised templates and CELPIP test integrity. Short of that line, a fully memorised email will cost you marks rather than earn them. Raters are trained to spot rehearsed language, and the pattern is easy to see: the structure is flawless while the content stays generic and never touches the specifics of the prompt.

There is a second tell that is harder to fake your way past. A memorized email produces a strange mismatch, where the vocabulary reads at a high level because it was polished in advance, while the sentences that actually respond to the prompt are noticeably simpler. Raters read the two together, and the gap between them is the giveaway.

Use the template for the shape: the salutation pair, the three signpost phrases, the paragraph order, the closing formula. Then fill it with detail that could only have come from this prompt. Names, dates, dollar amounts, unit numbers, the specific consequence you experienced. In the four samples above, the skeleton never changes and the content is different every time. That is the standard to aim for.

Writing Task 1 Template FAQ

Quick answers to the questions test takers ask us most.

01

How long should a CELPIP Writing Task 1 email be?

Write 150 to 200 words. Aim for 175 to 200 words when time allows, because a fuller email gives each bullet room to develop, but keep five minutes back to proofread. Word count is judged under Task Fulfillment, so an email well under 150 words reads as underdeveloped, and a very long one tends to accumulate errors.

02

How much time do you get for CELPIP Writing Task 1?

27 minutes. Use a 2 / 20 / 5 split: two minutes to read the prompt and decide formal or informal, twenty minutes to write without stopping to edit, and the last five minutes to proofread. The test has a manual spelling checker but no auto-correct and no grammar check, so that final block is not optional.

03

What is the official format for CELPIP Writing Task 1?

An email of 150 to 200 words responding to a scenario and three or four bullet points, written with a greeting, body paragraphs, and a sign-off. You do not need a subject line. Paragon Testing Enterprises publishes the format on its official test page.

04

How is CELPIP Writing Task 1 scored?

On four dimensions: Content/Coherence, Vocabulary, Readability, and Task Fulfillment. Each is rated against performance-level descriptors by trained raters. Writing is not scored as a raw mark out of 38.

05

Do you need a subject line in CELPIP Writing Task 1?

No. Skip the To, From and Subject lines and start at the greeting, unless the prompt explicitly asks for them. The task gives you a response box rather than an email client, and none of the four rating dimensions rewards a subject line. Words spent there are words not spent developing your three bullets.

06

Can you go over 200 words on CELPIP Writing Task 1?

Nothing stops you from typing past 200, and a few words over will not sink your score on its own. The risk is indirect: longer emails leak grammar errors and repetition, both of which are scored under Readability and Vocabulary. Aim for 175 to 200 and stop there.

07

Is there a CELPIP Writing Task 1 PDF with samples and answers?

Yes. CELTESTPIP's free Writing Task 1 PDF has the five-part email template, five sample emails with answers (formal and informal), the same prompt answered at Level 7, 9 and 10, and a checklist for your last five minutes. You can view and download it free on this page, without signing up. The samples are our own practice examples, not official CELPIP responses.
