Task 2 Essentials: Responding to Survey Questions

One 4-part map that works for both Task 2 prompt shapes, how to pick a side in 30 seconds, where examples come from, and why the closing nod is scored, not optional.

Task 2 is the survey response. You're given a short situation, your city, your building, or your workplace is deciding something, and you pick one side and explain why.

The specification

Length150–200 words. Aim for ~180.
TimeAbout 26 minutes (Task 1 gets 27 — they're different).
FormatNo greeting, no sign-off. This is not the email task. Start with your opinion.
On screenTimer, word counter, basic spell check.

The danger zones. Under about 130 words your answer looks thin and empty. Over about 220 you start repeating yourself and making more mistakes. Both drag your score down, so 180 is the sweet spot — long enough to develop two reasons properly, short enough to stay controlled.

How it's scored: four criteria

  • Content & Coherence — do your ideas make sense and connect?
  • Vocabulary — the right words, not the biggest words.
  • Readability — clean grammar, easy sentences, clear layout.
  • Task Fulfillment — did you actually answer the question asked?

Every mistake in this lesson is one of those four going wrong.

Two prompt shapes, one map

Most preparation only covers the first shape. Then people meet the second one on test day and freeze.

Shape 1 — Two-option (A vs B). You're given two choices and asked to pick one.

Should delivery drivers leave orders at the lobby desk, or bring them to each door?

Shape 2 — Agree/disagree. You're given one proposal and asked whether you support it.

The community centre should extend its weekday hours until 9:00 p.m. — do you agree or disagree?

The map does not change. Only two things differ:

Two-optionAgree/disagree
Your opening"I support Option B because…""I agree that… because…"
Your closing nodacknowledges the other optionacknowledges the opposing view

Everything between is identical. Don't memorise two templates; learn one map and read the prompt carefully enough to know which shape you're in.

The 4-part map

#PartLengthWhat it does
1The choice1–2 sentencesName your side and your main reason. No warm-up, no "both options are good."
2Reason A + example1 paragraphOne idea, made real with a small concrete example.
3Reason B + example1 paragraphA different angle on the topic, again with an example.
4The close + the nod2–3 sentencesAcknowledge the other side, answer it, restate your choice.

Four paragraphs, blank line between each. That layout alone helps your readability score — a grader reading a wall of text has to work to find your structure, and working is not what you want them doing.

Step 1 — Pick a side in 30 seconds

Most people lose three or four minutes here, hunting for the "right" answer.

There is no right answer. Nobody is scoring your opinion. You are scored on how well you defend whichever side you take.

So don't ask which side is correct? Ask:

Which side can I give two concrete examples for, right now?

That's the whole decision. If you can immediately picture two situations that support Option A, choose A, even if you personally prefer B. The side you can evidence fastest is always the better side to argue, because your examples are what earn the marks.

Give yourself 30 seconds, commit, and don't reopen the question. Changing your mind at minute twelve is how people run out of time.

The trap: writing "both options have advantages." It sounds balanced and mature, and it leaves the grader with nothing to score. There must be a side.

Step 2 — Find two reasons that genuinely differ

The instruction "give a second reason from a different angle" is useless unless you know what the angles are. Here they are. Pick any two rows, as long as they're different rows, your reasons won't overlap.

AngleThe question it answers
People affectedWho does this help or hurt most?
TimeDoes this save or waste people's time?
MoneyWhat does it cost, or save, for whom?
SafetyDoes anyone get hurt, or feel unsafe?
Fairness / accessDoes this leave a group out?
ReliabilityDoes it work consistently, or only sometimes?
EnvironmentWaste, energy, noise, green space.
PracticalityIs it actually workable day to day?

Worked example. Prompt: more park benches vs more park washrooms.

  • Two reasons from the same row (weak): "Washrooms help families." / "Washrooms help parents with kids." — that's one reason twice.
  • Two reasons from different rows (strong):
    • Access — "families with young children leave events early without them."
    • Reliability — "clean facilities keep the park usable through winter, not just in summer."

Two different rows, two different examples, no overlap. That's what the grader is looking for under Content & Coherence.

Step 3 — Where examples come from

This is the step people freeze on: "I've never lived in a building with a delivery policy. What do I write?"

You are not being fact-checked. A small, plausible, specific detail is exactly what's wanted. It doesn't have to have happened to you. What's being scored is whether you can make an abstract claim concrete.

Three reliable sources

  1. Something you've genuinely seen. Strongest, because the detail comes easily. "On my floor, an older neighbour…"
  2. A plausible situation from ordinary life. "A parent with a sleeping baby can't easily go down to the lobby." Nobody needs to verify this.
  3. A number that sounds like real life. "A 20-minute trip often takes 40." / "The line was 15 minutes long."

What makes an example count. It needs at least one of:

  • a time,
  • a number,
  • a place,
  • or a specific person.
❌ Not an example✅ An example
"This causes many problems for residents.""Last Saturday the queue by the playground was 15 minutes long."
"Buses are often late.""On Main Street a 20-minute trip regularly takes 40."
"It is difficult for some people.""A parent with a sleeping baby can't leave the apartment to collect a meal."

Step 4 — The nod is scored, not optional

Older advices call the closing acknowledgement "optional, if you have space." That was wrong, and it costs you marks.

When you name the opposing view and answer it, you demonstrate that you considered both sides and chose deliberately. That earns credit under Content & Coherence and Task Fulfillment, two of your four scores. An answer that never acknowledges the other side reads as though you didn't notice there was one.

The three-move formula

Concede → Limit → Redirect

  1. Concede — name the other side's real point. "Admittedly, some residents worry about security."
  2. Limit — show it's smaller or solvable. "But that's fixable — check driver ID, or set delivery hours."
  3. Redirect — return to your choice. "…without ending a service people depend on."

Three moves, two or three sentences. Never more — this is a closing note, not a second essay.

Conceding without collapsing

The nod must not become a second argument. Watch the difference:

❌ Collapsing: "Of course, extra parking is also very useful for visitors and would help local businesses, and there are good reasons to support it too."
✅ Nodding: "Free evening parking does help visitors, but residents need somewhere to park every night, not just on event nights.

The first one hands your position back. The second concedes one point and immediately closes it.

Step 5 — Say it plainly

The single most common way strong English speakers cap their own score is by reaching for size instead of precision.

An answer can be grammatically flawless, structurally correct, on word count — and still stall, because it reads like a company memo rather than a person answering a survey about their own building. "Temperature degradation" just means the food gets cold. The bigger phrase isn't more advanced; it's only heavier. And reaching for heavy words at speed is how small errors creep in.

The padding diagnostic

Run this over your own draft. Every pattern below is a symptom of writing to impress rather than to communicate.

PatternExampleReplace with
Padded opener"After careful consideration, I strongly support…""I support…"
Announcing yourself"It is worth noting that…" / "I would like to state that…"(delete — just say the thing)
Latinate abstract noun"temperature degradation""the food gets cold"
Corporate verb"optimizes," "safeguards," "facilitates""helps," "protects," "makes easier"
Institutional phrase"promotes inclusivity," "fosters widespread dissatisfaction""includes more people," "annoys residents"
Formal inevitability"would inevitably lead to""would cause"
Essay-speak closer"In summation, the aforementioned proposals…""For these reasons, …"

The test: read your sentence out loud. Would you say it to a friend describing the same situation? If not, rewrite it in the words you'd actually use. Plain and exact beats formal and vague every time.

This is scored under Vocabulary — and vocabulary measures precision and fit, not difficulty. See Lesson 7 — Vocabulary for Precision.

Your 26 minutes

MinutesWhat you're doing
0–3Read the prompt. Identify the shape. Pick your side (30 seconds) and jot two reasons from different angles.
3–21Write all four parts. Don't stop to polish.
21–26Check word count, run the self-score below, fix the top two problems.

If you're still choosing a side at minute five, you've already chosen — go with whichever one you were leaning toward and start writing.

Model answer 1 — Two-option

Prompt. Your city can add more bus-only lanes or reduce bus fares by 10%. Choose one and explain why it helps commuters more.

I support adding more bus-only lanes, because they cut travel time and make service reliable. Price matters, but people stop using buses when trips take too long or the schedule can't be trusted.

First, dedicated lanes keep buses moving during rush hour. On Main Street, a trip that should take 20 minutes often becomes 40 because buses sit behind single-occupant cars. A bus lane would let them bypass that queue and stay on schedule.

Second, faster and more predictable trips bring in new riders. Commuters plan around time, not small savings. If the ride is quicker, more students and workers will leave the car at home, which reduces traffic for everyone.

A 10% fare cut does help people who already ride, and for someone on a tight budget that is real money every month. But a cheaper ticket doesn't make the bus arrive. When buses run late, people miss classes and shifts, and a discount doesn't repair that. For these reasons, bus-only lanes deliver the bigger benefit for daily commuters.

170 words. Choice in line one. Reason A = time/reliability. Reason B = people affected / environment. The nod concedes that the fare cut genuinely helps someone specific, limits it, and redirects.

Model answer 2 — Agree/disagree

Prompt. Your employer proposes allowing staff to work from home two days a week. Statement: "The company should allow two work-from-home days." Do you agree or disagree?

I agree that the company should allow two work-from-home days. The main reason is that the office day and the productive day are not the same thing for everyone.

First, quiet time is hard to find in an open office. Work that needs concentration like writing reports and checking figures, gets broken up by interruptions every few minutes. Two days at home would give people a stretch of focused time without changing their total hours.

Second, the commute is a real cost. Many staff spend over an hour a day travelling, which is time and money spent on nothing productive. Removing that twice a week gives people back most of a working day each month.

Of course, some work genuinely needs people in the same room, and new staff learn far faster sitting beside someone experienced than they do over a video call. That's a fair concern, but it argues for agreeing which two days everyone is in the office, not for scrapping the idea. For these reasons, two days at home is worth trying.

173 words. Same four parts. Only the opening ("I agree that…") and the direction of the nod changed. Reason A = practicality, Reason B = time/money.

Error fixes

No clear choice
❌ "Both options have advantages and the city should consider carefully."
✅ "I support bus-only lanes because they cut travel time."

Reasons with no support
❌ "It's cheaper, cleaner, and easier."
✅ "It's faster in rush hour — the 5:40 bus regularly arrives after 6:00, so riders miss the transfer."

Two reasons that are really one
❌ "It helps families." + "It helps parents with children."
✅ Pick two different rows from the angle grid.

Arguing both sides equally
❌ "Lanes are good, but fares are also good, and both help people."
✅ Commit to one; the other side appears only in the closing nod.

No nod at all
❌ Ending on "For these reasons, lanes are better."
✅ Concede → limit → redirect, then your final line.

Essay tone
❌ "In summation, the aforementioned proposals exhibit considerable merit."
✅ "For these reasons, bus lanes help more people."

Treating it like an email
❌ "Dear Sir or Madam, … Kind regards, Amir."
✅ No greeting, no sign-off. Open with your opinion.

A conclusion that repeats everything
❌ Three sentences summarising both reasons again.
✅ One line. You have 180 words; spend them on evidence, not on recap.

Score your own answer

Give yourself 0–3 on each. Under 9 out of 12, fix it before you submit.

Practice

1. Spot the shape (2 min). Label each prompt Two-option or Agree/disagree, and write only your opening line:
(a) "Lobby desk or door delivery?" (b) "The library should open until 9 p.m. — agree or disagree?" (c) "More benches or more washrooms?" (d) "The city should ban cars downtown on Sundays — agree or disagree?"
Answers: a & c two-option ("I support Option…"); b & d agree/disagree ("I agree/disagree that…").

2. Angle drill (5 min). Take one prompt: the building should install security cameras in all hallways. Write four possible reasons, each from a different row of the angle grid. Then pick the two you can example fastest — that's your answer's spine.

3. Example upgrade (5 min). Turn each into a real example by adding a time, number, place or person:
(a) "Parking is difficult in the evening." (b) "The gym gets crowded." (c) "Recycling is confusing for residents."

4. Write the nod (3 min). For model answer 2 above, write a different three-move nod using concede → limit → redirect. Compare it to the original — there's more than one right answer.

5. Full timed run (26 min). Take a Task 2 prompt on CELPIP Writing Task 2: Survey Questions, use the 3 / 18 / 5 split, self-score with the rubric, then submit for instant AI feedback and compare your score to your own.
