CELPIP Speaking Score Chart: Levels, CLB and IELTS (2026)

Your CELPIP Speaking level maps directly to the Canadian Language Benchmark (CLB). A Speaking score of 9 equals CLB 9. Levels run from 0 to 12, rated by trained raters across your 8 speaking tasks on four criteria: Content/Coherence, Vocabulary, Listenability, and Task Fulfillment. In IELTS terms, CLB 9 is roughly Speaking 7.0.

What is the CELPIP Speaking score chart?

The chart is simply the map between your CELPIP Speaking level, the CLB level Canadian immigration uses, and the equivalent band on other tests. CELPIP reports Speaking as a single level from 0 to 12. That number is your CLB level for Speaking, and there is no conversion math, unlike IELTS, where each skill needs its own lookup.

The 1-to-1 CELPIP-to-CLB mapping is confirmed by IRCC's official Express Entry language table: a CELPIP-General Speaking result of 9 is entered as CLB 9, an 8 as CLB 8, and so on.

How is the CELPIP Speaking test scored?

CELPIP Speaking is scored by trained raters on four criteria, not out of a fixed number of points. You complete 8 tasks: giving advice, talking about a personal experience, describing a scene, making predictions, comparing and persuading, dealing with a difficult situation, expressing opinions, and describing an unusual situation. A trained rater judges each response, and your overall level reflects your consistent performance across all of them.

Raters weigh four criteria:

  • Content/Coherence: how many relevant ideas you give, how well you develop them, and whether your response is organised with supporting detail. Thin, one-line answers cap your level here.
  • Vocabulary: range and precision. Using the right word beats using a big word. Vague, repeated wording holds you back.
  • Listenability: how easy you are to follow: pronunciation, intonation, rhythm, and grammar working together. You do not need a "perfect" accent; you need to be effortless to understand.
  • Task Fulfillment: did you actually do what the task asked, cover all of it, use the right tone, and speak for the full time? Drifting off-topic or finishing early costs you here.

This is why memorised templates only take you so far. A template gives you structure, but the rater is listening for real, on-topic content delivered clearly under time pressure. What do the CELPIP Speaking level descriptors say?

CELPIP publishes level descriptors for Speaking: short "At this level, I can" statements for each level, in the four categories your eight tasks are rated on (Content/Coherence, Vocabulary, Listenability and Task Fulfillment). They are the closest thing to a public rubric the Speaking test has, and the official wording is in the CELPIP Level Descriptors document on celpip.ca. Below is my own summary of what each category asks for as the levels rise. It is written in my words, not quoted from the document.

Read each category on its own. Your level is a judgement across all four, and two people can reach the same level in different ways. After each summary there is a short note on what a 9 does that a 7 does not, because that is the gap most of my students are trying to close.

For what each level means in plain words, and what it is enough for in Canada, see what each CELPIP level means

Content/Coherence

LevelsIn plain words
10 to 12You handle demanding, unfamiliar situations and develop your ideas fully, with clear and precise detail.
7 to 9You give concrete information and some abstract ideas, each backed by a reason with some depth to it.
5 to 6You give opinions and feelings with simple reasons, describe familiar people and situations, and tell a simple story.
3 to 4You say what you like, dislike or need, and tell very short, simple stories.

What a 9 does that a 7 does not. In this category the descriptors for 7, 8 and 9 are very close: all three want ideas supported with reasons that have some depth, and 7 and 8 read the same. So the jump from 7 to 9 is small here, and the real step comes at 10, where you develop ideas in unfamiliar, non-routine situations. What this category does demand at 7 and up is a reason that has some shape to it: not just "because it is cheaper", but "because it is cheaper, and with the money we save we could finally fix the car". One claim, one reason, one consequence. If your answers are a list of opinions with no reasons, this is the category holding you down.

Vocabulary

LevelsIn plain words
10 to 12A wide range of concrete and abstract language, with idioms and figurative expressions used naturally.
7 to 9Everyday words plus more precise words that suit the situation, and the occasional idiom.
5 to 6Common words and phrases.
3 to 4Very common, basic words.

What a 9 does that a 7 does not. First, notice what does not change: idioms are mentioned in much the same way from 7 right through to 10. Idioms are not what moves you from 7 to 9, so stop memorising lists of them. What changes is vocabulary that fits the context. A 7 reaches for a more precise word now and then. A 9 uses the words that belong to the situation in the prompt. If the task is about choosing between two apartments, that means words like lease, deposit, commute and utilities, used naturally, not "the place" and "the money" over and over.

Listenability

LevelsIn plain words
10 to 12Good to very good control of a broad range of complex grammar, with speech that flows naturally almost all the time.
8 to 9Some control of complex sentences, mostly fluent rhythm and pronunciation, and very few restarts or awkward pauses.
6 to 7Simple grammar under control and delivery that is easy to understand, with some restarts or pauses at 6 and very few at 7.
3 to 5Simple grammar with limited or patchy control.

What a 9 does that a 7 does not. Two shifts. The grammar moves from good control of simple sentences at 7 to some control of complex ones at 9. And the delivery moves from easy to understand to mostly fluent. In practice a 7 speaks in short, safe sentences that are easy to follow. A 9 adds sentences with "although", "if", "which" and "so that" in them, and keeps them going without stopping halfway to restart. You do not need a perfect accent for either level. You need the complex sentences you can say in one go, so practise a few of those until they come out smoothly, rather than attempting long sentences you lose control of.

Task Fulfillment

LevelsIn plain words
10 to 12You adapt your style and tone to a broad range of listeners and situations, and your meaning comes across precisely.
9You adjust your style and tone to a range of listeners and situations, formal or informal.
7 to 8You adjust your tone for some listeners and situations, mostly familiar ones, and your intended meaning comes across.
3 to 6You get information across on familiar topics, adjusting your style only in familiar situations.

What a 9 does that a 7 does not. At 7 the descriptors talk about adjusting your tone for familiar listeners, the friend or family member. At 9 the range widens to many kinds of listener and situation. The Speaking prompts tell you who you are talking to, and the tone has to fit that person: a friend asking for advice and a manager hearing a complaint get different answers, even when the ideas are the same. Before you start speaking, ask yourself who is listening, and let that decide how direct, how polite and how casual you sound.

The descriptors also say which situations each level applies to. From level 9 up, that includes speaking with authority figures as well as peers, in formal or informal settings. At 7 and 8 authority figures are not mentioned, and below 7 the settings become more casual. Several Speaking prompts put you in front of a boss, a landlord or an official, which is exactly the kind of listener the descriptors first name at level 9.

What CELPIP Speaking level equals which CLB?

From level 3 up, every CELPIP Speaking level equals the same-numbered CLB level. A 2 corresponds to CLB 1 to 2, and 0 or 1 means there was not enough language to assess. That single fact is what immigration officers read off your result. So when a program says "CLB 7 Speaking," it means a CELPIP Speaking level of 7.

For how those CLB levels turn into Express Entry points and program minimums, see CELPIP scores for Canadian immigration and CLB requirements, which breaks down how your scores affect your CRS points.

Speaking is rated as a level, so there is nothing to convert, but the CELPIP score calculator puts all four skills together and shows the Express Entry points each level is worth, single or with a spouse.

How does CELPIP Speaking compare to IELTS Speaking?

This is where most charts online get it wrong, and the error can cost you. IRCC's official equivalency table converts each IELTS skill to a CLB level separately. The Speaking band that equals a given CLB is not the same as the Writing or Listening band. Here is the correct CELPIP-to-IELTS Speaking mapping, straight from IRCC (updated June 2026):

Note the top of the scale: IRCC groups CLB 10 and above into a single IELTS Speaking range of 7.5–9.0. It does not assign separate bands to CLB 11 or 12. So a CELPIP 10, 11, or 12 all sit in that 7.5–9.0 window for immigration purposes.

If you have seen a chart claiming CELPIP 9 equals IELTS Speaking 7.5, it is off by half a band. The correct figure is 7.0.

What CELPIP Speaking score do I need for Express Entry?

It depends on the program, and the Speaking minimum moves with it. Per IRCC:

  • Federal Skilled Worker & Canadian Experience Class (NOC TEER 0 or 1): CLB 7 in all four abilities, so CELPIP Speaking 7 minimum.
  • Canadian Experience Class (NOC TEER 2 or 3): CLB 5 with Speaking 5 minimum.
  • Federal Skilled Trades: CLB 5 for Speaking and Listening with Speaking 5 minimum.

Those are floors, not targets. Because CLB feeds your CRS score, most competitive Express Entry candidates aim for CLB 9 (Speaking 9) across the board, since that unlocks the higher language points. If your goal is CLB 9-plus, our guide to speaking confidently enough to score above 9 walks through what raters reward at the top of the scale.

How can I raise my CELPIP Speaking score?

Work the four criteria directly instead of practising vaguely:

  • For Content/Coherence, push past your first idea. Give two or three developed points with a specific example each, and signpost them so the rater hears the structure.
  • For Vocabulary, build topic-specific word banks (workplace, community, personal life) and practise using them precisely, not stuffing in "impressive" words that do not fit.
  • For Listenability, record yourself and listen back. Flat delivery and long pauses hurt more than a mild accent. Aim for a natural, steady rhythm.
  • For Task Fulfillment, read the prompt twice, answer every part, match the tone it asks for, and keep talking until time is called. Finishing 15 seconds early is a wasted opportunity to add detail.

The fastest feedback loop is doing real, timed tasks and seeing where you actually land.
