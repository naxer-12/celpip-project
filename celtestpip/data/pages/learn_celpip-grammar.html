CELPIP Grammar 2026: How It Is Scored and What to Fix

CELPIP has no separate grammar score. See where grammar counts in Writing and Speaking, what each CLB level asks for, and the 6 mistakes to fix.

CELPIP gives no separate grammar score. In Writing, grammar and sentence structure is one factor inside Readability. In Speaking, it sits inside Listenability. Raters are not counting your mistakes. They judge how well you control grammar and how wide a range of sentences you use, and the higher levels ask for more complex sentences, not perfect ones.

Does CELPIP score grammar?

Not on its own. Your Writing and Speaking results are each built from four rated dimensions, and grammar is not one of them. It is one of the factors inside a dimension. Here is our plain-English version of the factors in the Writing and Speaking Performance Standards on celpip.ca:

Writing dimensionWhat raters look at
Content/CoherenceHow many ideas you have, how good they are, how you order them, and the examples that back them up
VocabularyChoosing the right words, using them naturally, having enough variety, and being exact
ReadabilityLayout and paragraphs, linking words between ideas, grammar and sentence structure, spelling and punctuation
Task FulfillmentAnswering what was asked, covering every part, the right tone, and the right word count
Speaking dimensionWhat raters look at
Content/CoherenceHow many ideas you have, how good they are, how you order them, and the examples that back them up
VocabularyChoosing the right words, being exact, having enough variety, and using words naturally
ListenabilityRhythm, pronunciation and intonation; pauses, fillers and self-correction; grammar and sentence structure; how much you vary your sentences
Task FulfillmentAnswering what was asked, covering every part, the right tone, and speaking for a suitable length

So grammar is one line in each table. Sixteen factors per skill, and grammar is one of them.

Here is how the rating works, according to celpip.ca. Every Writing and Speaking performance is rated by several trained raters, each working without seeing the others' ratings. Each rater gives you a level in each of the four dimensions, and your Writing or Speaking score is derived from those dimension ratings. What Prometric does not publish is how much each factor weighs. If someone tells you grammar is "25% of your Readability score", they made that number up.

That is why you will hear two opposite myths. One says grammar does not matter on CELPIP. The other says grammar is everything and every mistake costs a Level. Neither is true. Grammar is one part of one dimension, and it still shows up in every single sentence you write or say.

Where does grammar count in CELPIP Writing?

In Readability, the dimension that asks one question: how easy is your response to read and understand? Grammar and sentence structure sit there next to three other factors: paragraphing, connectors, and spelling and punctuation.

Notice that spelling is a separate factor from grammar. The Writing test gives you a spell checker, so a misspelled word is the easier problem to catch. There is no grammar check and no auto-correct, though. The spell checker will happily accept "he go to work every day", because every word in it is spelled correctly. If you want the full detail on what the spell checker does and does not do, read does CELPIP Writing have spell check.

A grammar problem rarely stays inside Readability, and this is my teaching view, not an official rule. A sentence that falls apart can hide the idea inside it, and a hidden idea does not help your Content/Coherence either. That is the real cost of weak grammar in Writing: not the error itself, but the meaning the reader loses.

Where does grammar count in CELPIP Speaking?

In Listenability, which is about how easy you are to follow. Two of its four factors are about your sentences: grammar and sentence structure, and variety of sentence structure. The other two are your rhythm, pronunciation and intonation, and your pauses, interjections and self-correction.

That last factor matters more than people think. Self-correction is rated inside the same dimension as grammar. If you stop to fix every small slip, you swap a grammar problem for a flow problem, and you lose time from a 60 or 90 second answer.

My rule for students is simple. If the slip changes your meaning, correct it quickly and keep going. If it does not, let it go. "Yesterday I go to the clinic, sorry, I went to the clinic" is fine once. Three times in one answer is a habit the raters will hear.

How good does your grammar need to be for CLB 7, 8 or 9?

At CLB 7 in Writing you need good control of simple grammar and adequate control of complex sentences. From Level 8 up, the official Writing descriptions stop mentioning simple grammar, and from Level 9 they ask for a range of complex and diverse structures. From Level 3 to Level 12, your CELPIP level and your CLB (Canadian Language Benchmark) level are the same number, so the table below reads straight across to the immigration level you need.

Below is our plain-English summary of the grammar lines in the CELPIP Level Descriptors (May 2026), published by Paragon Testing Enterprises, a Prometric company. The official wording is on celpip.ca.

LevelWriting (Readability)Speaking (Listenability)
10 to 12Good to very good control of a wide range of complex and diverse structuresGood to very good control of a broad range of complex structures
9Control of a range of complex and diverse structuresSome control of complex structures
8Good control of complex structures, spelling and punctuationSome control of complex structures
7Adequate control of complex structures, good control of simple grammarGood control of simple structures
5 to 6Good control of simple grammarControl of simple structures, less steady at 5
4Some control of simple grammarLimited control of simple structures

Read the Writing column from the bottom up and you can see the whole story. Levels 4 to 6 are about getting simple sentences right. Level 7 is the first level that asks for complex sentences. From Level 9 up, the key words change to "range" and "diverse". Nobody at any level is asked for zero mistakes.

Speaking moves one step later. Complex structures first appear at Level 8, and "range" appears at Level 10. For the CLB level each immigration program asks for, see our guide to CELPIP scores for Express Entry and CLB requirements.

Is perfect grammar enough for a high CELPIP score?

No. From Level 7 up, every Writing descriptor asks for complex structures, and from Level 9 up it asks for a range of them. Simple sentences with no mistakes do not show either one. To go higher you need more complex sentences, and more complex sentences carry more risk of small mistakes.

The official Writing Pro Target 9+ Study Pack (2022 edition) makes the same point in its Level 9 profile. In our words: a Level 9 writer still makes the odd mistake, but the reader can always follow the meaning. The errors are there. They just do not get in the way.

So the safe strategy is not the safest-looking one. Writing every sentence in the simple pattern you are sure of keeps you accurate and keeps you in the middle levels. Use one or two complex sentences in each paragraph, built on patterns you know well: because, although, which, if, when. The Listenability lesson in our CELPIP Speaking course walks through a rated pair of answers where the one with minor grammar errors scored higher, because it tried more.

Here is the difference on one sentence from a Task 1 email:

SimpleComplex
The heater is broken. It is very cold. My children are sick.Since the heater broke on Monday, the apartment has been so cold that both my children are now sick.

Both are correct. Only the second one shows a rater that you can control a longer sentence.

Which grammar mistakes cost the most points?

The six below are the ones I see most in student answers, and they match the list in our 15 common CELPIP mistakes. Each one is small. What costs points is the same mistake repeated across a whole response, because a pattern makes your writing harder to read and your speaking harder to follow.

1. Switching tenses in the middle of a story

You start in the past and slide into the present without noticing. This one hurts most in Speaking Task 2, where you tell a story about a past experience. The official Speaking study packs give a short piece of advice for that task that is worth remembering: tell the story in the past tense.

  • Wrong: Last summer I rent a car and the company tell me the tank is full.
  • Right: Last summer I rented a car, and the company told me the tank was full.

For the structure of the whole story, use our Speaking Task 2 template. With the structure settled, your attention is free for the verbs.

2. Subject and verb that do not agree

The verb has to match its subject, even when other words come between them. Long subjects are where this breaks.

  • Wrong: The manager of the two buildings don't answer my emails.
  • Right: The manager of the two buildings doesn't answer my emails.
  • Wrong: Everyone in my class have a different schedule.
  • Right: Everyone in my class has a different schedule.

3. Missing or extra articles (a, an, the)

If your first language has no words like a and the, this is probably your most frequent error. Use a or an when you mention something for the first time, and the when your reader already knows which one you mean.

  • Wrong: I am writing about problem with heater in my apartment.
  • Right: I am writing about a problem with the heater in my apartment.

4. The wrong preposition

Prepositions do not translate. You learn them with the word they belong to.

  • Wrong: I am interested for the volunteer position. It depends of the schedule. We discussed about the plan.
  • Right: I am interested in the volunteer position. It depends on the schedule. We discussed the plan.

5. Two sentences joined with a comma

This is called a comma splice, and it is one of the most common errors in typed answers. Use a full stop, or join the two ideas with a word like so, but or because.

  • Wrong: The bus was forty minutes late, I missed the start of the meeting.
  • Right: The bus was forty minutes late, so I missed the start of the meeting.

6. A long sentence that falls apart

This is the opposite problem to number 5. You try to sound advanced, pack four ideas into one sentence, and lose control of it halfway through.

  • Wrong: The reason that I think the city should build the park which many residents including my neighbours who have children want it because there is no green space near us.
  • Right: I think the city should build the park. Many residents want it, including my neighbours with children, because there is no green space near us.

Complex sentences are good. One idea per clause is what keeps them under control.

Do grammar mistakes depend on your first language?

Yes, very much. Most of your grammar errors come from your first language, which is why two students with the same score can make completely different mistakes. Speakers of languages without articles drop a and the. Speakers of languages with one word for he and she mix them up in the middle of a story.

That is good news, because it means your list of problems is short and predictable. If Persian is your first language, our Persian guide to the six mistakes Persian speakers make (فارسی) covers the six mistakes Persian speakers make most often, with examples.

How do you fix your grammar before test day?

Find your pattern first, then fix one pattern at a time. Studying every grammar rule in a textbook is the slow way. You only need to fix the errors you actually make.

  1. Write three practice answers and mark every error. Keep a short list. After three answers you will see the same two or three mistakes coming back.
  2. Fix one pattern a week. If your pattern is articles, check every noun in your next answer for a, an or the. Nothing else.
  3. Keep review time in Writing. Task 1 gives you 27 minutes. Save the last 2 to 3 minutes: read once for grammar, then once for spelling with the spell checker.
  4. Record your Speaking answers and listen back. You will hear tense slips on a recording that you never notice while you speak.
  5. Get feedback on every answer. Our Writing practice and Speaking practice score your answer with AI in minutes, so you can check whether your pattern is actually gone.

Good to know

Frequently Asked Questions

1

Does CELPIP have a grammar check?

No. The Writing test gives you a personal timer, a word counter and a spell checker. There is no grammar check and no auto-correct, so a correctly spelled sentence with a grammar error will not be flagged.

2

Will one grammar mistake lower my CELPIP score?

Almost certainly not on its own. Raters judge your control of grammar across the whole response, and even the Level 9 profile in the official Writing study pack mentions occasional errors. A mistake you repeat all the way through is what pulls a score down.

3

Should I use complex sentences if I might make mistakes?

Yes, a few. The official descriptors ask for complex structures from Level 7 in Writing and Level 8 in Speaking. Use patterns you know well, and keep simple sentences for everything else.

4

Is there a grammar section on the CELPIP test?

No. CELPIP has four components: Listening, Reading, Writing and Speaking. Grammar is assessed inside Writing and Speaking, as one factor of Readability and Listenability.

5

Do spelling mistakes count as grammar on CELPIP?

No. In Writing, spelling and punctuation is its own factor inside Readability, separate from grammar and sentence structure. The spell checker helps with spelling. Grammar is up to you.
