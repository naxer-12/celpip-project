How many questions are on the CELPIP Listening test?

38 scored questions across 6 parts, split 8, 5, 6, 5, 8 and 6. The test may also contain unscored items anywhere within Listening and Reading; they look like scored items and do not count toward your result.

How long is the CELPIP Listening test?

About 46 to 55 minutes, depending on the version you sit.

03

What is on the CELPIP Listening test?

6 parts: Problem Solving, Daily Life Conversation, Information, News Item, Discussion and Viewpoints. All audio uses Canadian English accents, every question is multiple choice, and each clip plays once.

04

What does a CELPIP Listening question in the real test format look like?

Multiple choice, with four options that paraphrase the audio rather than quote it. Wrong options are usually true statements attached to the wrong condition, speaker or time. Our free Part 1 item shows a worked example with all four options explained.

How many free CELPIP Listening questions can I do?

Six items are free, one in each of the six parts, always the first item in each part. Those are the Listening sets of the free CELPIP practice test, which covers all 20 task types after a free sign-up. Together they cover every question type on the section.

06

Can I replay the audio or see a transcript?

Yes in practice here, and no on the real test. Practice items include replay, a full transcript and an answer key. The official CELPIP plays each clip once with no transcript.

07

How is CELPIP Listening scored?

Your raw score out of 38 converts to a CELPIP level from 0 to 12, which from level 3 up is your CLB level. Roughly 27 correct maps to CLB 7, 30 to CLB 8 and 33 to CLB 9. Exact cut points vary by test version.

08

What CELPIP Listening score do I need for immigration?

It depends on the program. Express Entry sets a Listening minimum of CLB 7 for the Federal Skilled Worker Program and for Canadian Experience Class TEER 0 or 1 jobs, and CLB 5 for Canadian Experience Class TEER 2 or 3 jobs and the Federal Skilled Trades Program. Canadian citizenship requires CLB 4 in Listening and Speaking only, for applicants aged 18 to 54.

09

Is CELPIP Listening harder than IELTS Listening?

Neither is harder overall, but they test differently. CELPIP uses a single North American accent and everyday Canadian situations, is fully multiple choice, and plays each clip once. IELTS uses a range of accents and mixes question formats.

10

What topics appear in CELPIP Listening?

Everyday Canadian situations: shops, workplaces, appointments, community issues, news reports and discussions of social topics. No specialist or technical knowledge is assumed.

How many questions are on the CELPIP Listening test?

The CELPIP Listening test has 38 scored questions. The test may also contain unscored items anywhere within Listening and Reading, used for test development, so the number of questions you actually sit can be higher than 38. They look exactly like scored items, they do not count toward your score, and you will not be told which ones they are.

Here is where the 38 come from.

PartWhat you hearQuestions
1. Problem SolvingTwo people talking through a problem, one of them looking for a solution8
2. Daily Life ConversationAn everyday exchange, such as coworkers or neighbours5
3. InformationOne speaker sharing specialised knowledge on a topic6
4. News ItemA single-speaker news report, like Canadian radio or television5
5. DiscussionSeveral speakers giving different views on the same subject8
6. ViewpointsOne speaker laying out contrasting positions on a social issue6

Paragon Testing publishes the section structure and timings on its official test format page at celpip.ca. You can also see how Listening fits against the other three sections in our CELPIP Listening section format breakdown.

Two details matter more than the count. Every question is multiple choice, and each audio clip plays once. There is no rewind button on test day.

What is on the CELPIP Listening test?

CELPIP Listening tests whether you can follow spoken Canadian English in situations you would actually meet in Canada: a shop, a workplace, a news bulletin, a group argument. All audio uses a single North American accent. Nothing is read slowly for your benefit.

The six parts get harder in a specific way. Parts 1 and 2 are concrete, so you are listening for facts, names and numbers. Parts 3 and 4 add density, so more information arrives per minute. Parts 5 and 6 turn abstract, and you are tracking who thinks what and why, which is a different skill from remembering a detail.

That progression is why practising the section as one undifferentiated block is a mistake. If Part 6 is what breaks your score, doing more Part 1 questions will not fix it. Work the specific part:

  • Part 1, Problem Solving
  • Part 2, Daily Life Conversation
  • Part 3, Information
  • Part 4, News Item
  • Part 5, Discussion
  • Part 6, Viewpoints

What does a CELPIP Listening question in the real test format look like?

Here is a real question from our free Part 1 item, and it is the one most people get wrong.

The audio is a conversation at a bike rental shop. A woman works there, a man wants to rent a bike. Ordinary stuff. Then this comes up:

What is the shop's policy if it rains after the bike is picked up?

  1. No refund is provided
  2. A partial refund is given based on usage time
  3. A rain check is issued for another weekend
  4. A full refund is guaranteed regardless of usage

73% of people who attempt this get it right. Compare that to 94% on question 7 of the same conversation. Something about this one costs people marks, and it is worth understanding what.

The answer is 2. In the audio the woman says: "If you've already picked up the bike and the weather turns bad, you can return it early and we'll only charge for the time you used it."

Now look at why option 4 is so tempting. The conversation does mention a full refund. It just attaches it to a different condition: full refunds need 24 hours of notice before pickup. The question asks what happens after pickup. If you heard the words "full refund" and did not hold onto the condition attached to them, option 4 sounds exactly right.

That is the actual skill CELPIP Listening measures. Wrong options are rarely invented. They are usually true statements from the conversation, moved to the wrong condition, the wrong speaker, or the wrong point in time.

Notice the second trap too. The correct answer says "partial refund", and the woman never says those words. She says "we'll only charge for the time you used it." The right answer is almost always a paraphrase, never a quote. If you sit there waiting to hear the option read back to you, you will pick the option that echoes the audio most loudly, and that is usually the distractor.

Two habits follow from this, and they are worth more than any vocabulary list:

  • Track conditions, not just facts. When you hear a policy, a rule or a number, note what it depends on. "Full refund" is not a fact. "Full refund with 24 hours notice before pickup" is.
  • Expect the answer to be reworded. Match meaning, not sound.

Try this exact question yourself, free, then check the answer key, which explains all four options, not just the correct one.

Can I replay the audio or see a transcript?

Yes in practice, no on the real test. Every practice item here lets you replay the audio and open a full transcript. The official CELPIP gives you neither: the clip plays once and there is no text version.

That gap is deliberate on our side, and you should use it deliberately. Do a set once under real conditions, one play, no transcript, no pausing. Then go back, open the transcript, and find the exact sentence that decided each question you missed. You are looking for the moment your attention dropped, which is information you cannot get from a score alone.

Every item also has an answer key that explains all four options. On the bike rental question above, it tells you why "no refund" is wrong and why "full refund" is wrong, not just that they are.

How is CELPIP Listening scored?

Your raw score out of 38 converts to a CELPIP level from 0 to 12, which from level 3 up is your Canadian Language Benchmark (CLB) level. As a rough guide:

Correct out of 38CLB level
About 277
About 308
About 339

These are approximate. Paragon equates each test version statistically, so the exact cut points move slightly between sittings. For the full raw score table, what each band means, and what it is worth for immigration, see the CELPIP Listening score chart, including how the raw score out of 38 is calculated.

Two thresholds are worth knowing now, because they change what you should be aiming at.

For Express Entry, the minimum depends on which program you apply under. Immigration, Refugees and Citizenship Canada (IRCC) sets these floors:

ProgramMinimum Listening level
Federal Skilled Worker, first official languageCLB 7
Canadian Experience Class, TEER 0 or 1 jobsCLB 7
Canadian Experience Class, TEER 2 or 3 jobsCLB 5
Federal Skilled TradesCLB 5

All of these are floors, not targets. Meeting the minimum gets your profile into the pool; it does not get you invited. Your test results must also be less than 2 years old when you submit.

For Canadian citizenship the bar is lower and narrower: CLB 4, and only in Listening and Speaking, for applicants aged 18 to 54. Reading and Writing are not tested for citizenship at all, which is why CELPIP-General LS exists. See the official citizenship language proof requirements on canada.ca for more details.

If citizenship is your goal, this section and Speaking are the only two that matter. That makes Listening half your test, not a quarter of it.

How do I prepare for CELPIP Listening?

You cannot see the questions before the audio starts, so use the on-screen description and picture to work out who is speaking and why before the clip plays. Knowing what kind of conversation you are about to hear turns a memory test into a search task, and that single change is worth more than most study advice.

Take notes on conditions and speakers, not on everything. Trying to transcribe is how people lose Part 5, where the marks come from knowing who held which opinion. A note like "woman = against, cost" beats half a paragraph you cannot read back.

Then review properly. A wrong answer you have not diagnosed will happen again. For part-specific strategy, work through our CELPIP Listening tips guide, which breaks down Part 1, Problem Solving and Part 4, News Item among others. If you would rather be taught it in order, the free CELPIP Listening course runs 13 sections.

One habit to drop: listening to podcasts and calling it preparation. It builds general comprehension, slowly, but it never trains you to hold a condition in your head for 40 seconds while four paraphrased options compete for it. Only questions do that.
