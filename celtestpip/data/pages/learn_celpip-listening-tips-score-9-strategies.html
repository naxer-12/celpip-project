CELPIP Listening Tips and Tricks: Note-Taking and How to Score 9+ (2026)

CELPIP Listening questions come after the audio, so your notes decide your score. Note-taking and tips for all 6 parts, plus the mistakes that cost points.

The most important CELPIP Listening tip is this: in every part, the questions come after the audio, and each clip plays only once. So set up your notes before the audio starts, write keywords, numbers and who said what, then answer from your notes and keep moving. A wrong answer costs you nothing, so never leave a question blank.

Most people who lose points in Listening do not lose them because their English is weak. They lose them because they walk in expecting to see the questions first, try to remember everything, and freeze when a clip moves on. This guide fixes that, part by part. What is the most important CELPIP Listening tip?

Know what you will see before the audio, and what you will not. Paragon's own Listening study pack is clear: the questions for each part come after the audio. Before the clip starts you only get a short introductory statement on screen, telling you who is talking and where.

That changes how you prepare. You cannot listen for the answer to a question you have not seen yet. You listen for the things questions usually ask about, and you write them down:

  • The situation: who is speaking, where, and why. The introductory statement gives you most of this, so read it every time.
  • The problem and the decision: what goes wrong, what solutions come up, which one they choose.
  • Details: numbers, days, times, prices, names.
  • Opinions: who thinks what, and whether they change their mind.

There is no penalty for a wrong answer. Paragon gives one point for each correct answer and takes nothing away for a wrong one, so a guess can only help you.

How should you take notes on CELPIP Listening?

You get paper and a pen at the test centre, and you can ask for more paper. Nobody reads your notes and they have no effect on your score, so write whatever helps you, as messy as you like.

Set up your page before the audio starts. This is the habit that separates a 7 from a 9. The moment you read the introductory statement, draw your layout:

  • Two speakers (Parts 1, 2 and 3): draw a line down the middle. Put M on one side and W on the other. Paragon says that in every two-person conversation, one speaker is a man and one is a woman, so this layout always fits.
  • Three speakers (Part 5): three columns, labelled by where each person sits on screen as they are introduced.
  • One speaker (Parts 4 and 6): one column, with space down the side for names. When a news report names someone, it gives the full name once and then uses only the last name, so write the last name the first time you hear it.

Write keywords, not sentences. "Mon, 3pm, $200" is faster and more useful than "the meeting is on Monday at 3 pm and the budget is $200". Use symbols you already know: an arrow for "leads to", a tick for agree, a cross for disagree, a question mark for unsure.

Keep your notes in order. The questions come in roughly the same order as the answers appeared in the audio. If your notes run top to bottom in the order you heard things, you can find the answer fast.

Keep writing when the questions start in Parts 1 to 3. In these parts you hear each question read out. Note its keywords, then look at your notes from the audio.

Practise this before test day with pen and paper, not a keyboard. Typing notes on a screen is not the same skill.

What are the best tips for each CELPIP Listening part?

Each part below has a free practice set you can try after reading the tips.

Part 1: Listening to Problem Solving

What it is: one conversation between two people, played in three sections of about 1 to 1.5 minutes each. After each section you answer 2 or 3 questions, 8 questions in all.

What makes it tricky: the first idea is rarely the final one. Speaker A suggests one thing, speaker B pushes back, and they end up somewhere else.

  • Track the problem AND the solution. Questions often ask what was decided, not what was discussed. Listen for closing phrases: "So let's go with...", "I think the best option is...", "Why don't we..."
  • Note every option and cross out the losers. If they reject Saturday, put a line through it. The answer is what is left standing.
  • Use the pause between sections. You answer questions after each section, so tidy your notes for the next one.

Part 2: Listening to a Daily Life Conversation

What it is: one conversation of about 1.5 to 2 minutes between two people who know each other: neighbours, friends, coworkers. Five questions.

What makes it tricky: the language is casual, so speakers imply things instead of saying them. "I'm not sure that's the best idea" does not mean "I'm unsure". It means "I disagree."

  • Listen for tone, not just words. Questions often ask how someone feels or what they mean. Hesitation, enthusiasm and politeness all carry meaning.
  • Track who says what. When a question asks "What does the woman suggest?", your M and W columns answer it.
  • Watch for a change of mind. A speaker may start with one view and end with another. Questions ask about where they end up.
  • Don't panic over an idiom. If you hear "it's not my cup of tea", the tone and context tell you the meaning.

Part 3: Listening for Information

What it is: one conversation of about 2 to 2.5 minutes between a man and a woman. One of them has information the other wants, such as a customer asking a salesperson about a new car. Six questions.

What makes it tricky: it is packed with facts. If you did not catch the detail, you cannot reason your way to it.

  • This is your heaviest note-taking part. Write every number, date, name and requirement you hear. Even if you don't know which question it belongs to, "$150" or "March 15" on your page can be matched later.
  • Listen for the correction. "Normally it's $200, but this month there's a discount." The question will ask about the price that applies. Words like "except", "however", "unless" and "actually" point to the real answer.
  • Number the steps. If someone explains a process, write 1, 2, 3. Questions like to test the order.

Part 4: Listening to a News Item

What it is: one speaker reading a news report of about 1.5 minutes. Five questions, all on one screen, where you complete each sentence by choosing from four options.

What makes it tricky: news packs a lot into a short clip, and the people in it get mixed up easily.

  • Listen for the five Ws: who, what, where, when, why. The first sentence usually gives you the main story. Write it down.
  • Write the last names. A person is introduced once by full name, then only by last name. If you missed "Dr. Elena Ruiz", you will not know who "Ruiz" is.
  • Catch the numbers. Figures, percentages and dates are favourite question targets.
  • Keep the sources apart. "The mayor said..." and "Critics argue..." are two positions. Note which belongs to whom.
  • The last sentence matters. Reports often end with what happens next ("The project is expected to finish by..."), and that is a common question.

Part 5: Listening to a Discussion

What it is: a video of three people discussing something, about 1.5 to 2 minutes, often a decision at work or in the community. Eight questions, the most of any part, all on one screen.

What makes it tricky: three people means three positions, and they shift as people agree and disagree.

  • Label the speakers by screen position as they are introduced. Left, middle, right, with a name if you catch it. Then note each stance: "L = expand, M = cautious, R = agrees with L."
  • Use faces to read feelings. Facial expressions and body language help you tell how each person feels. You will not be asked about visual details that have nothing to do with the conversation, like the colour of someone's shirt, so don't waste attention on them.
  • Listen for agreement and disagreement. "I see your point, but...", "Exactly!", "I'm not so sure about that" tell you where each person stands.
  • Give yourself about 30 seconds per question. That is Paragon's own guide for this part. Answer the ones you are sure of first.

Part 6: Listening for Viewpoints

What it is: one speaker giving a prepared talk of about 3 minutes, presenting different views on an issue. Six questions, all on one screen. The language is harder here, with less common and more specialised words.

What makes it tricky: one voice carries several opinions, and it is easy to mix up the speaker's own view with the views they report.

  • Separate reported views from the speaker's own. "Dr. Smith argues...", "Critics say...", "Supporters believe..." are other people. The speaker's own view often comes near the end: "Ultimately..." or "The evidence suggests..."
  • Note who, what they think, and why. Last names again: once you hear the full name, you will usually hear only the last name after that.
  • Track the turns. "However", "on the other hand", "in contrast", "while some argue" mean a new view is starting. Start a new line in your notes.
  • Unknown word in a question? Work out whether it sounds positive or negative from the sentence around it. That is often enough to choose.

How do you manage your time on CELPIP Listening?

The timing works differently in the two halves of the test, and knowing this stops the panic.

  • Parts 1 to 3: you hear each question and see four answers, one question at a time. You get 30 seconds to hear and answer each one, then the test moves on by itself. Pick your best answer before the time runs out. There is no going back.
  • Parts 4 to 6: all the questions for the part sit on one screen with one timer for the whole screen. You can answer them in any order, so do the easy ones first, then come back to the hard ones. Aim for about 30 seconds per question.

In both halves, if you are stuck, guess and move on. One missed question costs you one point. Getting stuck on it and losing the next two costs you three.

How do you get a 9 on CELPIP Listening?

You get a 9 by getting enough questions right across all six parts, especially the later ones. Paragon gives one point per correct answer, and your level is then calculated from your points and the difficulty of the questions. The audio and questions get harder from Part 1 to Part 6, so Parts 5 and 6 are where most 9s are won or lost.

To see roughly how many correct answers a 9 usually takes, check our CELPIP Listening score chart. From level 3 up, your CELPIP level is your CLB (Canadian Language Benchmark) level, so a CELPIP 9 is a CLB 9.

The habits that move a 7 or 8 to a 9 are the ones above: notes set up before every clip, last names written down, and no blank answers.

How should you practise for CELPIP Listening?

Strategy only works once it is a habit. Here is a plan that builds it.

Every day (15 to 20 minutes)

  • Listen to Canadian radio or podcasts, like CBC's The Current or Front Burner. The CELPIP test uses a single North American accent, and Paragon describes its speakers as having Canadian accents, so everyday Canadian audio is close to test day.
  • Watch short Canadian news clips from CBC, CTV or Global News. They sound a lot like Part 4.
  • Practise one part with a timer, taking notes on paper as you go.

Every week

  • Take a full Listening practice test under real conditions: no pausing, no replaying.
  • Review every mistake. Listen again and ask: did I miss a fact, an opinion, or the speaker's tone?
  • Try a new note layout on one part and keep the one that works.

The week before the test

  • Don't start new material. Review your strategies and do light practice.
  • Keep your ears warm with English audio every day. Our CELPIP Listening Podcast is a good place to start.
  • Fix your sleep. Listening is the first part of the CELPIP test, so you need to be sharp from the very first minute.

On test day, the headset at the centre may feel different from your earbuds at home. Paragon's Listening study pack lists a short unscored practice task before Part 1. If your test has one, use it to set a comfortable volume and make sure you hear clearly.

Good to know

Frequently Asked Questions

1

Can I listen to the audio more than once on CELPIP?

No. Every audio clip plays one time only. It starts automatically and you cannot pause it, which is why setting up your notes before each clip matters so much.

2

Can you take notes during the CELPIP Listening test?

Yes. You get paper and a pen at the test centre, and you can ask for more paper. Your notes are not scored and nobody reads them, so use whatever layout helps you.

3

How is the CELPIP Listening test scored?

You get one point for each correct answer and lose nothing for a wrong one. Your CELPIP level, from 0 to 12, is then calculated from your points and the difficulty of the questions. From level 3 up, the CELPIP level equals the CLB level. See the CELPIP Listening score chart for typical raw scores at each level.

4

What accent is used in CELPIP Listening?

CELPIP uses a single North American accent, and Paragon describes its speakers as having Canadian accents. In every two-person conversation, one speaker is a man and one is a woman. Canadian radio and TV news are the best everyday practice.

5

What is the difference between CELPIP Listening Parts 1 to 3 and Parts 4 to 6?

In Parts 1 to 3 you hear each question, read four answers, and get 30 seconds for each question, one at a time. In Parts 4 to 6 you complete sentences, all the questions are on one screen with one timer, and you can answer them in any order.

6

I keep running out of time on CELPIP Listening. What should I do?

In Parts 1 to 3 the test moves on by itself after 30 seconds, so choose your best answer before then. In Parts 4 to 6, answer the questions you are sure of first and give yourself about 30 seconds each. Practising under timed conditions makes this automatic.
