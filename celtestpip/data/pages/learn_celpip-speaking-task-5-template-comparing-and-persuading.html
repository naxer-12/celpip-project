    I understand your point, but looking at the bigger picture, I think...

  2. Price

Practise Speaking Task 5

CELPIP Speaking Task 5 (Comparing and Persuading) in short: two parts. In Part 1 you get 60 seconds to pick one of two options, and you do not speak at all. In Part 2 you get 60 seconds to prepare and 60 seconds to persuade someone that your choice beats the option they are proposing. Greet them, accept their idea politely, then compare the two options on price, on fit for the person involved, and on one specific detail, with the real numbers said out loud.

How long is CELPIP Speaking Task 5?

Three minutes in total, split across two parts: 60 seconds to choose, 60 seconds to prepare, 60 seconds to speak. Task 5 is the only CELPIP Speaking task built this way, and it is the longest single task on the test.

Those figures come from CELPIP's 2026 Speaking Pro study pack. Here is where Task 5 sits against the rest:

The practical consequence: you get twice the planning time of most tasks, and almost nobody uses it well. Sixty seconds to choose feels generous until you are sitting in front of it with a clock running and no microphone to fill.

Sixty seconds of speaking is roughly 130 to 150 words at a comfortable pace. That is enough for a greeting, three comparison points and a closing sentence. Not more.

What does CELPIP Speaking Task 5 ask you to do?

In Part 1 you choose one of two options. In Part 2 you persuade someone that your choice is better than the option they are proposing. CELPIP's own description of the task makes one point explicit: there is no speaking at all in Part 1.

Hold on to that. Part 1 is silent. It is the single most useful fact about this task and most prep material skips it, including the AI summaries that appear above these search results.

CELPIP's overview material makes the same point in different words: the first screen is only a choice, and the second screen gives you a new preparation time and then the speaking time. Two screens, one voice recording.

Three things follow from the wording:

You choose, and then somebody disagrees with you. Part 2 is not a fresh start. Someone has come back with the other option, or a new one, and you are responding to a person who has already made a case.

There is no wrong choice. CELPIP does not say one option scores better than the other. Pick the one you can support with specific details, not the one you would actually buy.

"Persuade someone" means a person, not an essay. Greet them. Acknowledge their idea. Then disagree with reasons. A response that reads like a product review misses the task.

The task is also called Comparing and Persuading.

What does a CELPIP Speaking Task 5 question look like?

The real screen shows a short situation, then two options side by side, each with a picture, a few details and a price. Here is one in that format, written by us.

Part 1 (60 seconds, no speaking). Your sister is starting university in September and wants a laptop. Choose the one you would recommend.

  • Option A: 14-inch ultrabook. 1.2 kg. Battery about 12 hours. $1,150.
  • Option B: 15.6-inch laptop with a large screen and a number pad. 2.1 kg. Battery about 6 hours. $780.

Part 2 (60 seconds to prepare, 60 to speak). Your sister says she prefers Option B. It is cheaper, and she says she will use it at her desk anyway. Persuade her that your option is better.

Notice what Part 2 adds: she has a reason. A good answer answers her reason, not just the price tag. Here is a 60-second answer that argues for the more expensive option, using the template on this page (140 words):

Hi Mina. I saw that you prefer the bigger laptop, and I understand why, because seven eighty is a lot less than eleven fifty. However, I think the ultrabook is the better choice for your first year.

First, about the price, yes, it costs three hundred and seventy dollars more. But you said you will work at your desk, and from what I remember of first year, you will not. You will be in the library, in lectures and on the bus, and that is where the twelve-hour battery and the one-point-two kilos matter.

Also, the bigger laptop weighs almost a kilo more. Carrying that across campus every day gets old by October.

Finally, even though the number pad is handy, a ten-dollar external one solves that.

So I really think the ultrabook is the one. What do you think?

What makes this answer work is what CELPIP's own rated samples for this task reward, in our reading of them: three reasons that build on each other instead of three separate complaints, specific details that go past what the screen says (library, bus, October), varied sentences, a warm tone, and a finish inside the minute. A weaker answer reads the two prices back, says the ultrabook is "better" twice and runs out of time on the third point.

What should you do in Part 1, when you cannot speak?

Choose fast, then start building your case with the time left over. CELPIP publishes six key strategies for this task, three for each part. Here they are in our words, with the place in this template where each one happens:

Part 1. Work out who the choice is for and what they need.The "Context Rule" in Step 2: who is this for?
Part 1. Put the two options side by side and spot where they really differ.Point 1 and Point 3: the two prices and one concrete difference

Note the word quickly. The choosing is not the work. Once you have picked, the remaining seconds are free planning time that most candidates waste sitting still because the screen has not asked them for anything yet.

Use them for three things: the two prices, said out loud in your head the way you will say them in Part 2, one contextual reason your choice fits the person in the scenario, and one weakness in the other option you can name politely.

The Part 2 strategies are the ones that separate scores, and the last of them most of all.

Going beyond the picture is the move. The prompt gives you a price and two or three features. A weak answer reads them back. A strong one adds what they mean for the person in the scenario.

Goal

Persuade a friend, family member, or colleague to change their mind and agree with your choice. You must compare two options (the one you chose and the one they suggested) using comparative adjectives and specific data (prices and numbers).

What CELPIP actually rates on Task 5

CELPIP Speaking is rated on four dimensions: Content/Coherence, Vocabulary, Listenability and Task Fulfillment. Those four names come from CELPIP's Speaking Performance Standards, and they apply to all eight tasks.

01Content/Coherence

What CELPIP looks at

Your ideas, how well they are organised, and the details that back them up

What that means on a persuasion task

Do your reasons build a case, or are they three unrelated observations?

02Vocabulary

What CELPIP looks at

Picking exact words that suit the situation, with some range

What that means on a persuasion task

More economical beats cheaper twice. Sturdier beats better.

03Listenability

What CELPIP looks at

How easy you are to follow: delivery, pace, grammar and sentence variety

What that means on a persuasion task

This is where prices live. "Six ninety-nine ninety-nine" said smoothly, not spelled out digit by digit

04Task Fulfillment

What CELPIP looks at

Whether the answer is relevant, complete, in the right tone, and the right length

What that means on a persuasion task

Doing what the task asks, in a fitting tone, using the time

Three things about this list, because most CELPIP prep gets them wrong:

Each response is rated separately on all four dimensions, and one hard task does not decide your level. CELPIP's 2026 study pack explains that your Speaking level comes from all eight tasks together, so there are many routes to the same level. No points formula is published, and no per-dimension weighting is published either. Task 5 has a reputation as the hardest task. It is still one of eight.

There is no separate Grammar score. Look at the Listenability row. Grammar is not one of the four dimensions, it is one item inside Listenability. That does not make the comparative rules below optional, because "more cheaper" is exactly the kind of error that pulls a listener out of your argument. But nobody scores your comparatives on their own line.

"Coherence and Cohesion", "Lexical Resource" and "Fluency and Pronunciation" are IELTS terms. If a Task 5 template promises to fix your Lexical Resource, it was written for a different test.

For how the four dimensions turn into a level, see the CELPIP Speaking score chart.

What strong Task 5 answers do well

These are not the official rating criteria. They are the three habits that tend to earn what the criteria reward on a persuasion task.

  • Soft rejection

    You cannot just say "Your choice is bad." You must be polite: Acknowledge their idea first, then disagree.

  • Comparatives

    They are listening for the correct use of "More" vs. "-er". You must use "Much cheaper" or "More expensive". Never say "More cheaper."

  • Reading numbers

    This is a critical test. You must read prices naturally. Do not say "point" or "dollars" in the middle.

    • $100 → Say "A hundred dollars".
    • $699.99 → Say "Six ninety-nine ninety-nine" or "Almost seven hundred dollars".

Step 1: The Opening (Greeting & Soft Rejection)

Goal: Be polite but firm. You need to show that you have read their suggestion, but you are sticking to your own choice.

Structure

  1. 1Greeting

    Hi [Name].

  2. 2Acknowledgment

    "I saw your suggestion to choose..."

  3. 3The Pivot (Transition)

    "I understand why you chose it, however..."

  4. 4The Thesis

    "...I believe [Option B] is the better choice."

Step 2: The Comparison (The 3-Point Strategy)

Goal: Compare the items point by point. You must always follow this order: Price, then Contextual Value, then Detail.

Grammar Alert: Comparative Errors

Even native speakers make mistakes here. Use this table to avoid common pitfalls.

Common Error

Correct Usage

  • Rule: Rule 1: -er Words (Short adjectives)

    Common Error:

    "This option is more cheaper." / "It is more faster."

    Correct Usage:

    "This option is cheaper." / "It is faster."

  • Rule: Rule 2: Emphasizing (Big difference)

    Common Error:

    "My option is more cheaper." / "It is very faster."

    Correct Usage:

    "My option is much cheaper." / "It is way faster."

The "Context Rule" (Crucial for High Scores)

You cannot use random adjectives. You must choose words that fit the Person and the Situation.

Example: Buying a bed for a 5-year-old child.

OptionAdjective Strategy (Why?)
Option A (Car-shaped bed)"This is more fun and exciting." (Fits a toddler's personality)
Option B (Vintage wooden bed)"This might be too boring or too serious." (Does not fit a child)

Always ask yourself: "Who is this for?"

Point 1: The Price (ALWAYS First)

Your first argument must always be about money. Use specific numbers.

If your option is cheaper

  • "First, regarding the price, my option only costs a hundred dollars."

  • "This is much cheaper than your option, which costs three hundred dollars. We can save money for other things."

If your option is more expensive

  • "First, looking at the cost, my option is more expensive at six ninety-nine."

  • "However, I think it is worth it because it will last longer and is better quality."

Point 2: Contextual Value (Features that matter)

Based on the context (the "Who" and "Why"), explain why your feature adds more value.

For Convenience

"Also, since we are in a rush, my option is much more convenient because it is closer."

For Fun (Kids/Parties)

"Also, looking at the entertainment, my choice is more exciting for the kids. A car-shaped bed is more engaging than a plain one."

For Comfort

"My option offers a larger seat, which makes the journey more comfortable for grandma."

Point 3: The Detail (Specific Comparison)

Compare a specific detail to seal the deal.

  • "Finally, even though your choice has [Detail A], I think [Detail B] is better because..."

  • "My option is lighter, which makes it easier to carry."

Step 3: The Conclusion

Goal: A confident closing sentence asking for agreement.

Structure

Reiterate your stance and ask for confirmation.

Example

"For these reasons, I think we should go with my suggestion. Let me know if you agree."

Three more CELPIP Task 5 sample answers

Each runs the same three points and lands inside the 60-second window. Notice that two of the three argue for the more expensive option, because that is the version most people never practise.

1Two moving companies, cheaper option2Two weekend courses, more expensive option3Two used cars for a family, more expensive option

Sample 1: Two moving companies, cheaper option (139 words)

Hi Priya. I saw your suggestion to book Maple Movers, and I understand why, because they include packing materials. However, I think Cross-Town Moving is the better choice for us.

First, regarding the price, Cross-Town charges four eighty, while Maple is six fifty. That is a hundred and seventy dollars saved, which covers the deposit on the new place.

Also, since we only have one weekend before the lease starts, Cross-Town is much more convenient. They have a Saturday slot available and Maple does not, so we would have to move on a weekday and both take time off work.

Finally, even though Maple includes boxes, we already have boxes from the last move. We would be paying for something sitting in the hallway.

For these reasons, I think we should book Cross-Town. Let me know if you agree.

Sample 2: Two weekend courses, more expensive option (144 words)

Hi Daniel. I saw your suggestion to sign up for the online course, and I see the appeal because it is only ninety-five dollars. However, I believe the in-person workshop is the better choice for you.

First, looking at the cost, the workshop is more expensive at two twenty. That is a real difference. But I think it is worth it, because the online course has no instructor feedback at all.

Also, given that you want to change careers this year, the workshop is much more valuable. You get four sessions with someone who hires for these roles, and you leave with a portfolio piece rather than a certificate.

Finally, even though the online course is self-paced, that is the reason you did not finish the last one.

For these reasons, I think you should take the workshop. Let me know what you think.

Sample 3: Two used cars for a family, more expensive option (144 words)

Hi Mum. I saw that you prefer the older hatchback at eighty-four hundred, and I understand, because it is the cheaper car by a long way. However, I think the estate at eleven nine hundred is the better choice.

First, regarding the price, yes, the estate costs thirty-five hundred more. But the hatchback needs new tyres and a timing belt, which the listing mentions, so the real gap is closer to two thousand.

Also, since you are driving the grandchildren every week, the estate is much safer and far more comfortable. It has newer airbags and a boot that actually fits the pram.

Finally, even though the hatchback has lower mileage, it is nine years older, and age matters more than mileage for rust on these models.

For these reasons, I think we should go with the estate. Let me know if you agree.

Will the exact Task 5 question you practiced come up?

Almost certainly not, and chasing one specific scenario is the worst way to prepare for this task.

A handful of Task 5 scenarios circulate widely in study groups, Reddit threads and coaching classes, and people search for them by name hoping to find the "right" answer before test day. It is an understandable instinct. It does not work, for three reasons.

Nobody can confirm any of them. CELPIP does not publish a public bank of Task 5 prompts. What it does publish free is two full CELPIP-General practice tests, which between them contain two Task 5 items, plus the practice items inside the paid Speaking Pro study packs. Beyond those, any scenario described online as "the real one" is somebody's recollection. There is no way to verify it and no reason to build your preparation on it.

Start with those two free practice tests. They are the only Task 5 items anyone can point to and say where they came from.

A memorised answer is easy to hear. Task Fulfillment includes tone, and Vocabulary rewards precision fitted to the situation. An answer assembled for a different scenario and bent to fit this one produces exactly the generic adjectives the Context Rule above warns you about. Raters listen to eight of these responses in a row.

The structure transfers and the scenario does not. Every Task 5 prompt has the same skeleton: two options, at least one price, a person with a reason to prefer the other one. Greeting, soft rejection, price, contextual value, detail, close. That shape handles a hotel, a fridge, a dog breeder, a childcare centre or a car without any adjustment.

What to do instead. Practice the shape across many different scenarios until the opening comes out automatically, and force yourself to argue for the more expensive option half the time, because that is the version that catches people out. Our Task 5 question bank has tens of them, and it scores what you record, so you find out whether your third point actually landed or you ran out of things to say at second forty.

Universal Templates

Learn the shape of these lines, not the exact words. A sentence repeated word for word sounds rehearsed, and raters hear eight of these in a row. For the laptop question above, "I understand your point, but looking at the bigger picture, I think..." becomes "I get why the price matters, but think about where you will actually use it." Same move, your words.

Where to practice CELPIP Task 5 questions

Task 5 is the task where reading the template is furthest from being able to do it. Three points in sixty seconds, with two prices said aloud naturally and an adjective chosen to fit a stranger in a scenario you have never seen, is a lot to assemble live. It comes apart in the gap between point two and point three.

Record against a real clock at least fifteen times before you book, more than you would for any other task. Our Task 5 question bank holds more than 80 comparing and persuading scenarios and scores what you record.

FAQ

Speaking Task 5 Template FAQ

Quick answers to the questions test takers ask us most.

Still have a question?Ask the AI CELPIP Coach, any time.
01

How long is CELPIP Speaking Task 5 in total?

Three minutes. Sixty seconds to choose between two options in Part 1, then 60 seconds to prepare and 60 seconds to speak in Part 2. It is the longest single task on the Speaking test.

02

Do you speak during Part 1 of Task 5?

No. CELPIP's own task description says there is no speaking in Part 1. It is a silent 60 seconds in which you read two options and select one. Knowing this in advance is worth more than any phrase in this template, because an unexpected silent screen costs people the first ten seconds of Part 2 as well.

03

Does it matter which of the two options you choose?

CELPIP does not say one option scores better than the other, and there is no published rule here. Our advice, and it is a teaching call rather than an official one: pick the option you can support with specific details from the prompt, not the one you would genuinely choose. Practise arguing for the more expensive option too, because most people default to the cheaper one and then freeze when the prompt makes that awkward.

04

What are CELPIP's key strategies for Speaking Task 5?

CELPIP's study pack gives six, three for each part. In our words: in Part 1, work out who the choice is for, see where the two options really differ, and pick one fast so the rest of the minute is planning time. In Part 2, check what the other person wants instead, find reasons that make your option the better fit for them, and use exact words that add something the picture and caption do not say.

05

What does a real CELPIP Speaking Task 5 question look like?

A short situation, then two options side by side, each with a picture, two or three details and a price. You pick one in Part 1 without speaking. In Part 2 the screen tells you the other person prefers the other option, often with a reason, and you have 60 seconds to prepare and 60 to persuade them. A full example in that format, with an answer, is on this page.

06

Does a Task 5 answer need a conclusion?

Not a formal one. One closing sentence that asks for agreement is plenty. If you reach second 50 with your third comparison unfinished, finish the comparison. A missing point costs more than a missing sign-off. That is our teaching advice, not a published CELPIP rule.

07

Is Task 5 the hardest CELPIP Speaking task?

Many test takers say so, and Task 6 is the other common answer. CELPIP publishes no difficulty ranking, so treat this as opinion rather than fact. What is true is that Task 5 asks for more moving parts in 60 seconds than any other task: two options, two prices, a polite disagreement and three ordered points.

08

How many words should a 60-second Task 5 answer be?

Around 130 to 150 words at a comfortable speaking pace. That is our pacing guide, not a CELPIP rule. CELPIP publishes no word count for Speaking. If your practice answers run past 180 words, the recording will cut you off before your conclusion.

09

Is grammar scored on CELPIP Speaking Task 5?

Not as a separate score. CELPIP Speaking is rated on Content/Coherence, Vocabulary, Listenability and Task Fulfillment. CELPIP's Speaking Performance Standards count grammar and sentence structure as one item inside Listenability. Grammar still matters, because a wrong comparative or a tense slip that confuses the listener lands there.
