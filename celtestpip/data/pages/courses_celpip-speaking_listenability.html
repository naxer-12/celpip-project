Listenability Toolkit: Rhythm, Pausing, Self-Correction

Listenability is one of the four CELPIP Speaking scores. Here are its four official factors, what holds an answer at Level 8, and the four joining words that separate a Level 8 answer from a 12.

Listenability is one of the four areas every speaking answer is scored on. The name makes it sound like it is about your accent, or how smooth your voice is.

Half of it is. The other half is how your sentences are built, and that half is where most answers lose their level.

The four factors

CELPIP publishes what Listenability measures. It is four things:

Rhythm, pronunciation, and intonation
Pauses, interjections, and self-correction
Grammar and sentence structure
Variety of sentence structure

The first two are about delivery: how it sounds when it comes out. The last two are about construction: how the sentence was put together before it came out.

Most advice on this area, including the old version of this page, spends nearly all its time on the first two. From the answers we score every week, that is the wrong way round.

What separates a Level 8 from a Level 10

Here is one Task 1 question, answered twice. We wrote both answers for this page.

The question: Your friend wants to start exercising, but works long hours and has very little free time. Give your friend some advice.

A Level 8 answer (the middle part):

I think you should start with walking, because it is easy. You can walk to work and you can walk at lunch. It is free and it is good for your health. You can also do exercise at home, so you don't need a gym. And you can use an app, so it tells you what to do.

A Level 10 answer (the same part):

If you only have twenty minutes a day, I would start with walking, which fits into time you already have. Although a gym feels more serious, it adds travel time, whereas a short routine next to your bed costs you nothing. Once that becomes a habit, you can add one longer walk on the weekend.

What we hear. Say the first one slowly and clearly and the delivery is fine. What holds it at 8 is that every sentence has the same shape, joined with and, so, also, because.

The second one gives the same advice with no harder words. What changed is the joins: if, which, although, whereas, once. Five different kinds of sentence in four lines. Fewer ands, more kinds of sentence. That is the jump.

The ladder, in three lines

Sentence variety climbs in three steps:

Level 8: mostly simple sentences.

Level 9: complex sentences used fairly well, but mostly the same kind.

Higher levels: a variety of complex sentences, joined with words like although, which, if and whereas.

Three steps. Simple. Complex but the same kind. Complex and different kinds.

Notice what the Level 9 step is missing. Not more complex sentences. More variety. Four sentences all built with because is one kind of sentence four times.

The four words

WordWhat it doesExample
althoughAdmits something, then moves past itAlthough the course is expensive, it is the only one with evening classes.
whichAdds a detail without starting a new sentenceThe bus stops outside the library, which is open until nine.
ifShows a result that depends on somethingIf you leave at seven, you miss the worst of the traffic.
whereasPuts two things side by sideThe first apartment is a ten-minute walk, whereas the second is two buses.

One of each is enough. In a sixty-second answer, four joins like these, each used once, is the variety the top step describes. Four althoughs is not.

And these are not rare words. You already know all four, and you have probably used every one of them this week without thinking about it, so the problem is almost never that you do not know them; it is that under a clock, with a stranger's question on the screen and sixty seconds running, and is the word that comes out first.

The same content, joined two ways

A piece of Task 1 advice, built only with and, so, but:

The bigger apartment is cheaper and it has a balcony, but it is far from your work. You would take two buses and it takes an hour, so you would be tired. The small one is close, so you could walk.

Same information, one join of each kind:

Although the bigger apartment is cheaper, it is an hour from your work, which means two buses every morning and two every evening. If you take it, that is ten hours a week on a bus, whereas the small one is a fifteen-minute walk.

The second version is shorter. It has no new ideas and no difficult words. It sounds more organised because the sentences carry the relationships between the ideas, instead of leaving them all joined by and.

Because, so and for example are not the problem

They are good words and you should use them. The problem is using nothing else.

Good control of simple sentences is a real strength. Simple sentences, done well, are a Level 8 skill. What moves an answer above that is adding the other kinds of sentence alongside them, not throwing the simple ones away.

So keep because and so. Add although, which, if and whereas.

Stories are joined differently

For Task 2, where you tell a story, the joining words that matter are about time, not logic.

Strictly, this sits under Content and Coherence rather than Listenability, because it is about how the story is organised. It belongs on this page anyway, because it is about the words that join your sentences.

A story joined with common transitions like and, but, so, because reads like a list. A story joined with time words like when, the first time, from there, once, and then reads like a story.

Joins a story like a listJoins a story like a story
and · but · so · becausewhen · the first time · started · from there · once · and then

❌ I got off the train and I went the wrong way and my phone died, so I was lost.
✅ When I got off the train I turned the wrong way, and then my phone died, and from there I was completely lost.

Trying beats playing safe

This is the idea that should change how you practise.

A higher answer often tries harder structures and does not always get them right. Take Although the rent is lower, but it is far from your work. The but should not be there. Still, the speaker reached for a two-part sentence, and that shows more range than three perfect simple sentences in a row.

Emerging control means trying the harder structure and not always getting it right, and it is typically rewarded above an answer that stays in simple sentences and makes fewer errors.

So when you practise, do not avoid although because you are worried about the grammar after it. Say it. A complex sentence with a small error in it is the Level 9 pattern. A perfect simple sentence is the Level 8 one.

The other two factors: delivery

Rhythm, pronunciation and intonation. Pauses, interjections and self-correction. These matter too. The full page on them is Fluency and Pronunciation. Three things are worth knowing now.

Faster is not better. A quick pace can make an answer hard to follow, even at Level 9. Speaking quickly to sound fluent can cost you on this area.

Long pauses cost you twice. A long silence while you look for a word hurts your rhythm, and it also leaves less of the task done. It is a Listenability problem and a completeness problem at the same time.

Self-correcting is allowed, repeatedly is not. A few hesitations and self-corrections are normal, even in a Level 9 answer. Fixing a word is normal. Fixing every other word breaks the listener's thread.

Common mistakes

Joining everything with and. The habit that most often keeps a good answer at 9.

Four complex sentences, all the same kind. The top of the ladder is variety, not quantity.

Avoiding complex sentences because you might make an error. A complex sentence with a small slip still shows more range than a safe simple one.

Joining a story with so and because. Stories move with time words: when, the first time, from there, once, and then.

Speaking faster to sound fluent. Pace that is too quick makes you harder to follow.

Stopping in silence to find the perfect word. Say it in simpler words and keep going. The pause costs more than the plain word.

Before you speak

  • I have although, which, if and whereas ready, and I plan to use each at least once

  • If it is a story, I have time words ready, not only and and so

  • I will try the harder sentence even if the grammar after it is not perfect

  • I will speak at a pace a stranger could follow, not as fast as I can

Practice

1. Count your joins (5 min). Record any sixty-second answer. Listen back and write down every word that joins two ideas. If the list is mostly and, so, but, because, you have found your Level 8 pattern.

2. One of each (5 min). Take the same answer and rewrite it so that although, which, if and whereas each appear once. Do not add new ideas. Record it again and compare.

3. The story version (5 min). Tell any short story from your week using only when, the first time, once, from there, and then, after that to move between events. No and so.

4. Try and miss (3 min). Say five sentences that start with Although, about anything. Some will come out wrong. That is the exercise.
