What is the CELPIP Speaking test format?

The CELPIP Speaking test gives you 8 tasks and one prompt per task, with 15 minutes allotted for the speaking itself. Expect closer to 20 minutes in the chair once you add the on-screen instructions.

Everything happens on a computer. You read each prompt on screen, you get a short window to prepare, then you speak into a headset microphone while the system records you. There is no interviewer sitting across from you, and no conversation. That surprises people who have taken IELTS, and it changes how you should practise: you are talking to a screen with a countdown running, not to a person who nods along.

Speaking appears in both versions of the test. CELPIP-General includes it alongside Listening, Reading, and Writing for permanent residence and professional purposes. CELPIP-General LS includes only Listening and Speaking, and that is the version IRCC (Immigration, Refugees and Citizenship Canada) accepts for Canadian citizenship. The Speaking section is identical in both.

You can confirm the task list and timing on the official CELPIP test format page from Paragon Testing.

What are the 8 CELPIP Speaking tasks?

The 8 tasks run in a fixed order, and each one tests a different job you do with language: advising, narrating, describing, predicting, persuading, negotiating, arguing, and explaining something strange.

What does a high-scoring CELPIP Speaking answer sound like?

A high-scoring answer opens with a direct response, builds two or three supported points, and closes on purpose rather than trailing off when the timer dies. Here is a full Task 1 example so you can hear the shape.

Prompt (Task 1: Giving Advice). Your cousin has been offered a promotion, but the job is in another city and she would have to leave her friends behind. She has asked what you think. Give her advice. You have 30 seconds to prepare and 90 seconds to speak.

Model answer.

"Hi Nadia, I know this one is keeping you up at night, so let me tell you how I would think about it.

First, I would separate the job from the move. The promotion sounds like a real step up, and turning down a title change often means waiting two or three years for the next one. So ask yourself whether you would take this role if it were down the street. If the answer is yes, then the real question is just the city.

Second, give the friendship worry a time limit. You are two hours away by train, not on another continent, and the first six months are the hard part. Book one visit home a month for that period and put it in your calendar now, before you move, so it is a plan rather than a hope.

One more thing. Ask your manager whether the company will cover relocation, because that answer tells you how much they want you.

Honestly, I think you should take it. You have wanted this role for a long time, and the distance is a problem you can manage."

Why it scores well. Read it against the four criteria the examiners actually use:

CriterionWhat this answer does
Content and CoherenceTwo clear pieces of advice plus a practical extra, each with a reason. Signposted with "First", "Second", "One more thing", so a listener never loses the thread.
VocabularyPrecise and natural, not showy: "a real step up", "cover relocation", "a problem you can manage". No memorised idiom dropped in where it does not fit.
ListenabilitySentences vary in length, so the rhythm sounds like speech rather than recital. The opening line is short and gives you a confident start while your brain catches up.
Task FulfillmentIt advises a named person about her actual decision, and it commits to a recommendation. Task 1 asks you to advise, so sitting on the fence costs you here.

Notice what the answer does not do. It does not open with "Well, that is a very interesting question", it does not list five thin points, and it does not use a single memorised template sentence. Examiners hear rehearsed openers constantly, and a canned start followed by natural speech reads as coached rather than fluent.

Now try the same prompt yourself before you look at anything else. Record it, play it back, and count how many seconds you spend saying nothing.

What topics come up on the CELPIP Speaking test?

CELPIP Speaking topics stay in everyday Canadian life, so you will not be asked about economics, politics, or your professional field. The scenarios are the sort of thing you would actually talk about with a friend, a neighbour, or a manager.

Across our prompts, the recurring territory looks like this:

Topic areaTypical prompts
Work and study decisionsTaking a promotion, handling a difficult coworker, changing shifts, joining a study group
Home and neighbourhoodRenting versus buying, a noisy building, a community cleanup, a shared cottage
Money and consumer choicesBuying a used car, meal prepping to cut costs, choosing between two laptops
Travel and leisureA first camping trip, a destination wedding, booking flights, a fitness class
Family and social plansHosting a dinner party, a family reunion, an exchange student, adopting a pet
Scenes and objectsMarkets, parks, stations, plus deliberately strange objects for Task 8

Two practical consequences. You do not need specialist vocabulary, so stop memorising academic word lists for this section. You do need fluent everyday language for describing, comparing, and persuading, which our per-task vocabulary sets group by task.

How is the CELPIP Speaking test scored?

Trained raters score CELPIP Speaking across all 8 tasks on four criteria, and it is not marked out of 38. That last point matters, because Reading and Listening do use a raw score out of 38 and candidates routinely assume Speaking works the same way. It does not. There is no number of correct answers to count.

The four criteria are Content and Coherence, Vocabulary, Listenability, and Task Fulfillment. Trained raters weigh them together and place you on the CELPIP level scale, which runs from M through 12. Your CELPIP Speaking level then equals your Canadian Language Benchmark (CLB) level one to one, so a CELPIP Speaking 9 is CLB 9.

CELPIP Speaking levelCLB levelWhat it generally means for you
7CLB 7Clears the Express Entry minimum for most main programs
8CLB 8Comfortably above the floor, better Comprehensive Ranking System position
9CLB 9The level worth targeting, since CLB 9 across all four abilities unlocks the higher points band

The scale is described on Paragon's CELPIP scoring levels page. For the full level by level breakdown, including how Speaking compares with the other three sections, see our CELPIP Speaking score chart.

One caution on estimates, ours included. Any AI or self-assessed CLB figure is an estimate, not an official result. Use it to see whether you are trending up, and let the official score report settle the number.

What CELPIP Speaking score do I need for PR or citizenship?

Most Express Entry applicants need CLB 7 in Speaking, which covers the Federal Skilled Worker program and the Canadian Experience Class for TEER 0 and TEER 1 jobs. The Federal Skilled Trades program and Canadian Experience Class TEER 2 and TEER 3 accept CLB 5 in Speaking.

Those figures come from IRCC's Express Entry language requirements, which sets CLB 7 in all four abilities for the Federal Skilled Worker program, CLB 7 for Canadian Experience Class TEER 0 and 1 jobs, CLB 5 for Canadian Experience Class TEER 2 and 3, and CLB 5 in speaking and listening for the Federal Skilled Trades program.

Canadian citizenship works differently. It uses CELPIP-General LS, which tests Listening and Speaking only. If you are 18 to 54 years of age you must prove CLB Level 4 or higher in speaking and listening. Outside that age range you are exempt.

IRCC assesses each of the four abilities on its own. Your Speaking level has to clear the bar by itself, so a strong Reading score will not lift it. That is worth knowing before you decide where to spend your remaining study weeks.

Aim past the floor rather than at it. If CLB 7 is your minimum, target CLB 9, because the gap between clearing the bar and scoring well is what shows up in your Comprehensive Ranking System (CRS) points. Program minimums change, so check the current thresholds for your stream against the minimum CELPIP scores for each immigration program guide before you book.

How can I improve my CELPIP Speaking score?

Record yourself, listen back, and fix one thing at a time. That single habit moves more candidates up a level than any amount of reading about the test.

Use the preparation window properly. In 30 seconds you cannot write a script, and trying to is why people freeze. Plan three beats instead: your opening line, point one, point two. The opening line is the one worth having ready, because a confident first sentence buys you five seconds of thinking time for everything after it.

Fill the time, then stop. Running short is common and it costs you on Content and Coherence, since a 35-second answer to a 60-second task looks underdeveloped no matter how clean the grammar is. Running long is less damaging: the recording simply cuts, and a mid-sentence cut on a well-developed answer scores better than silence.

Work one task at a time rather than sampling all 8. Take Task 6, record ten prompts across a week, and read the feedback on each. Patterns show up fast, and they are usually the same two or three habits repeating.
