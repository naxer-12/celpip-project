Practise Writing Task 2

CELPIP Writing Task 2 gives you 26 minutes to write 150 to 200 words. You read a short survey about a local issue, choose one of two options, and explain why. No email format is needed, so there is no greeting and no sign-off. Prometric (which owns CELPIP; it acquired the test's maker, Paragon Testing Enterprises, in 2021) rates your answer on four things: Content/Coherence, Vocabulary, Readability, and Task Fulfillment.

How long is CELPIP Writing Task 2 and how many words?

You get 26 minutes and 150 to 200 words. Task 1 gets 27 minutes, so the two tasks are not the same length, and the whole Writing component runs 53 minutes.

Those numbers come from Prometric directly. Its CELPIP-General Overview Study Pack gives Task 2 a 26-minute time limit and a length of 150 to 200 words, and its Writing Pro: Target 9+ Study Pack confirms the split: 27 minutes for Task 1 and 26 minutes for Task 2.

On word count, people want a hard number for how far they can stray. There isn't one. Prometric's published guidance is that you have a small amount of room either side of the range, but your score can drop if you go well over or well under it. Any blog quoting a precise buffer like 135 to 220 words invented it.

Here is the more useful anchor: aim for about 180 words. A strong answer does not need to reach 200, and the extra 20 words at the end are usually where people repeat themselves and make mistakes.

Aim for about 180 and stop.

The screen gives you basic editing tools, an automatic word counter, and a spell check. Prometric does warn that picking the right word can be hard when the spell check gives you several suggestions. In plain terms, the spell check will flag a word and then hand you five options with no help picking one. It catches spelling only. It will not catch affect where you meant effect, and it will not catch a verb that disagrees with its subject.

What is the CELPIP Writing Task 2 template?

Four parts: your choice, reason A with an example, reason B with an example, and a close that acknowledges the other side. Four paragraphs, one blank line between each.

PartLengthWhat it does
  1. 1

    1Part: The choice

    Length

    1 to 2 sentences

    What it does

    Name your side and your main reason. No warm-up, no "both options are good."

  2. 2

    2Part: Reason A plus example

    Length

    1 paragraph

    What it does

    One idea, made real with a small concrete detail.

  3. 3

    3Part: Reason B plus example

    Length

    1 paragraph

    What it does

    A different angle on the topic, again with a detail.

  4. 4

    4Part: The close plus the nod

    Length

    2 to 3 sentences

    What it does

    Acknowledge the other option, answer it, restate your choice.

That layout is doing work for you before the rater reads a word. Paragraphing sits inside Readability, one of the four scored dimensions, and a rater who has to hunt through a wall of text for your structure is a rater you have already made work harder than necessary.

Two of those four parts are not just my preference. CELPIP's own Task 2 guidance says to open with your opinion, close with a concluding statement, and compare your choice with the other option. Parts 1, 4 and the nod are the test owner's advice, not a template someone invented.

Where reasons come from

You need two reasons that genuinely differ. "It helps families" and "it helps parents with young children" is one reason written twice, and it costs you under Content/Coherence.

Pick any two rows from this grid, as long as they are different rows.

AngleThe question it answers

  • People affected

    Who does this help or hurt most?

  • Time

    Does this save or waste people's time?

  • Money

    What does it cost, or save, and for whom?

  • Safety

    Does anyone get hurt, or feel unsafe?

  • Fairness and access

    Does this leave a group out?

  • Reliability

    Does it work consistently, or only sometimes?

  • Environment

    Waste, energy, noise, green space.

  • Practicality

    Is it actually workable day to day?

Where examples come from

This is the step people freeze on. You have never lived in a building with a delivery policy, so what do you write?

You are not being fact-checked. Prometric's own advice is to bring in real places, people and situations so your details feel specific and easy to picture, and a small plausible detail does that job perfectly well. What is being scored is whether you can turn an abstract claim into something specific.

A detail counts if it has at least one of these: a time, a number, a place, or a specific person.

Not an example

An example

  • Not an example:

    "This causes many problems for residents."

    An example:

    "Last Saturday the queue by the playground was 15 minutes long."

  • Not an example:

    "Buses are often late."

    An example:

    "On Main Street a 20-minute trip regularly takes 40."

  • Not an example:

    "It is difficult for some people."

    An example:

    "A parent with a sleeping baby cannot leave the apartment to collect a meal."

What are the two Task 2 prompt shapes?

Two-option prompts ask you to pick between A and B. Agree/disagree prompts give you one proposal and ask whether you support it. The template does not change between them.

Most preparation only covers the first shape, which is fine right up until you meet the second one on test day with 26 minutes on the clock.

  • Shape 1, two-option.

    Should delivery drivers leave orders at the lobby desk, or bring them to each door?

  • Shape 2, agree/disagree.

    The community centre should extend its weekday hours until 9:00 p.m. Do you agree or disagree?

Only two things differ.

Everything in between is identical. Learn one map and read the prompt carefully enough to know which shape you are in.

A note on where this comes from: Prometric describes Task 2 as choosing between two options. The agree/disagree framing is my own observation from the prompts my students actually report seeing, not something Prometric publishes. Prepare for both and you cannot be surprised.

How is CELPIP Writing Task 2 scored?

Prometric rates Writing on four dimensions: Content/Coherence, Vocabulary, Readability, and Task Fulfillment. There is no points formula and no fixed number of marks per dimension. Prometric publishes the four and what sits inside each one, and does not publish weights.

01Content/Coherence

What the rater is weighing

How many ideas you have, how good they are, how you order them, and the examples that back them up

The question behind it

Are your ideas well organised and fully developed?

02Vocabulary

What the rater is weighing

Choosing the right words, using them naturally, having enough variety, and being exact

The question behind it

Do you have enough words, and do you use them naturally?

03Readability

What the rater is weighing

Layout and paragraphs, linking words between ideas, grammar and sentence structure, spelling and punctuation

The question behind it

Can a reader follow your response without effort?

04Task Fulfillment

What the rater is weighing

Answering what was asked, covering every part, the right tone, and word count

The question behind it

Did you do everything the task asked, in a tone that suits the reader?

That table is our own summary of CELPIP's published Writing performance standards. The official version is on celpip.ca.

Two things in it surprise people.

Grammar is not its own score. It sits inside Readability, alongside spelling, punctuation and paragraphing. A grammatically perfect answer with no paragraph breaks and no transitions can still lose Readability marks.

Word count is scored under Task Fulfillment. Not as a separate penalty, but as one of four factors alongside relevance, completeness and tone. Being significantly short is a Task Fulfillment problem, the same category as ignoring half the prompt.

If you have seen "Coherence and Cohesion" or "Lexical Resource" on a CELPIP page, ignore it

Those are IELTS terms, and they turn up constantly on CELPIP prep sites, including on an earlier version of this page. CELPIP does not use them. There is no CELPIP dimension called Coherence and Cohesion, no standalone Grammar score, and no Lexical Resource. If a resource is using IELTS vocabulary to describe CELPIP scoring, it is describing a different test, and you should be careful about what else it gets wrong.

For how these four dimensions translate into a CELPIP Level and a CLB (Canadian Language Benchmark) number, see how CELPIP Writing scores map to CLB. CELPIP Levels and CLB levels run one to one from 2 through 12.

Should you write Task 2 like an email or an essay?

Neither. Prometric's own Task 2 advice makes two points that most prep material ignores: Task 2 is not an academic essay, and you do not need to use email format.

Both appear in Prometric's own published study materials, the first in the Writing Pro: Target 9+ Study Pack and the second in the 2026 CELPIP-General Overview Study Pack. Read them together and the target register is clear. You are a person answering a survey about your own street, your own building, or your own workplace. Not a consultant filing a report.

This matters more than any template line, because the essay register is the single most common way a strong English speaker caps their own score. An answer can be grammatically flawless, correctly structured and exactly 180 words, and still stall, because it reads like a memo instead of a person.

"Temperature degradation" just means the food gets cold. The longer phrase is not more advanced. It is only heavier, and reaching for heavy words at speed is how small errors creep in. Vocabulary is scored on precision and fit, not difficulty.

The padding diagnostic

Run this over your own draft. Every pattern below is a symptom of writing to impress rather than to communicate.

Example

Replace with

  • Pattern: Padded opener

    Example:

    "After careful consideration, I strongly support…"

    Replace with:

    "I support…"

  • Pattern: Announcing yourself

    Example:

    "It is worth noting that…" / "I would like to state that…"

    Replace with:

    (delete, just say the thing)

  • Pattern: Latinate abstract noun

    Example:

    "temperature degradation"

    Replace with:

    "the food gets cold"

  • Pattern: Corporate verb

    Example:

    "optimizes," "safeguards," "facilitates"

    Replace with:

    "helps," "protects," "makes easier"

  • Pattern: Institutional phrase

    Example:

    "promotes inclusivity," "fosters widespread dissatisfaction"

    Replace with:

    "includes more people," "annoys residents"

  • Pattern: Formal inevitability

    Example:

    "would inevitably lead to"

    Replace with:

    "would cause"

  • Pattern: Essay-speak closer

    Example:

    "In summation, the aforementioned proposals…"

    Replace with:

    "For these reasons, …"

The test: read your sentence out loud. Would you say it to a friend describing the same situation? If not, rewrite it in the words you would actually use.

On greetings and sign-offs

Prometric says email format is not needed. That is not the same as forbidden, and a greeting will not cost you marks on its own.

My recommendation is still to skip it. Open with your opinion instead. You have 180 words, a greeting and a sign-off cost you roughly ten of them, and they buy you nothing on any of the four dimensions. Spend those ten words on a concrete detail instead.

CELPIP Writing Task 2 sample answers

Six answers below, three of each prompt shape. Each one states its word count and the two angles it uses, so you can see the machinery rather than just the output.

Read them for structure, not for phrases to memorize. A rater who has seen the same sentence four hundred times is not impressed by the four hundred and first, and Prometric's policy goes further than that: memorised or copied content can invalidate a result. Here is where the line sits between a structure and a script.

Sample 1: Two-option, transit (169 words)

Prompt

Your city can add more bus-only lanes or reduce bus fares by 10%. Choose one and explain why it helps commuters more.

I support adding more bus-only lanes, because they cut travel time and make service reliable. Price matters, but people stop using buses when trips take too long or the schedule cannot be trusted.

First, dedicated lanes keep buses moving during rush hour. On Main Street, a trip that should take 20 minutes often becomes 40 because buses sit behind single-occupant cars. A bus lane would let them bypass that queue and stay on schedule.

Second, faster and more predictable trips bring in new riders. Commuters plan around time, not small savings. If the ride is quicker, more students and workers will leave the car at home, which reduces traffic for everyone.

A 10% fare cut does help people who already ride, and for someone on a tight budget that is real money every month. But a cheaper ticket does not make the bus arrive. When buses run late, people miss classes and shifts, and a discount does not repair that. For these reasons, bus-only lanes deliver the bigger benefit.

Angles: time and reliability, then people affected. The nod concedes that the fare cut genuinely helps someone specific, limits it, and redirects.

Sample 2: Agree/disagree, workplace (176 words)

Prompt

Your employer proposes allowing staff to work from home two days a week. Statement: "The company should allow two work-from-home days." Do you agree or disagree?

I agree that the company should allow two work-from-home days. The main reason is that the office day and the productive day are not the same thing for everyone.

First, quiet time is hard to find in an open office. Work that needs concentration, like writing reports and checking figures, gets broken up by interruptions every few minutes. Two days at home would give people a stretch of focused time without changing their total hours.

Second, the commute is a real cost. Many staff spend over an hour a day travelling, which is time and money spent on nothing productive. Removing that twice a week gives people back most of a working day each month.

Of course, some work genuinely needs people in the same room, and new staff learn far faster sitting beside someone experienced than they do over a video call. That is a fair concern, but it argues for agreeing which two days everyone is in the office, not for scrapping the idea. For these reasons, two days at home is worth trying.

Angles: practicality, then time and money. Only the opening line and the direction of the nod changed from Sample 1.

Sample 3: Two-option, parks (162 words)

Prompt

Your neighbourhood association has money for one improvement to the local park: more benches, or a new washroom building. Which do you support?

I support building the washroom, because it decides whether people can stay in the park at all.

First, the current single washroom cannot handle a busy weekend. At the summer concert in July, the line by the playground ran about 15 minutes, and I watched three families with small children pack up and leave before the music finished. Benches would not have kept any of them there.

Second, a proper washroom keeps the park usable in the cold months. Right now the portable unit is closed from November, so the walking path sits empty all winter even on clear days. Older neighbours in particular will not walk a loop that has nowhere to stop.

More benches would be genuinely pleasant, and anyone who has carried a toddler across that field knows the value of somewhere to sit. But a bench only improves a visit you were already able to make. For these reasons, the washroom is the better use of the money.

Angles: fairness and access, then reliability.

Sample 4: Agree/disagree, library (160 words)

Prompt

Your city library is considering staying open until 9:00 p.m. on weekdays instead of closing at 6:00 p.m. Statement: "The library should extend its weekday hours." Do you agree or disagree?

I agree that the library should stay open until 9:00 p.m., because the current closing time locks out the people who need it most.

First, most shift workers and newcomers finish work around 6:00 p.m. By the time they arrive the doors are shut. My neighbour is studying for a licensing exam and has one quiet place to do it, and three evenings a week she gets there ten minutes too late.

Second, later hours would spread the crowd out. Between 4:00 and 6:00 every computer is taken and there is a wait for the printers, while the study rooms sit empty at lunchtime. Adding three evening hours moves some of that pressure off the late afternoon.

Longer hours do cost more in staffing, and that money has to come from somewhere in a tight budget. But a building that is closed when working people are free is not really serving them. For these reasons, I support the extended hours.

Angles: people affected, then fairness and access.

Sample 5: Two-option, apartment building (158 words)

Prompt

Your building is choosing a new delivery policy: drivers leave all orders at the lobby desk, or drivers bring orders to each apartment door. Which do you support?

I support keeping deliveries at the lobby desk, because it is the only version that works when nobody is home.

First, door delivery assumes someone is there to answer. Most people on my floor work until at least 5:00 p.m., so a package left in a hallway sits unattended for hours. Two were taken from the seventh floor last winter, and neither was recovered.

Second, a single collection point is far easier to run. The desk staff already sign for mail, so adding parcels costs the building nothing, while door delivery means letting a different driver into the residential floors several times a day.

Door delivery is undeniably better for anyone who struggles with stairs or heavy boxes, and that is a real group of people in this building, not a small one. But the desk can hold a box for someone and bring it up on request. For these reasons, the lobby desk is the safer choice.

Angles: safety, then practicality.

Sample 6: Agree/disagree, city centre (168 words)

Prompt

Your city is considering closing the four downtown blocks of Main Street to cars every Sunday. Statement: "Main Street should be closed to cars on Sundays." Do you agree or disagree?

I agree that Main Street should close to cars on Sundays, because the street is worth more to the city as a space than as a road.

First, Sunday traffic there is light and the noise is not. Two lanes of cars run past cafes with outdoor seating that nobody uses, and the exhaust and engine noise are the reason. Closing the road for one day turns four blocks of asphalt into the largest public space downtown at no construction cost.

Second, the shops would gain rather than lose. When the city closed the same stretch for the food festival in May, several owners said it was their best day of the year. People who walk past a window stop; people driving past it do not.

The one group with a fair objection is drivers who use Main Street to reach the hospital, and a Sunday closure cannot simply ignore them. But two parallel routes already exist one block over. For these reasons, I support the Sunday closure.

Angles: environment, then money.

What does a Level 7, Level 9 and Level 10 Task 2 answer look like?

At every level the writer picks an option. What moves the score is how concrete the reasons are, how fairly the other option is treated, and how cleanly the sentences are built. Below is one survey question answered three times, as our examples of a Level 7, a Level 9 and a Level 10 response, each checked against Prometric's four rating categories.

These are our own practice answers, written to match Prometric's published Writing level descriptors (May 2026) and compared with the rated Task 2 responses (Levels 8, 9 and 10) in its Writing Pro: Target 9+ Study Pack. They are not official CELPIP responses, and no CELPIP rater scored them. CELPIP levels match CLB levels one to one.

Prompt

Your town has enough money for one new project next year. Which option do you prefer? Explain the reasons for your choice.

  • Option A: Build an outdoor skating rink in the town park.
  • Option B: Run a free weekend shuttle bus between the town centre and the hospital.

Level 7 example (155 words)

I think the town should choose the shuttle bus to the hospital, because it is more useful for many people.

First, a lot of people in our town don't have a car, for example old people and students. When they need to go to the hospital on the weekend, the bus is not frequent and a taxi is expensive. A free shuttle can help them to go there easy and safe.

Second, the hospital is far from the town centre. My grandmother goes there every Saturday for her check-up, and my mother must to drive her. If there is a shuttle, my mother can use her time for other things.

The skating rink is also a good idea for children and families in winter. But it is only for fun and it is useful only for few months. The shuttle is important for health and it works all year. So I choose the shuttle bus.

The errors in this answer are deliberate.

Why this is a Level 7 answer.

Content/Coherence

a clear choice and two reasons, each with a supporting detail. That fits the Level 7 descriptor, which in our words asks for a main idea backed by some detail. But "more useful for many people" is general.

Vocabulary

common words throughout ("useful" twice, "good idea", "for fun"), with few words specific to transport or health.

Readability

good paragraphing and mostly correct grammar. Errors appear ("must to drive", "go there easy and safe", "for few months"), but none of them block the meaning, which fits what the Level 7 descriptor expects: complex sentences handled adequately, not perfectly.

Task Fulfillment

the other option is mentioned fairly, and the tone suits a survey. The ending, "So I choose the shuttle bus", repeats the opening rather than concluding it.

Level 9 example (175 words)

I believe the town should fund the free weekend shuttle between the town centre and the hospital, because it meets a real need for many residents.

First, not everyone in our town can drive. Seniors, teenagers and people with low incomes often depend on buses, but on weekends the regular bus runs only once every two hours. A free shuttle would let them reach appointments or visit family members in hospital without paying for an expensive taxi.

Second, the shuttle would help families like mine. My grandmother has a check-up every Saturday, and my mother has to take half a day off to drive her. With a shuttle, my grandmother could travel on her own, and my mother could spend that time with her children.

A skating rink would certainly be fun for families during the winter, and I understand why many people support it. However, it would only be used for three or four months a year, while the shuttle would serve people every weekend. For these reasons, the shuttle is the better choice.

Why this is a Level 9 answer.

Content/Coherence

the reasons now carry facts ("once every two hours", "half a day off"). That is what the Level 9 descriptor asks for, in our words: main ideas backed by facts and details that fit.

Vocabulary

accurate and suited to the topic ("low incomes", "depend on", "travel on her own"). "Shuttle" is repeated often, a small weakness.

Readability

well-organized paragraphs, good control of spelling and punctuation, and no errors that affect meaning. Transitions are correct but predictable ("First", "Second", "However").

Task Fulfillment

choice stated at the start, the other option compared directly, and a real concluding sentence.

What changed from Level 7: general claims became numbers and consequences, the grammar errors disappeared, and the comparison with the rink now does real work.

Level 10 example (183 words)

The town should put this money into a free weekend shuttle to the hospital. A rink would be enjoyable, but the shuttle solves a problem that people face every week.

On weekends, the regular bus to the hospital runs only every two hours, and a taxi costs about $25 each way. For seniors on a fixed income, or teenagers visiting a parent after surgery, that combination turns a simple visit into an ordeal. A free shuttle removes both the wait and the cost.

It would also take pressure off families. My grandmother has a check-up every Saturday morning, which means my mother loses half of her weekend driving her there and waiting in the parking lot. A reliable shuttle would give my grandmother some independence back and give my mother her Saturdays back.

To be fair, an outdoor rink would bring families together during the long winter months. Still, it would sit empty from March to November, whereas the shuttle would be used all year. When one project is about enjoyment and the other is about access to care, I would choose access.

Why this is a Level 10 answer.

Content/Coherence

each reason is built from a range of specific facts and shows who is affected and how. That matches the Level 10 descriptor, which in our words asks for a wider variety of facts and details behind each main idea.

Vocabulary

precise, natural choices: "fixed income", "an ordeal", "access to care". The Level 10 descriptor, in our words, asks for words chosen to describe and compare exactly.

Readability

varied structures ("which means", "whereas", "To be fair ... Still") connect ideas within and between paragraphs, not only at the start of each one.

Task Fulfillment

the choice comes first, the rink gets a fair hearing, and the last sentence turns the comparison into a principle instead of repeating the opening.

What changed from Level 9: the transitions stopped being labels ("First", "Second") and started showing how ideas relate, and the ending says something new.

How should you spend the 26 minutes?

My recommendation is 3 minutes planning, 18 minutes writing, and 5 minutes checking. Prometric's own recommendation is slightly different, and both work, so here is each one plainly.

0 to 3
3 to 21
21 to 26
  1. Minutes:

    0 to 3

    What you are doing

    Read the prompt. Identify the shape. Pick your side in 30 seconds and jot two reasons from different angle rows.

  2. Minutes:

    3 to 21

    What you are doing

    Write all four parts. Do not stop to polish.

  3. Minutes:

    21 to 26

    What you are doing

    Check the word count, reread twice, fix the two worst problems.

Prometric suggests about 4 to 5 minutes of planning for each response and about 3 minutes of checking once you finish writing. That is a real alternative from the test owner, and if you plan slowly and edit fast, use it.

I push the planning shorter and the checking longer for one reason: almost nobody loses marks for a thin plan, and almost everybody loses marks in the last paragraph they wrote in a hurry. Neither split is official. Prometric publishes no required breakdown of the 26 minutes, so this is a coaching choice and you should take whichever one matches how you work.

The 30-second rule on picking a side is the part I would not bend. There is no correct answer, and nobody is scoring your opinion. Ask only this: which side can I give two concrete examples for, right now? Choose that one, even if you personally prefer the other, and do not reopen the question at minute twelve.

CELPIP Writing tips

What mistakes cap your Task 2 score?

The three that cost the most marks are treating it like an email, arguing both sides equally, and writing in an essay register. Here is the full list with the fix.

  • No clear choice

    Weak:

    "Both options have advantages and the city should consider carefully."

    Better:

    "I support bus-only lanes because they cut travel time."

  • Treating it like an email

    Weak:

    "Dear Sir or Madam, … Kind regards, Amir."

    Better:

    No greeting, no sign-off. Open with your opinion. This is Task 1's job, not Task 2's.

  • Essay tone

    Weak:

    "In summation, the aforementioned proposals exhibit considerable merit."

    Better:

    "For these reasons, bus lanes help more people."

  • Reasons with no support

    Weak:

    "It is cheaper, cleaner, and easier."

    Better:

    "It is faster in rush hour. The 5:40 bus regularly arrives after 6:00, so riders miss the transfer."

  • Two reasons that are really one

    Weak:

    "It helps families." plus "It helps parents with children."

    Better:

    Pick two different rows from the angle grid.

  • Arguing both sides equally

    Weak:

    "Lanes are good, but fares are also good, and both help people."

    Better:

    Commit to one. The other side appears only in the closing nod.

  • A nod that collapses

    Weak:

    "Of course, extra parking is also very useful for visitors and would help local businesses, and there are good reasons to support it too."

    Better:

    "Free evening parking does help visitors, but residents need somewhere to park every night, not just on event nights."

  • Point form

    Weak:

    Three bullet points instead of a paragraph.

    Better:

    Full sentences and paragraphs. Prometric's Task 2 guidance says to avoid list and point form outright.

  • A conclusion that repeats everything

    Weak:

    Three sentences summarising both reasons again.

    Better:

    One line. You have 180 words. Spend them on evidence, not recap.

The closing nod, in three moves

The nod is the part people cut when they run short, and it is the wrong thing to cut. Prometric lists comparing your choice with the other option as part of giving convincing support, which means acknowledging the alternative earns credit under Content/Coherence and Task Fulfillment both.

Concede → Limit → Redirect

  1. 01

    Concede

    Name the other side's real point. "Admittedly, some residents worry about security."

  2. 02

    Limit

    Show it is smaller or solvable. "But that is fixable. Check driver ID, or set delivery hours."

  3. 03

    Redirect

    Return to your choice. "…without ending a service people depend on."

Two or three sentences, never more. This is a closing note, not a second essay.

Universal sentence starters (the skeleton)

Mix and match to build your paragraphs. These are scaffolding for practice, not lines to memorize word for word.

Body 1(primary argument)

Topic sentence

  • First, the strongest reason to support [Option A] is [reason].
  • The main argument for [Option A] is [concept].
  • To begin with, [Option A] has a clear advantage in [reason].

Supporting templates

  • Right now, [describe the problem with the status quo].
  • If [Option A] goes ahead, it would let [people] [benefit], and cut [the problem].
  • That change would mean [outcome] for everyone who [uses it].

Body 2(secondary argument)

Topic sentence

  • Second, the [financial / social] side of this matters too.
  • This would also [help / protect / prevent] [concept].
  • Another point is [concept], because [Option A] would let us [goal].

Supporting templates

  • [Verb-ing] would [clear outcome].
  • By [verb-ing], the [company / city] makes sure [benefit].
  • It gives [people] a chance to [action].

Body 3(the nod)

Topic sentence

  • Admittedly, [Option B] does help with [point].
  • Some people would say [Option B] is [adjective].
  • It is true that [Option B] would offer [benefit].

Supporting templates

  • But [Option A] is the better choice because [reason].
  • Even so, [Option A] fits our needs better, because it [reason].
  • [Option B] deals with [issue], but it misses [flaw], and [Option A] does not.

Conclusion(summary)

Closing statement

  • For these reasons, [Option A] would [benefit] the most.
  • In short, [Option A] fixes the main problem, so it is the better choice.
  • Taking all of that together, [Option A] is the more practical option.

Compared with the previous version of this table, the padded openers are gone. "After careful consideration, I strongly support," "the most compelling reason," "cannot be overstated" and "I strongly urge" all fail the padding diagnostic above, so they are no longer here.

FAQ

Writing Task 2 Template FAQ

Quick answers to the questions test takers ask us most.

Still have a question?Ask the AI CELPIP Coach, any time.
01

How long is CELPIP Writing Task 2?

26 minutes. Task 1 gets 27 minutes, and the full Writing component is 53 minutes. Prometric publishes these figures in its CELPIP-General Overview Study Pack.

02

How many words should you write for CELPIP Writing Task 2?

150 to 200 words. Aim for about 180. A strong answer sits comfortably inside the range rather than at the top of it, because the last 20 words are where repetition and mistakes creep in.

03

Is there a penalty for going over 200 words?

Prometric says you have a small amount of room either side of the range, and that your score can drop if you go well over or well under it. No exact tolerance is published. Any specific buffer you see quoted online was invented by the site quoting it.

04

How is CELPIP Writing Task 2 scored?

On four dimensions: Content/Coherence, Vocabulary, Readability, and Task Fulfillment. There is no points formula, and Prometric publishes no weighting per dimension. Grammar is not a separate score, it sits inside Readability. Word count is one of the four factors inside Task Fulfillment.

05

Do you need a greeting and a sign-off in Task 2?

No. Prometric says you do not need to use email format in Task 2. My recommendation is to skip both and open with your opinion, since they cost you around ten words and earn nothing on any dimension.

06

Should Task 2 be written as a formal essay?

No. Prometric's own Task 2 advice is that this is not an academic essay. Write as a person answering a survey about their own neighbourhood or workplace. Precise plain language scores better than heavy formal vocabulary.

07

What are the two Task 2 prompt shapes?

Two-option prompts ask you to choose between A and B. Agree/disagree prompts give you one proposal and ask whether you support it. The four-part template works for both. Only your opening line and the direction of your closing nod change.

08

Do you have to acknowledge the other option?

Yes, and it is worth marks. Prometric's Task 2 guidance lists comparing your choice with the other option as part of giving convincing support, so the closing nod earns credit under Content/Coherence and Task Fulfillment. Keep it to two or three sentences.

09

Is there a spell check in the CELPIP Writing test?

Yes. The screen provides basic editing tools, an automatic word counter, and a spell check. Prometric notes that picking the right word can be hard when the spell check gives you several suggestions. It flags spelling only, so it will not catch grammar errors or the wrong word correctly spelled.
