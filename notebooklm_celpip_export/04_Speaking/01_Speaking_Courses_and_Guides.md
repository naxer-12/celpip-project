## Common Errors & How to Fix Them

Here is something worth knowing before you read a list of mistakes.

Most of the mistakes on this page are not made by weak speakers. They are made by good ones: people who talk for the full time, in clear English, on topic, and still sit at an 8 instead of a 9, or a 9 instead of a 12.

That is the list you want. The mistakes that cost points when your English is already fine.

There are eighteen. They fall into four groups: before you start, the middle, the ending, and the words.

## Scoring & Performance Standards

Almost every guide tells you CELPIP Speaking is scored on four things. That is true, and it is not enough to act on.

Inside those four dimensions, CELPIP publishes sixteen named factors , four in each. Knowing the four tells you what the areas are called. Knowing the sixteen tells you what to change.

---

## Task 1: Giving Advice & Templates

In Task 1 you speak to one person who needs help deciding something or preparing for something. You get 30 seconds to prepare and 90 seconds to speak .

Almost every free template teaches two suggestions. Two suggestions do not fill ninety seconds.

What happens next is predictable. You finish at about fifty-seven seconds, you are left with more than half a minute of silence, and length is one of the things this test scores . So you either stop early and lose marks for it, or you say the same thing twice in different words and lose marks for that instead.

The problem is not your English. It is the template.

---

## Task 2: Talking about a Personal Experience & Templates

In Task 2 you get 30 seconds to prepare and 60 seconds to speak . CELPIP describes the task in one line: tell a story about a past experience.

Two things in the official material surprise almost everyone, and both are printed in plain sight.

---

## Task 3: Describing a Scene & Templates

Here is the whole official description of Task 3:

> Look at an illustration and describe what is happening.

Nine words, and the important one is happening . Present tense. What is in front of you, right now.

Compare it with the task that follows, which uses the very same picture:

> Look at the same illustration from Task 3 and describe what you think will happen next.

Task 3 is the picture's present. Task 4 is its future. Nothing in Task 3 asks you to say a single word about something you cannot see, and a guess spends your seconds on the next task instead of this one.

You get 30 seconds to prepare and 60 seconds to speak , and the prompt tells you something worth taking literally: the person you are speaking to cannot see the picture.

---

## Task 4: Making Predictions & Templates

Here is the complete official description of Task 4, all fourteen words of it:

> Look at the same illustration from Task 3 and describe what you think will happen next.

Read the first six words again. The same illustration from Task 3.

Almost every lesson on this task teaches it on its own, as though a new picture appears. It does not. You have just spent sixty seconds looking at that picture and describing it out loud, and the notes you made for Task 3 are already your notes for Task 4.

You get 30 seconds to prepare and 60 seconds to speak , the same as Task 3.

---

## Task 5: Comparing & Persuading & Templates

Task 5 takes three minutes of your test and you speak for one of them.

That sentence is the whole task, and almost every method you will find online plans the minute you speak and ignores the two you do not.

Here is the task in plain words:

> In Part 1, you choose one of two options, and you do not speak. In Part 2, you persuade someone that your choice is better than the option they are suggesting.

Read the last part again. The option they are suggesting. You do not know what that is yet. In Part 1 you see two options and you pick one. Only then does the other person's suggestion appear, and it is not one of the two you were shown.

That is the part that surprises people on test day, and it is why a plan built entirely around Part 1 falls over.

## Task 6: Dealing with a Difficult Situation & Templates

In Task 6 you explain a decision to someone it affects: a friend, a family member, someone you know, or a customer.

Three things in that sentence decide how you answer it.

A decision. The prompt gives you a situation with two ways out. You choose one. Then you explain that choice. You are not asking anybody for anything, and you are not solving a problem with them. You have already decided.

To a person. Every other speaking task has you talking to a general listener. This one names somebody, and they are standing in front of you, and what you are about to say affects them.

Usually unwelcome. The decision is difficult because the person will not like it. That is the whole test.

You get 60 seconds to prepare and 60 seconds to speak . The preparation minute is double what most tasks give you, so use all of it.

## Task 7: Expressing Opinions & Templates

Task 7 asks what you think, and then gives you a minute and a half to hold that position.

You get 30 seconds to prepare and 90 seconds to speak . It is one of only two tasks with ninety seconds, and it is the one where people most often talk themselves out of their own answer without noticing.

CELPIP's tips for this task come down to two things that do most of the work: pick your side fast, and give about three reasons for it .

Look at the number. About three. Not two , and not at least three either. Task 1 asks for at least three pieces of advice. Task 7 asks for about three reasons, and those are different instructions for different tasks.

Only two of the eight speaking tasks publish a number at all , and both of them say three. When the test bothers to give you a number, it is worth using.

## Task 8: Describing an Unusual Situation & Templates

In Task 8 you see a picture of something strange or unexpected. You have 30 seconds to prepare and 60 seconds to speak , and your job is to describe that picture to a person on the phone who cannot see it.

Most people make the same two mistakes. They start with the small details, and they try to describe everything. Sixty seconds is not enough for either.

## Vocabulary for Speaking (Range + Relevance)

Picture two answers to the same Task 7 question. One reaches for advanced words: detrimental , paradigm , facilitate . The other uses common words exactly right: commute , rent , part-time .

The answer with the plainer words often scores higher. A final level comes from all four scoring areas, not vocabulary alone. But advanced words do not lift an answer when the phrases around them come out imprecise or unnatural, like make a big influence or in the nowadays .

Words that do not fit the sentence around them cost you. Words that are simple and exact do not.

That is this whole page in one example. Good speaking vocabulary is not rare. It is exact , and it sounds like something a person would actually say.

### How are these sample answers labelled?

Each sample is our example of a Level 9 answer, written against CELPIP's published Speaking level descriptors. None of them has been scored by CELPIP. Only trained raters can give a real CELPIP level, so no page on the internet can promise that an answer "scores 9". What we can do is build each answer to what the descriptors say a Level 9 speaker does, and show you where that happens.

CELPIP rates Speaking on four criteria: Content/Coherence, Vocabulary, Listenability and Task Fulfillment. In our own summary, CELPIP's Level 9 descriptors describe a speaker who backs up ideas with reasons that go beyond the obvious, uses everyday and topic words plus the odd idiom, handles some complex grammar, speaks mostly smoothly, and changes tone to suit the listener and the situation. Under every sample you will find three short notes on how it meets those lines.

One more honest point. Levels in Speaking are about how you say it as much as what you say, and a page cannot show pronunciation or rhythm. ### What does a Task 1 Giving Advice answer sound like? (90 seconds)

Prompt (free question 1 of 84): Your co-worker has been offered a promotion at work, but it requires him to relocate to another city. He is unsure whether to accept the offer or stay in his current position. Advise him on what he should consider when making this decision.

Sample answer, about 205 words:

> To be fair, this is a great problem to have, but I would not rush it. There are three things I would look at before you decide. First, think about the money, and not just the salary. A bigger title in a more expensive city can actually leave you with less at the end of the month, so compare rent, transport and childcare before you compare pay cheques. Second, think about the people. If you have family or close friends here, moving will cost you that support, at least for the first year. On the other hand, if you have been feeling a bit stuck, a fresh start can be exactly what you need. Third, and this is the one most people skip, ask what happens if it does not work out. Can you come back to your current role? Is there a trial period? A good company will give you a straight answer, and that answer tells you a lot about how much they value you. So my advice is to write those three things down, talk it through with your family, and give yourself a deadline so you do not go round in circles. Whatever you choose, make it a decision, not a drift.

What the rater hears:

- Content/Coherence: three signposted points, each with a reason, and a close that returns to the decision. That is what backing up ideas with real reasons looks like in practice.
- Vocabulary: context-specific words (trial period, support, pay cheque) plus natural idioms ("a great problem to have", "go round in circles", "a decision, not a drift").
- Task Fulfillment: the prompt asks what he should consider, so every point is a consideration, not an instruction to accept or refuse.

### What does a Task 2 Personal Experience answer sound like? (60 seconds)

Prompt (free question 1 of 91): Talk about an experience of getting lost in an unfamiliar place. Maybe you can talk about losing your way in a new city, getting separated from a group during a trip, or wandering into an unknown neighborhood. What happened, how did you feel during the experience, and why is it memorable to you?

Sample answer, about 150 words:

> The time I remember best was my second week in Montreal. I took the metro to meet a friend, got off one stop too early, and my phone died before I could open the map. At first I was fine, because I thought I could just follow the main road. Twenty minutes later I was in a residential area with no shops, no signs I recognised, and it was starting to get dark. I felt really nervous, and a bit embarrassed, because I had told everyone I knew the city already. In the end I asked an older man walking his dog, and he not only gave me directions, he walked me to the corner so I would not miss the turn. It is memorable because it was the first time I asked a stranger for help in English, and it went fine. After that, the city felt a lot smaller.

- Content/Coherence: a clear sequence (what happened, how it felt, why it matters) that answers all three parts of the prompt in order.
- Vocabulary: precise verbs and phrases (got off one stop too early, residential area, walked me to the corner) rather than general words like "went" and "place".
- Listenability: past tenses are controlled and consistent, with one longer sentence balanced by short ones, which keeps the rhythm natural.

### What does a Task 3 Describing a Scene answer sound like? (60 seconds)

Prompt (free question 1 of 74): Describe some things that are happening in the picture below as well as you can. The person with whom you are speaking cannot see the picture. The picture shows a busy outdoor community pool on a sunny afternoon.

Sample answer, about 160 words:

> This is an outdoor community pool on a sunny afternoon, and it is busy. In the foreground on the left, a lifeguard in a red swimsuit is sitting in her tall white chair, watching the water, with a rescue float beside her. In the middle of the pool, three children are jumping in and splashing each other, and you can see the water flying everywhere. Further back, a man is swimming laps, and at the far end someone is diving off the board. On the right-hand side, there is a patio with a blue umbrella where an older couple is sitting at a table. One of them is reading a newspaper. Next to them, a staff member in a white shirt is mopping the deck, and there is a yellow wet-floor sign beside him. At the bottom right, a father is helping a little girl in pink arm floats get ready to go in. It looks like a typical hot Saturday.

- Content/Coherence: the description moves in an order a listener can follow (left, middle, right, bottom), which matters because the other person cannot see the picture.
- Vocabulary: specific nouns (lifeguard, rescue float, patio, wet-floor sign, arm floats) instead of "thing" and "person".
- Task Fulfillment: it describes what is happening, in the present continuous, and does not drift into a story or an opinion.

### What does a Task 4 Making Predictions answer sound like? (60 seconds)

Prompt (free question 1 of 69): In this picture, what do you think will most probably happen next? The picture shows an apple orchard at harvest time: a woman on a ladder picking apples, a man loading crates onto a pickup truck, a tractor pulling a trailer, a cider stand, two children with rabbits, a dog, and dark clouds moving in.

Sample answer, about 155 words:

> I think the first thing that will happen is rain. The clouds on the left are dark and low, and the wind is already blowing papers off the cider stand, so I would say they have ten or fifteen minutes at most. Because of that, the man by the truck will probably start loading the crates faster, and he will likely call the woman on the ladder to come down, since a wet ladder is dangerous. She will probably pick two or three more apples first, because people always do that. The children with the rabbits will most likely be told to put them back in the hutch, and the dog will follow the kids wherever they go. I also expect the woman at the cider stand to pack up her jugs and move them under the barn roof. So in about half an hour, I predict everyone will be in the barn, drying off, drinking cider.

- Content/Coherence: each prediction is tied to evidence in the picture (dark clouds, papers blowing, a wet ladder), so the reasons are visible, not invented.
- Vocabulary: a range of prediction language (will probably, will likely, most likely, I expect, I predict) rather than "will" eight times.
- Listenability: future forms and modals are controlled, and the humour in "people always do that" sounds like a real speaker, not a script.

### What does a Task 5 Comparing and Persuading answer sound like? (60 seconds)

Prompt (free question 1 of 82): You work at a local grocery store and are responsible for selecting fruit baskets to feature in the front display. You found two suitable options. Choose the option that you prefer. Option 1: a discounted basket of apples, bananas and pears, some slightly overripe, deeply discounted, good for quick sale. Option 2: a standard mix of imported fruit at regular price, long shelf life, no particular marketing appeal. In Part 2, your manager prefers the other option. Persuade them that yours is better.

Sample answer (Part 2, you chose Option 1), about 155 words:

> I understand why you like the standard mix, because it will not go off, but I still think the discounted basket is the better choice for the front display, for two reasons. First, the front display is about getting people to stop. A deep discount on a basket of apples, bananas and pears is something customers notice from the door. The imported mix at regular price looks like every other shelf in the store. Second, the overripe fruit is actually the point. We already have those apples and pears, and if we do not sell them this week we throw them away. Putting them out front turns waste into sales instead of a loss. If you are worried about shelf life, we can keep the display small and refill it twice a day. That way we move the discounted fruit fast and the standard mix stays on the regular shelves for the people who want it.

- Content/Coherence: it names the other side's point first, then gives two reasons and a compromise, which is the shape of real persuasion.
- Vocabulary: business words that fit the setting (front display, shelf life, deep discount, move the fruit, turn waste into sales).
- Task Fulfillment: the whole answer argues for the choice; it does not describe both options again, which is the most common Task 5 mistake.

### What does a Task 6 Difficult Situation answer sound like? (60 seconds)

Prompt (free question 1 of 85): You work at a bakery and your manager has asked you to train a new employee, Tom. Tom is making many mistakes and learning slowly. Your manager wants you to tell Tom that he will be let go if he does not improve. Tom has told you he is going through personal difficulties and that this job matters a lot to him. Choose one: talk to your manager and explain why Tom should get more time, or talk to Tom and explain why he needs to improve quickly.

Sample answer (you chose to talk to the manager), about 155 words:

> Hi Maria, do you have two minutes? I wanted to talk to you about Tom before I say anything to him. I know he has made a lot of mistakes this week, and I am not going to pretend otherwise. But I have been working next to him every day, and I think the problem is pace, not attitude. He asks good questions, he writes everything down, and he stays late to clean up without being asked. He also told me he is dealing with something difficult at home right now. I will not go into details, but it explains a lot. So what I would like to suggest is two more weeks. I will give him a checklist for each shift and go through it with him at the end of the day. If he is still making the same mistakes after that, I will support whatever you decide. Would that work for you?

- Content/Coherence: it opens by naming the listener and the purpose, accepts the manager's concern, then gives evidence and a concrete proposal with a deadline.
- Vocabulary: workplace phrases used naturally (pace, not attitude; a checklist for each shift; support whatever you decide).
- Task Fulfillment and tone: it is spoken to the manager, as a conversation, in a respectful register, which is what "adjust my speaking style and tone" means in this task.

### What does a Task 7 Expressing Opinions answer sound like? (90 seconds)

Prompt (free question 1 of 90): Do you think landlords should be required by law to allow tenants to keep pets in rental apartments and houses? Explain your reasons.

> Yes, I think landlords should be required to allow pets, with some reasonable conditions, and I have two main reasons. The first is that for a lot of people a pet is family, not a hobby. When almost every listing says "no pets", families end up giving animals away or lying to the landlord, and neither of those is good for anyone. A law would take that pressure off tenants who are already struggling to find a place they can afford. The second reason is that the usual argument against pets, damage, can be handled without a blanket ban. Landlords could be allowed to ask for a reasonable pet deposit and to require that the tenant covers any repairs. In my experience, a well cared for dog does far less damage than a careless tenant with no pets at all. I do understand the other side. Some buildings have allergies to think about, and some animals are simply too big for a small apartment. That is why I would keep exceptions for size and for medical reasons. But as a general rule, I think the right to keep a pet should belong to the person who lives there, not the person who owns the building.

- Content/Coherence: a clear position in the first sentence, two developed reasons, a counter-argument acknowledged and answered, and a close that restates the view in new words.
- Vocabulary: topic-specific terms (listing, blanket ban, pet deposit, exceptions) and one idiom-level phrase ("take that pressure off").
- Listenability: longer sentences with conditionals and comparatives, balanced by short ones ("I do understand the other side"), which gives the rhythm a Level 9 answer needs.

### What does a Task 8 Unusual Situation answer sound like? (60 seconds)

Prompt (free question 1 of 102): You are walking through a neighborhood and notice a house with a very unusual front yard fence. Call your friend Dev, who is renovating his yard and looking for creative ideas. Describe in detail what the fence looks like, and ask him if he would like to come see it in person. The picture shows a fence built to look like a coral reef.

> Hey Dev, it's me. You have to hear about this fence I just walked past, because it is exactly your kind of thing. Imagine a normal house on a normal street, but the whole front fence is built to look like a coral reef. The posts are pieces of driftwood with seaweed wrapped around them, and in between there are chunks of orange and pink coral, with little anemones and shells stuck all over it. The gate is a giant pink and white seashell, taller than me, that opens in the middle. There are even sea creatures in it. I can see a blue starfish on the left, a green turtle near the top, and a couple of clownfish painted near the gate. From the sidewalk it really looks like the house is sitting at the bottom of the ocean. It is on Maple Street, near the park. Do you want to come and see it this weekend?

- Content/Coherence: it starts with the big picture (a fence like a coral reef) and then zooms in (posts, panels, gate, creatures), which is how a listener can build the image.
- Vocabulary: it names materials and colours precisely (driftwood, anemones, clownfish, pink and white seashell) instead of saying "strange" and "colourful".
- Task Fulfillment: both halves are done, the description and the invitation, and it sounds like a phone call to a friend, not a report.

### What is the difference between a Level 7 and a Level 10 Speaking answer?

Mostly the reasons, the range, and the control. The same Task 1 prompt answered twice shows it better than any explanation. Both answers below are complete, polite and on topic. One is our example of a Level 7, the other our example of a Level 10, each written to the published descriptors.

Our Level 7 example, about 130 words:

> I think you should think carefully before you decide. First, you should think about the salary. If the salary is higher, that is good, but the city may be more expensive. So you need to check the rent and the cost of living. Second, you should think about your family. If your family is here, it will be hard to move. You will miss them and they will miss you. But if you are single, it is easier. Third, you should think about your career. A promotion is a good opportunity, and maybe it will not come again. So if you say no, you may regret it later. In my opinion, you should make a list of good things and bad things, and then talk to your family. Then you can decide.

Our Level 10 example, about 205 words:

> This is a big decision, so rather than telling you what to do, let me give you three questions that should settle it. The first is whether the promotion is really a step up once you factor in the move. A higher salary in a city where rent is double can leave you worse off, so before you get excited about the title, sit down with the actual numbers for housing, transport and anything you would have to pay for that you currently get for free. The second is what you would be giving up here. If your partner's job, your kids' school or your parents are all in this city, relocation is not just your decision, and the people affected deserve a say before you answer your boss. The third question, and in my view the most important, is what your options look like if the new role does not work out. Ask whether there is a trial period or a path back. A company that handles that question well is a company worth moving for. If the answers to all three are reassuring, take it. If even one of them makes you uneasy, that unease is information, and you should listen to it.

What changed between the two:

- Reasons: the Level 7 answer states each point and gives a short, concrete reason. The Level 10 answer develops each point with a condition and a consequence ("a higher salary in a city where rent is double can leave you worse off"), which is "develop ideas with clear descriptions or reasons".
- Vocabulary: Level 7 uses common words and some precise ones (salary, cost of living, opportunity). Level 10 adds abstract and idiomatic language (factor in, a step up, deserve a say, that unease is information).
- Grammar and rhythm: Level 7 shows "good control of simple grammatical structures", with short sentences that repeat "you should think about". Level 10 shows "good control of a broad range of complex grammatical structures": conditionals, relative clauses and sentences that vary in length without losing the listener.
- Tone: both are polite. Level 10 also meets "adjust my speaking style and tone to a broad range of audiences and situations", speaking to the co-worker as an equal rather than instructing him.
A real Level 7 in Speaking equals CLB (Canadian Language Benchmark) 7, which many Express Entry profiles need. A Level 10 is the kind of performance you are aiming for if you want the full language points. Remember that only CELPIP's raters decide which level an answer actually earns. ### How is the CELPIP Speaking test scored?

CELPIP Speaking is scored by trained raters on four criteria, not out of a fixed number of points. You complete 8 tasks: giving advice, talking about a personal experience, describing a scene, making predictions, comparing and persuading, dealing with a difficult situation, expressing opinions, and describing an unusual situation. A trained rater judges each response, and your overall level reflects your consistent performance across all of them.

Raters weigh four criteria:

- Content/Coherence : how many relevant ideas you give, how well you develop them, and whether your response is organised with supporting detail. Thin, one-line answers cap your level here.
- Vocabulary : range and precision. Using the right word beats using a big word. Vague, repeated wording holds you back.
- Listenability : how easy you are to follow: pronunciation, intonation, rhythm, and grammar working together. You do not need a "perfect" accent; you need to be effortless to understand.
- Task Fulfillment : did you actually do what the task asked, cover all of it, use the right tone, and speak for the full time? Drifting off-topic or finishing early costs you here.
This is why memorised templates only take you so far. A template gives you structure, but the rater is listening for real, on-topic content delivered clearly under time pressure. If you want the structure without sounding robotic, our CELPIP Speaking templates for all 8 tasks show how to keep a frame while filling it with genuine ideas.

### What do the CELPIP Speaking level descriptors say?

CELPIP publishes level descriptors for Speaking: short "At this level, I can" statements for each level, in the four categories your eight tasks are rated on (Content/Coherence, Vocabulary, Listenability and Task Fulfillment). They are the closest thing to a public rubric the Speaking test has, and the official wording is in the CELPIP Level Descriptors document on celpip.ca . Below is my own summary of what each category asks for as the levels rise. It is written in my words, not quoted from the document.

Read each category on its own. Your level is a judgement across all four, and two people can reach the same level in different ways. After each summary there is a short note on what a 9 does that a 7 does not, because that is the gap most of my students are trying to close.

For what each level means in plain words, and what it is enough for in Canada, see what each CELPIP level means

#### Content/Coherence


| Levels | In plain words |
| --- | --- |
| 10 to 12 | You handle demanding, unfamiliar situations and develop your ideas fully, with clear and precise detail. |
| 7 to 9 | You give concrete information and some abstract ideas, each backed by a reason with some depth to it. |
| 5 to 6 | You give opinions and feelings with simple reasons, describe familiar people and situations, and tell a simple story. |
| 3 to 4 | You say what you like, dislike or need, and tell very short, simple stories. |

What a 9 does that a 7 does not. In this category the descriptors for 7, 8 and 9 are very close: all three want ideas supported with reasons that have some depth, and 7 and 8 read the same. So the jump from 7 to 9 is small here, and the real step comes at 10, where you develop ideas in unfamiliar, non-routine situations. What this category does demand at 7 and up is a reason that has some shape to it: not just "because it is cheaper", but "because it is cheaper, and with the money we save we could finally fix the car". One claim, one reason, one consequence. If your answers are a list of opinions with no reasons, this is the category holding you down.

#### Vocabulary


| Levels | In plain words |
| --- | --- |
| 10 to 12 | A wide range of concrete and abstract language, with idioms and figurative expressions used naturally. |
| 7 to 9 | Everyday words plus more precise words that suit the situation, and the occasional idiom. |
| 5 to 6 | Common words and phrases. |
| 3 to 4 | Very common, basic words. |

What a 9 does that a 7 does not. First, notice what does not change: idioms are mentioned in much the same way from 7 right through to 10. Idioms are not what moves you from 7 to 9, so stop memorising lists of them. What changes is vocabulary that fits the context. A 7 reaches for a more precise word now and then. A 9 uses the words that belong to the situation in the prompt. If the task is about choosing between two apartments, that means words like lease, deposit, commute and utilities, used naturally, not "the place" and "the money" over and over.

#### Listenability


| Levels | In plain words |
| --- | --- |
| 10 to 12 | Good to very good control of a broad range of complex grammar, with speech that flows naturally almost all the time. |
| 8 to 9 | Some control of complex sentences, mostly fluent rhythm and pronunciation, and very few restarts or awkward pauses. |
| 6 to 7 | Simple grammar under control and delivery that is easy to understand, with some restarts or pauses at 6 and very few at 7. |
| 3 to 5 | Simple grammar with limited or patchy control. |

What a 9 does that a 7 does not. Two shifts. The grammar moves from good control of simple sentences at 7 to some control of complex ones at 9. And the delivery moves from easy to understand to mostly fluent. In practice a 7 speaks in short, safe sentences that are easy to follow. A 9 adds sentences with "although", "if", "which" and "so that" in them, and keeps them going without stopping halfway to restart. You do not need a perfect accent for either level. You need the complex sentences you can say in one go, so practise a few of those until they come out smoothly, rather than attempting long sentences you lose control of.

#### Task Fulfillment


| Levels | In plain words |
| --- | --- |
| 10 to 12 | You adapt your style and tone to a broad range of listeners and situations, and your meaning comes across precisely. |
| 9 | You adjust your style and tone to a range of listeners and situations, formal or informal. |
| 7 to 8 | You adjust your tone for some listeners and situations, mostly familiar ones, and your intended meaning comes across. |
| 3 to 6 | You get information across on familiar topics, adjusting your style only in familiar situations. |

What a 9 does that a 7 does not. At 7 the descriptors talk about adjusting your tone for familiar listeners, the friend or family member. At 9 the range widens to many kinds of listener and situation. The Speaking prompts tell you who you are talking to, and the tone has to fit that person: a friend asking for advice and a manager hearing a complaint get different answers, even when the ideas are the same. Before you start speaking, ask yourself who is listening, and let that decide how direct, how polite and how casual you sound.

The descriptors also say which situations each level applies to. From level 9 up, that includes speaking with authority figures as well as peers, in formal or informal settings. At 7 and 8 authority figures are not mentioned, and below 7 the settings become more casual. Several Speaking prompts put you in front of a boss, a landlord or an official, which is exactly the kind of listener the descriptors first name at level 9.

From level 3 up, every CELPIP Speaking level equals the same-numbered CLB level. A 2 corresponds to CLB 1 to 2, and 0 or 1 means there was not enough language to assess. ### How does CELPIP Speaking compare to IELTS Speaking?

This is where most charts online get it wrong, and the error can cost you. IRCC's official equivalency table converts each IELTS skill to a CLB level separately . | CELPIP Speaking / CLB | IELTS Speaking band |
| --- | --- |
| 10, 11, 12 | 7.5–9.0 |
| 9 | 7.0 |
| 8 | 6.5 |
| 7 | 6.0 |
| 6 | 5.5 |
| 5 | 5.0 |
| 4 | 4.0 |

Note the top of the scale: IRCC groups CLB 10 and above into a single IELTS Speaking range of 7.5–9.0. It does not assign separate bands to CLB 11 or 12. So a CELPIP 10, 11, or 12 all sit in that 7.5–9.0 window for immigration purposes.

If you have seen a chart claiming CELPIP 9 equals IELTS Speaking 7.5, it is off by half a band. The correct figure is 7.0.

### What CELPIP Speaking score do I need for Express Entry?

It depends on the program, and the Speaking minimum moves with it. Per IRCC:

- Federal Skilled Worker & Canadian Experience Class (NOC TEER 0 or 1): CLB 7 in all four abilities, so CELPIP Speaking 7 minimum.
- Canadian Experience Class (NOC TEER 2 or 3): CLB 5 with Speaking 5 minimum.
- Federal Skilled Trades: CLB 5 for Speaking and Listening with Speaking 5 minimum.
Those are floors, not targets. Because CLB feeds your CRS score, most competitive Express Entry candidates aim for CLB 9 (Speaking 9) across the board, since that unlocks the higher language points. ### How can I raise my CELPIP Speaking score?

Work the four criteria directly instead of practising vaguely:

- For Content/Coherence, push past your first idea. Give two or three developed points with a specific example each, and signpost them so the rater hears the structure.
- For Vocabulary, build topic-specific word banks (workplace, community, personal life) and practise using them precisely, not stuffing in "impressive" words that do not fit.
- For Listenability, record yourself and listen back. Flat delivery and long pauses hurt more than a mild accent. Aim for a natural, steady rhythm.
- For Task Fulfillment, read the prompt twice, answer every part, match the tone it asks for, and keep talking until time is called. Finishing 15 seconds early is a wasted opportunity to add detail.
The fastest feedback loop is doing real, timed tasks and seeing where you actually land. The first dimension is Content and Coherence: our ideas and how they connect. Number of ideas, quality of ideas, organization of ideas, and examples and supporting details.

The second is Vocabulary, or word choice: suitable use of words, range of words, and precision and accuracy.

The third is Listenability: how easy we are to listen to. Rhythm, pronunciation and intonation; pauses and self corrections; grammar and sentence structure; and variety of sentence structure.

The fourth is Task Fulfillment: did you really answer the question they asked? Relevance, completeness, tone and length.

#### No weighting between the four is published

One thing is very important: CELPIP never says which of these 16 factors weighs more. So if you heard from someone that one item is more important than another, it's better not to trust that. Tell me in the comments which one is the hardest for you.

#### Why answer 1 scores lower

The first answer you heard will get a lower level than the second one. Why? The first one was complete, it had an appropriate tone, and it spoke for the full time. Tone and speaking for the full time are things many of my students spend a lot of time learning, and they learn only those two. But these are only two of the 16 items. If you only practise tone and speaking for the full 90 seconds, you are fulfilling two items, and in my experience your level does not go above 8.

#### Does getting cut off lower your score?

If you look at CELPIP's official documents, you will find many sample answers published by CELPIP that are cut off in the middle of a sentence, and CELPIP says they can get 12. So being cut off in the middle of a sentence is not a big deal. Cover all 16 items instead.

#### Difference 1: precision and accuracy

Answers that cannot get a top score often use words like "really good", "pretty expensive", "good thing", "a little bit", "some stuff". The answers that score at the top are more precise: "once you land there", "forward your resume", "afford accommodation". Precise means the phrase can only describe one thing. "Forward your resume" means one thing; it is not a general phrase. It focuses on a detail that can be counted. Compare our two answers: the first said "the money", "a problem", "many things". The second was precise: $600, 10%, HR, Sarah, her clients.

#### Difference 2: examples and supporting details

The first answer named only three ideas, three things to consider before moving to a new city. The second one mentioned them too, but went into more detail and gave supporting details and examples, so it scores higher.

#### Difference 3: variety of sentence structure

When we look at CELPIP's official sample answers, the ones that score around 8 mostly use simple sentence structures, and the ones that get top scores use a variety of complex structures. How can you do this? Practise enough. The words that build complex structures are listed in the same document; some of them are "although", "which", "if" and "whereas". The first answer connected sentences with simple words like "and", "so" and "but". The second used "because", "if", "which", "whether", "once" and "whichever". Both were 90 seconds, but the second had more complex structure.

So these are the three differences that can change your level a lot: precision, development and complex structure.

### 3 Speaking Strategies That Apply to Every Task

These three rules work across all eight tasks. Master them and you've already done the hardest part.

#### Strategy 1: Use Every Second of Prep Time

The 30 seconds (or 60 seconds for Tasks 5 and 6) before you speak are the most valuable seconds in the entire test. Here's how to use them:

- Read the prompt carefully — identify exactly what the task is asking
- Jot 2-3 bullet points on your noteboard — just keywords, not sentences
- Plan your opening sentence — knowing how to start eliminates the awkward first few seconds
If your noteboard says "1. time saved 2. less stress 3. health," you always know what to say next. You're reading a roadmap instead of driving blind.

> What not to do: Don't try to script your entire response during prep time. You won't finish, and you'll end up reading awkwardly instead of speaking naturally.

#### Strategy 2: Kill the Filler Words

"Um," "uh," "like," "so basically" — everyone uses these in casual conversation, but in CELPIP Speaking, they hurt your Listenability score. Excessive fillers signal hesitation to the raters.

How to reduce fillers:

- Pause instead of filling. A 1-2 second silence sounds far more natural than "um, uh, so." Raters don't penalize brief pauses — they penalize constant fillers
- Use transition phrases that buy you thinking time while sounding polished: "Another thing I'd mention is..." "On top of that..." "What's also worth considering is..." "The way I see it..."
- "Another thing I'd mention is..."
- "On top of that..."
- "What's also worth considering is..."
- "The way I see it..."
- Build your vocabulary. The more words you have ready, the less you hesitate. Our CELPIP vocabulary guide has 100+ phrases organized by task type
- Practice out loud. Record yourself answering practice questions and count your fillers. Most people don't realize how often they say "um" until they hear it played back
#### Strategy 3: Fill Your Response Time

Some test takers make one or two points and then go silent with 20-30 seconds left on the clock. That's wasted opportunity. Raters notice incomplete use of time, and it limits how much vocabulary and fluency you can demonstrate.

If you finish your main points early, expand with:

- A personal example: "For instance, when I was in a similar situation..."
- A contrast: "On the other hand, some people might prefer..."
- A consequence: "If they follow this advice, I think they'd find that..."
- A restatement: "So overall, I'd say the best approach is..."
Target: Speak until the timer is within 5-10 seconds of ending. You don't need to fill every millisecond, but 20+ seconds of silence at the end costs you points.

### Task-by-Task CELPIP Speaking Strategies

Each of the 8 tasks asks for something different. Tailoring your approach to the task type shows the evaluator you understood the prompt, and that's worth points on its own.

#### Task 1: Giving Advice (30 sec prep, 90 sec speaking)

What it asks: A friend or acquaintance has a problem and wants your advice. Give them practical suggestions.

Your approach:

- Give 2-3 specific pieces of advice , not just one vague suggestion
- Use phrases like "What I'd recommend is..." and "Another option would be..."
- Make the advice practical and actionable — not "be positive" but "make a list of pros and cons before deciding"
- 90 seconds is the longest response time — pace yourself and develop each point
Opening template: "That's a tough situation, but here's what I'd suggest..."

Practice with Speaking Task 1 templates and review task-specific vocabulary .

#### Task 2: Talking About a Personal Experience (30 sec prep, 60 sec speaking)

What it asks: Describe a personal experience related to a given topic.

- Tell a story with a beginning, middle, and end — don't just list facts
- Include sensory details: what you saw, heard, felt
- Connect the experience to how it affected you or what you learned
- It doesn't have to be a real experience — make one up if needed, as long as it sounds authentic
Opening template: "I remember a time when..." or "A few years ago, I had an experience that..."

Practice with Speaking Task 2 templates .

#### Task 3: Describing a Scene (30 sec prep, 60 sec speaking)

What it asks: Look at an image and describe what you see — who's there, what they're doing, where it's happening.

- Follow a spatial pattern : start with the overall setting, then move to specific details (left to right, foreground to background)
- Describe people (what they look like, what they're doing, how they seem to feel) and objects (what's in the scene, what stands out)
- Use the present continuous tense: "A woman is sitting at a desk," "Two children are playing in the park"
- Don't just list what you see — interpret : "She appears to be frustrated because..."
Opening template: "In this image, I can see..." or "This picture shows..."

Practice with Speaking Task 3 templates and study scene description vocabulary .

#### Task 4: Making Predictions (30 sec prep, 60 sec speaking)

What it asks: Based on a scene or situation, predict what will happen next and explain why.

- Make 2-3 predictions , not just one
- Support each prediction with evidence from the scene : "Because the man looks upset, I think he'll probably..."
- Use future tense and prediction language: "will likely," "might," "I expect that," "it seems probable that"
- Connect your predictions logically — don't just guess randomly
Opening template: "Based on what I see, I think several things will happen..."

Practice with Speaking Task 4 templates .

#### Task 5: Comparing and Persuading (60 sec prep, 60 sec speaking)

What it asks: Compare two options (places, plans, ideas) and persuade someone to choose one.

- You get 60 seconds of prep — use the extra time to note advantages of your preferred option AND one weakness of the alternative
- State your preference clearly at the beginning: "I'd strongly recommend Option A, and here's why..."
- Give 2 reasons with brief examples for your choice
- Briefly mention why the other option isn't as good: "While Option B does have [advantage], it doesn't compare to..."
- End with a persuasive closing: "That's why I really think you should go with..."
Opening template: "Between these two options, I'd definitely go with [choice] because..."

Practice with Speaking Task 5 templates .

#### Task 6: Dealing With a Difficult Situation (60 sec prep, 60 sec speaking)

What it asks: Handle a problem (a complaint, a disagreement, a request) with diplomacy and tact.

- Acknowledge the other person's feelings first: "I completely understand your frustration..."
- Explain the situation clearly without being defensive
- Offer a solution or compromise — show problem-solving skills
- Keep a calm, empathetic tone throughout — this isn't a debate, it's conflict resolution
- 60 seconds of prep is your advantage — plan what to acknowledge, explain, and propose
Opening template: "I really appreciate you bringing this up, and I want to make sure we resolve it..."

Practice with Speaking Task 6 templates and review difficult situation vocabulary .

#### Task 7: Expressing Opinions (30 sec prep, 90 sec speaking)

What it asks: State your opinion on a topic and support it with reasons.

- State your opinion in the first sentence — don't build up to it
- Give 2-3 reasons with examples or explanations
- Briefly acknowledge the other side: "Some people might argue that... but I think..."
- 90 seconds is a lot of time — develop your reasons fully instead of making five shallow points
- End by restating your position: "So that's why I firmly believe..."
Opening template: "In my opinion, [clear position statement], and I have a few reasons for this."

Practice with Speaking Task 7 templates .

#### Task 8: Describing an Unusual Situation (30 sec prep, 60 sec speaking)

What it asks: Describe something unusual in an image and offer explanations for what might be happening.

- Identify what's unusual about the scene first
- Offer 2-3 possible explanations — creativity is welcome here
- Use speculative language: "It's possible that," "One explanation could be," "Perhaps..."
- Be descriptive — paint a picture with your words so the rater can visualize what you're describing
Opening template: "This is definitely an unusual situation. What I notice is..."

### Speaking Pace: How Fast Should You Talk?

Speaking too fast makes you hard to understand. Speaking too slowly means you won't finish your points. Here's the balance:


| Response Time | How Many Points to Make | Pace Guide |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 60 seconds | 2-3 points | About 1 point every 20-25 seconds |
| 90 seconds | 3-4 points | About 1 point every 25-30 seconds |

Signs you're speaking too fast:

- You're making 5+ points in 60 seconds
- You're stumbling over words or sounds
- You finish with 15+ seconds left
Signs you're speaking too slow:

- You only get through 1 point in 60 seconds
- There are long pauses between every sentence
- You don't finish your thought before time runs out
The sweet spot: Talk like you're explaining something to a friend over coffee. Not a presentation, not a whisper. Conversational but clear.

### Microphone Tips: Sound Your Best

Since you're recording into a computer, how you handle the mic matters:

- Don't speak directly into the mic. Speak slightly to the side or above it. This reduces popping sounds on "P" and "B" words
- Keep a consistent distance. Don't lean in and out. About 6-8 inches from the mic is ideal
- Don't tap the desk or shuffle papers near the mic. Background noise can obscure your words
- Test during the practice task. The unscored practice at the beginning is your chance to hear yourself through the headset and adjust

### Frequently Asked Questions

#### Is the CELPIP Speaking test done with a real person?

No, you speak into a computer microphone. There is no live interviewer — your responses are recorded and later scored by trained raters. This is actually an advantage: there's no social pressure, no back-and-forth conversation, and you get dedicated prep time before every response.

#### How many tasks are in the CELPIP Speaking section?

There are 8 speaking tasks in the CELPIP Speaking section, each testing a different communication skill. Tasks range from giving advice and describing scenes to handling difficult situations and expressing opinions. The entire section takes about 15 minutes.

#### Can I use my noteboard during CELPIP Speaking?

Yes, you get an erasable noteboard and marker that you can use throughout the entire CELPIP test, including during Speaking prep time. Use it to jot down bullet points, key words, and your opening sentence during the 30 or 60 seconds of prep time. Don't try to write full sentences — keywords are faster and more useful.

#### What happens if I stop speaking before time runs out?

Your response is scored based on what you said, but unused time works against you. Leaving 20+ seconds of silence suggests you ran out of ideas, which limits how much vocabulary and fluency you can demonstrate. If you finish your main points early, expand with examples, contrasts, or consequences. Aim to speak until the last 5-10 seconds.

#### How is the CELPIP Speaking section scored?

CELPIP Speaking is scored on a scale of 0 to 12, and from level 3 up it maps directly to CLB levels (CELPIP 9 = CLB 9). Each task is scored by trained raters across four criteria: Content & Coherence, Vocabulary, Listenability, and Task Fulfillment. See our CELPIP scores and CLB guide for immigration point requirements.

#### What accent is best for CELPIP Speaking?

There is no preferred accent. CELPIP raters are trained to evaluate speakers of all accent backgrounds. What matters is clarity — can the rater understand what you're saying? Focus on clear pronunciation, natural intonation (rising pitch for questions, falling for statements), and consistent pacing rather than trying to imitate a Canadian or American accent.

#### How can I improve my CELPIP Speaking score quickly?

Master the prep time routine and eliminate filler words. These two changes make the biggest difference in the shortest time. Use prep time to plan 2-3 bullet points so you always know what to say next (reduces fillers automatically). Record yourself practicing and listen for "um," "uh," "like," and "so basically." Replace them with brief pauses or transition phrases. Build your word bank with task-specific speaking vocabulary so the right phrases come naturally. Practice daily with CELTESTPIP's Speaking section and follow our 4-week study plan for a structured approach.

#### Should I also work on my CELPIP Writing?

Yes — Speaking and Writing share the same scoring criteria (Content, Vocabulary, Task Fulfillment), so improving one helps the other. Many of the planning and vocabulary strategies overlap. See our CELPIP Writing tips guide for task-specific writing strategies that complement your speaking preparation.
