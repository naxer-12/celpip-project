## Part 1 Mastery: Listening to Problem Solving

In Part 1 you hear a man and a woman talk about a problem. One of them needs something, and the other one helps. You hear the conversation once, in three pieces, and after each piece you answer two or three questions.

There is no replay. So this lesson is about one thing: what to write down while you listen, so that the answers are already on your paper when the questions come.

---

## Part 2 Mastery: Listening to a Daily Life Conversation

In Part 2 you hear a man and a woman who know each other talk about something in their daily life. The conversation plays once, without a break, for about two minutes. Then the five questions come.

This is the big difference from Part 1. In Part 1 the conversation stops after each section and you answer two or three questions. In Part 2 you must hold the whole conversation until the end. So your notes matter even more.

---

## Part 3 Mastery: Listening for Information

In Part 3 you hear a man and a woman. One of them wants information, and the other one has it: a customer and a salesperson, a new employee and an experienced one, a parent and a shop worker. The conversation plays once, for about two and a half minutes, and then six questions come.

Part 3 is full of facts. Times, prices, dates and rules come quickly, one after another. Many people try to write all of them down. This lesson shows why that is not the best plan, and what to write instead.

---

## Part 4 Mastery: Listening to a News Item

In Part 4 you hear one person read a short news report, like a story on the radio or TV news. It plays once, for about a minute and a half. Then five questions appear on the screen.

Part 4 is also where the questions change. In Parts 1 to 3 you heard each question and had 30 seconds for it. From Part 4 on, you read all the questions on one screen, and you choose the order. This lesson covers both: what changes, and what to write while the news plays.

---

## Part 5 Mastery: Listening to a Discussion

In Part 5 you watch a short video of three people having a discussion. They share ideas, agree, disagree and make a plan. The video plays once. Then eight questions appear on one screen.

The hardest part is keeping three people apart. And there is a second problem many people only notice on test day: the questions almost never use the speakers' names. They say one colleague or the person who suggested a game . This lesson shows how to write notes that answer that kind of question.

---

## Part 6 Mastery – Listening to Viewpoints

Part 6 is the final listening section and it presents multiple viewpoints on a single topic. You will listen to either a dialogue or a series of monologues where different people express their opinions about a given issue. There are 6 questions here.

It could be structured as a moderated discussion (like a radio show where two callers share opposing views) or one speaker summarizing various people’s opinions (less likely but possible). The key is that you’ll hear more than one perspective about the same subject.

## The Question Types (Gist, Detail, Inference, Attitude)

Not all listening questions are created equal. They tend to fall into a few broad categories, each testing a different listening skill. By recognizing the type of question, you can tune your listening and reasoning to give the best answer. The four main types of questions in CELPIP Listening are:

- Gist/Main Idea Questions – Testing overall understanding.
- Detail Questions – Testing memory of specific facts and details.
- Inference Questions – Testing your ability to “read between the lines.”
- Attitude/Tone/Opinion Questions – Testing understanding of how something is said or what stance is taken.
Let’s break down each type, how to spot it, and strategies to answer correctly.

#### 1. Gist/Main Idea Questions

These ask about the overall idea or purpose of the conversation or talk. In other words, “What is this whole thing about?”

How to Recognize: Look for question stems like:

- “What is the conversation mainly about?”
- “What is the main issue being discussed?”
- “What is the purpose of the announcement?”
If you see keywords like “mainly about,” “overall,” “purpose,” or “why are the speakers talking,” it’s a gist question.

How to Answer: Since gist questions deal with the big picture, you should mentally summarize what you heard:

- During listening , pay attention to the opening lines and any repeated theme. Often the first few sentences and the conclusion give away the main point.
- Ask yourself, “In one sentence, what was that about?” after hearing the audio.
- Eliminate answers that focus on a small part of the conversation or a minor detail – those are usually traps. The correct answer will reflect the entire conversation’s focus.
- Beware of traps: A common trap answer for gist questions is one that mentions a detail that was in the audio but is not the central focus . For example, if a conversation is primarily about planning a surprise party (main idea) and at one point they discuss cake flavors, a trap answer might be “They are discussing favorite cake flavors.” Yes, cake came up, but that wasn’t the main purpose of the conversation – it was just a supporting detail.
Example: Suppose you hear a dialogue where a man and woman talk about renovating their kitchen – they discuss budget, choosing paint colors, and hiring a contractor. A gist question might be, “What are the speakers mainly talking about?” The correct answer should be along the lines of “remodeling a kitchen.” An option focusing only on one aspect (“choosing paint colors”) would be too narrow and likely incorrect.

#### 2. Detail Questions

These questions probe specific pieces of information mentioned in the audio. They often begin with Who, What, Where, When, or How .

How to Recognize:

- “What time will X happen?”
- “How much does X cost?”
- “Which of the following is true about X?”
- “What did the man say about…?”
Anything that clearly points to a particular fact or detail is this type. They often contain phrases like “according to the conversation,” “what does X mention about Y,” etc.
How to Answer:

- Listen for key facts: During the audio, jot down or mentally note names, dates, numbers, places – these often become detail questions.
- When the question appears, re-read it carefully to know exactly which detail is being asked. Sometimes detail questions are tricky in phrasing. For example, “Which of the following is NOT one of the reasons…?” – here they want the one thing that was not mentioned.
- Use your notes (if you took any) to locate the needed detail. If you didn’t note it, try to recall the part of the audio when that topic came up.
- Eliminate options that you know are incorrect. Usually, two options will clearly mismatch what was said.
- Beware of traps: The test loves to include options that are factually plausible but wrong . For instance, if the audio says “the ticket costs $50,” trap options might be “$15” (similar sounding) or “$60” (close, but not correct). Another trap: something that was mentioned but doesn’t answer the question. For example, if the question asks “When is the meeting?” and the conversation mentioned both the meeting time and another event’s time, a wrong option might give the time of the other event.
Example: The conversation says: “Let’s meet at the restaurant at 7:30 PM. It closes at 9, so we’ll have enough time to eat.” A detail question could be “When will they meet at the restaurant?” A correct answer: “7:30 PM.” Trap answers: “9:00 PM” (the closing time, not the meeting time), or “7:00 PM” (sounds kind of similar but wasn’t actually stated).

#### 3. Inference Questions

Inference questions go beyond what is directly said, asking you to deduce or conclude something from context and tone. Essentially, why or what does it imply?

- “What can be inferred about X…?”
- “What will likely happen next?”
- “Why do you think the woman says … ?” (implying there’s a hidden reason or meaning)
- “The man’s response implies that…”
If the question uses words like “infer,” “imply,” “suggest,” or asks for a conclusion or a likely outcome, it’s an inference question.
- Use context and tone: Think about not just what was said but how it was said. Did someone sound hesitant? Were they hinting at something without saying it outright?
- Combine clues: Sometimes the answer is the logical result of two or three things mentioned. For example, if a person said, “I lost my job” and later, “I’m worried about rent,” you can infer they might be having financial trouble.
- Be careful to stay within what’s reasonable given the conversation. Inferences shouldn’t be wild guesses – they should be supported by evidence in the audio, even if indirectly.
- Beware of traps: A common trap is an answer that goes too far beyond the information or an answer that reflects a misunderstanding of the context. If the speakers joke and laugh while saying something, an inference question might ask “How did the speakers feel about…?” A trap answer could be “They were very upset,” which contradicts the joking tone. Stick to the attitude or implication that actually fits the tone and content. Another trap: confusing who an inference is about. If the question asks “What can be inferred about the woman?”, make sure the answer you choose is about the woman , not the situation in general or the other person.
Example: If a man in the conversation says with a sigh, “Well, I guess I’ll be eating instant noodles again this week,” and earlier he talked about saving money, you can infer he’s low on funds or not happy about his meals. A possible inference question: “What can be inferred about the man’s situation?” Correct answer: “He is likely experiencing financial difficulties (tight budget).” A trap might be “He loves cooking at home,” which misinterprets his sigh.

#### 4. Attitude/Tone/Opinion Questions

These questions focus on the speaker’s attitude, feelings, or the opinion they hold. They test whether you understood how something was said or the speaker’s perspective, not just the facts.

- “How does the woman feel about…?”
- “What was the man’s attitude toward…?”
- “Which of the following best describes the speaker’s tone when discussing…?”
- “What is likely the woman’s opinion on…?”
If you see words like “feel,” “attitude,” “tone,” or “opinion,” that’s your cue.

- Listen to voice cues: Tone of voice can indicate if someone is happy, frustrated, uncertain, sarcastic, excited, etc. Did their voice rise or sound enthusiastic? Or was it flat and unenthusiastic? Emotional cues are often conveyed through intonation.
- Pay attention to word choice: If someone says, “I suppose it’s okay…”, that sounds hesitant or reluctant. If they say, “That’s absolutely fantastic!” the attitude is clearly positive and excited.
- Consider the context: Sometimes a speaker’s opinion is not stated outright but can be gleaned from what they emphasize or how they react. For example, if someone changes the subject quickly after an idea is mentioned, maybe they feel uncomfortable or disapproving of that idea.
- Beware of traps: Answers that are too extreme . Real human attitudes in the contexts given are often moderate. If a choice says “She is furious about the decision” but in the audio she sounded merely a bit annoyed and said something mildly negative, “furious” would be an exaggeration (trap). Also, watch out for answers that use the wrong emotion word. People sometimes mix up similar-sounding attitudes. For instance, “confused” vs “curious” – if the speaker was asking many questions, they might be curious, but a trap answer might say confused. Choose the nuance that truly matches what you heard. Another trap is attributing an opinion when the speaker actually stayed neutral. If the question asks for the speaker’s opinion on something and the speaker never clearly gave one, the safest answer might be neutral or “no strong opinion,” while a trap would put a strong stance in their mouth.

A correct answer: “Reluctant but resigned” (she doesn’t want to, but feels she may have to).
Trap answers: “Enthusiastic about the opportunity” (clearly wrong, opposite attitude) or “Angry about having to move” (she didn’t sound angry, just unhappy/reluctant).

#### General Strategy for Identifying Question Types

When the question appears, scan it for keywords like the ones mentioned above. Train yourself with practice questions to instantly categorize: “Ah, this one says ‘mainly about’ – it’s a main idea question. Let me recall the overall conversation.” Or “This one asks ‘What does X mean by…’ – that’s inference, I need to interpret that phrase in context.”

By knowing the question type, you allocate your mental energy correctly:

- For gist , you’ll think big picture.
- For detail , you’ll hunt in your memory/notes for a specific fact.
- For inference , you’ll piece together clues and tone.
- For attitude , you’ll recall how it was said and the speaker’s tone/words.
#### Common Trap Patterns by Question Type

- Gist traps: focus on a minor detail or use a term from the conversation out of context.
- Detail traps: correct info, wrong question (mentioned but irrelevant), or very close numbers/names (e.g., mixing up 15 vs 50, “John” vs “Jack”).
- Inference traps: an overly strong interpretation or one not supported (extrapolating too much), or something that would make sense in real life but wasn’t implied by what was said.
- Attitude traps: wrong emotion (e.g., saying “happy” when the tone was clearly unhappy but polite), or an answer that describes what we feel about the situation rather than the speaker’s feelings.
Being alert to these patterns will help you avoid falling for them.

### What is the CELPIP Listening score chart?

The CELPIP Listening score chart converts your raw number of correct answers into a standardized performance level that Canadian immigration authorities recognize. CELPIP measures listening on a 12-point scale, where each point corresponds directly to a Canadian Language Benchmark (CLB) level. CELPIP 7 = CLB 7, CELPIP 9 = CLB 9, and so on.

The Listening section itself contains 38 scored questions distributed across six parts:

- Problem Solving (Part 1)
- Conversations (Part 2)
- Information (Part 3)
- News Items (Part 4)
- Discussions (Part 5)
- Viewpoints (Part 6)
The test runs for about 46 to 55 minutes and is fully computer-delivered. The system counts your correct answers and converts that raw score to your final CELPIP level using equating tables calibrated by Paragon Testing Enterprises to ensure fairness across test forms of varying difficulty.

### What CLB is 27–31 correct answers on CELPIP Listening?

If you score between 27–31 correct on the CELPIP Listening test, you've reached CELPIP Level 7 , which equals CLB 7 . This is the minimum required for most Canadian immigration pathways, including Express Entry under Federal Skilled Worker and Canadian Experience Class programs. CLB 7 demonstrates "adequate proficiency in workplace and community contexts". It means you can understand moderately complex spoken English, follow detailed instructions, and engage in professional conversations, though you may occasionally need clarification.

For Express Entry specifically, CLB 7 in all four skills (Listening, Reading, Writing, Speaking) is the baseline threshold. Scoring higher (CLB 8–12) increases your Comprehensive Ranking System (CRS) points and strengthens your application significantly.

### How is the CELPIP Listening raw score (out of 38) calculated?

The CELPIP Listening test uses computer-based, objective scoring with no partial credit. Here's how it works:

Scoring method:

- Each of the 38 questions is marked either correct or incorrect
- One point per correct answer; no points for blank or incorrect responses
- No penalty for guessing , if you're unsure, it's always worth selecting an answer
- Your total correct answers form your "raw score"
Conversion to CELPIP level: Once the test is complete, Paragon's system applies a test-form-specific conversion table to your raw score. This equating process accounts for the difficulty of the particular questions you received, ensuring that a score of 28 on an easier test form converts to the same CELPIP level as a score of 28 on a harder form. This is why the chart gives approximate ranges that overlap, and why the same raw score can land on two levels.

Your final Listening level appears on your score report, usually within a few business days.

CELPIP Listening has six parts and 38 scored questions, and the whole section takes about 46 to 55 minutes. The questions are not spread evenly, so the parts carrying eight questions are worth more of your raw score than the parts carrying five.

Two things surprise people on test day. First, your test includes one unscored Practice Task, and it may also contain other unscored content used for test development. There is no replay, no pause and no going back, which is why note-taking matters more here than anywhere else on the test.

### What do I need on CELPIP Listening for Canadian citizenship?

Canadian citizenship applicants aged 18 to 54 must show CLB 4 in Listening and Speaking. The CELPIP-General LS test covers just those two skills, which is why most citizenship applicants take it. On the full-length CELPIP General test, you'd still need CLB 4 in Listening alongside the other three skills.

CLB 4 corresponds to roughly 11 to 18 correct answers on the Listening test (out of 38). This level shows "adequate proficiency for daily life activities". It means you can understand straightforward workplace or community conversations, follow simple instructions, and communicate your basic needs, though more complex or rapid speech may challenge you.

The citizenship threshold is notably lower than Express Entry's CLB 7 requirement. If you're targeting citizenship, focusing on CLB 4 in Listening and Speaking will satisfy your language requirements.

### What do I need for Express Entry?

Express Entry applications require CLB 7 across all four skills , including Listening. On CELPIP, that's CELPIP Level 7 in Listening , which typically means 27–31 correct answers (out of 38).

CLB 7 ("adequate proficiency in workplace and community contexts") is the baseline. However, scoring higher significantly boosts your CRS:

- CLB 8 (roughly 30 to 33 correct): 23 CRS points for Listening
- CLB 9 (roughly 33 to 35 correct): 31 CRS points for Listening
- CLB 10 or higher (35 to 38 correct): 34 CRS points for Listening
These are IRCC's points for one skill in your first official language, for a single applicant. CLB 7 earns 17. With a spouse or partner the figures are 16, 22, 29 and 32.

For competitive Express Entry applications, especially in high-draw rounds, many successful candidates aim for CLB 8 or higher in all skills. A CLB 9+ in Listening can make the difference in a crowded applicant pool.

### How do I improve my CELPIP Listening score?

Listening is often the easiest CELPIP skill to improve with targeted practice because you get immediate feedback on every question. Here are evidence-based strategies:

1. Practice with official CELPIP materials Paragon publishes free sample tests on celpip.ca . These reflect the exact format, timing, and difficulty of the real exam. Practice under timed conditions (47–55 minutes) to build stamina and train yourself to focus for the full duration.

2. Predict answers before you hear them For each question, read the stem and options carefully, then predict what the speaker might say. This active strategy trains your brain to listen for key information rather than passively absorbing everything. When the audio plays, you're already primed to catch the answer.

3. Develop listening-specific note-taking In Parts 5 (Discussions) and 6 (Viewpoints), where speakers share longer passages, jot down key nouns and verbs, not transcriptions. Practice capturing the main idea and opinion without trying to write every word.

4. Train with Canadian accents and workplace contexts Listen to CBC podcasts, Canadian news broadcasts, and workplace-style scenarios. CELPIP tests North American English in realistic professional and community settings. Familiarity with Canadian speech patterns, idioms, and communication norms directly improves comprehension.

5. Target your weak question type After each practice test, analyze which question types trip you up: inference questions (Part 5–6), detail-focused questions (Part 1–3), or listening-for-gist questions. Drill those types specifically before the test.

6. Use instant AI feedback Practice listening questions on CELTESTPIP with instant AI scoring so you know exactly which questions you miss and why. Repeat the same questions until they become automatic.

7. Set micro-targets If you're at Level 5 (roughly 17 to 23 correct), don't aim for Level 10 overnight. Target Level 6 (22 to 28 correct) over two to three weeks. Small, achievable wins build momentum and confidence.

### Is my CELPIP Listening score valid for 2 years?

Yes. CELPIP scores remain valid for two years from your test date for all Canadian immigration purposes, including Express Entry and citizenship applications. After two years, Immigration, Refugees and Citizenship Canada (IRCC) will not accept your score, and you'll need to retake the test.

If your score is approaching expiry and you're still in the application process, you can retake CELPIP before the two-year window closes. Many candidates take the test multiple times to improve their score, especially if they're aiming for higher CRS points in Express Entry.

### What is the CELPIP Listening test format?

Listening is the first section of the CELPIP-General test and of the CELPIP-General LS test, and the format is the same in both. You sit at a computer with a headset, the audio starts on its own, and you cannot pause it or play it again. After each clip, the questions appear.

CELPIP's own overview, published in the Listening Pro Study Pack, lists the parts like this. The minutes are approximate figures from that document, and the current Guidebook gives the whole section as 46 to 55 minutes.


| Part | What you hear | Questions | Time (about) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Practice Task (not scored) | one short clip to get used to the audio | 1 | 1 minute |
| Part 1: Listening to Problem Solving | a conversation in three pieces | 8 | 8 minutes |
| Part 2: Listening to a Daily Life Conversation | one conversation | 5 | 5 minutes |
| Part 3: Listening for Information | one conversation | 6 | 6 minutes |

Add those up and you get 38 scored questions. The part minutes are CELPIP's approximate figures and they come to less than the 46 to 55 minutes the section is timed at, partly because any unscored content you meet on the day counts toward the total.

Two facts about the whole section shape everything else. First, the clips get harder as you go: CELPIP states that the audio passages and questions increase in difficulty from Part 1 to Part 6. Second, there is no going back. Once a part's time is up, the test moves you forward on its own, and a previous part is closed for good.

### What does each CELPIP Listening part sound like?

Knowing the count is the easy half. What actually helps on the day is knowing who is talking, for how long, and about what, before the audio starts. This is what each part sounds like, in the shape CELPIP describes it.

#### Part 1: Listening to Problem Solving

Two speakers, one man and one woman, and they do not know each other. Think of a customer asking a cashier about a return, or two coworkers where one has a problem at home and the other wants to help. The conversation is split into three sections of about 1 to 1.5 minutes each, and after each section you get 2 or 3 questions about what you just heard. That makes Part 1 the gentlest opening: the clips are short and the questions arrive while the details are fresh. Practise it on the Part 1 question bank .

#### Part 2: Listening to a Daily Life Conversation

Two speakers again, but this time they know each other: coworkers on a project, teammates before a game, a couple planning the weekend. It is one clip of about 1.5 to 2 minutes, then about 5 questions. The tone is friendly and polite but not personal. The trap here is that you hear the whole conversation before any question, so your notes have to carry you. The Part 2 questions are the place to build that habit.

#### Part 3: Listening for Information

Still a conversation between two people, one man and one woman. One of them knows something the other wants: a salesperson walking a customer through a new car, an experienced employee explaining a procedure to a new one, a parent renting party decorations. One clip of about 2 to 2.5 minutes, then about 6 questions. The register moves from informal to somewhat formal, and the questions chase details in the order the speaker gave them. Work through the Part 3 questions with a pen in your hand.

#### Part 4: Listening to a News Item

The first single speaker. It is a news report of about 1.5 minutes, formal and factual, like a radio bulletin: a local event where something unexpected happened, a new technology, an encounter with animals. About 5 questions follow. From this part onward the question format changes, which the next section explains, so treat Part 4 as a reset. The Part 4 question bank has news items in this shape.

#### Part 5: Listening to a Discussion

The only video. Three people talk for about 1.5 to 2 minutes, then you answer about 8 questions. They might be neighbours planning a block party or classmates dividing up a group project, and they will sometimes disagree. CELPIP says you are not tested on visual details that have nothing to do with the conversation, so ignore the shirt colours and watch faces instead: a raised eyebrow tells you who is unconvinced. Practise tracking three voices on the Part 5 questions .

#### Part 6: Listening for Viewpoints

One speaker, about 3 minutes, and it is the longest clip of the test. This is a prepared talk, not a chat: a speech on climate change, a presentation on a technology, a description of a community issue and the options for solving it. CELPIP warns that the speaker uses low-frequency and specialized language, which is why this part sits last. About 6 questions. If you only have time to practise one part before test day, make it Part 6 .

### How do the questions work in Parts 1 to 3 versus Parts 4 to 6?

This is the format detail most guides skip, and it changes how you take notes.

Parts 1 to 3 are heard. Each question is read to you, one at a time, with the four answer choices on screen. You get 30 seconds to hear and answer each one, and you must take them in order. You cannot skip ahead and come back. So in the first three parts your notes need to be in sequence, because the questions arrive in roughly the same order as the answers appeared in the audio.

Parts 4 to 6 are read. The questions are sentence completions. All of them appear on one screen, you read the question and the four choices yourself, and you get one block of time for the whole screen. You can answer in any order and change an answer as often as you like until that time runs out. Here the smart move is to answer what you are sure of first, then use what is left on the ones you are guessing.

In every part, each question has one correct answer out of four, and no points are taken off for a wrong one. Answer everything.

### What are the three CELPIP Listening question types?

CELPIP sorts every Listening question into three types, and the same three appear on the Reading test. Once you can spot the type from the wording, you know where in your notes to look.

General Meaning. A big-picture question that asks you to pull together ideas from a long stretch of the clip or from more than one speaker. It sounds like "The two speakers are mainly discussing..." or "All three speakers agree that...". Look at your notes as a whole, not at one line.

Specific Information. A close-up question about one fact a speaker mentioned in one place: a name, a date, a price, a reason. It sounds like "The woman works as..." or "The man cannot come on Friday because...". This is where sequenced notes pay for themselves.

Inference. A question about something nobody said outright: how a speaker feels, what will probably happen next, what someone would most likely agree with. The giveaway words are "probably", "most likely", "sounds" and "feels". The answer is in the tone of voice and, in Part 5, in the faces.

Expect the correct option to be a paraphrase. The words on screen will not match the words in the audio, so a choice that repeats a phrase you heard is more often a distractor than the answer.

### Is any part of CELPIP Listening unscored?

Yes, in two ways, and neither should cost you a minute of worry.

The first is the Practice Task. CELPIP's Listening Pro Study Pack lists it before Part 1 as one unscored question taking about a minute, and the 2025 Guidebook for Test Takers lists it as a Listening component. The July 2026 Guidebook does not mention it by name, so treat the exact placement as CELPIP's description rather than a promise. Its job is to let you check the headset and the volume.

The second is development content. The current Guidebook says the Listening and Reading tests may contain unscored content used for test development, that it can appear anywhere, and that it looks exactly like the scored content. You will not be able to tell which questions those are. CELPIP also says the time spent on that content is included in the total test time and does not take time away from scored tasks. The only sensible response is to treat every question as real.

### Can you replay the audio or go back in CELPIP Listening?

No to both. Every clip plays one time only, it starts on its own, and there is no pause button. When a part's time ends, the test moves to the next screen and the previous part is locked.

What you do get is paper and a pen at the test centre, and you can ask for more paper. Your notes are never seen by anyone and have no effect on your score, so write badly and write fast. Two other things worth knowing before you sit down: the current CELPIP Guidebook describes the audio as a single North American accent, and in every two-person conversation one speaker is a woman and one is a man, which makes "the man said" and "the woman said" easy to track in your notes.

### What is the most important CELPIP Listening tip?

Know what you will see before the audio, and what you will not. Paragon's own Listening study pack is clear: the questions for each part come after the audio. Before the clip starts you only get a short introductory statement on screen, telling you who is talking and where.

That changes how you prepare. You cannot listen for the answer to a question you have not seen yet. You listen for the things questions usually ask about, and you write them down:

- The situation: who is speaking, where, and why. The introductory statement gives you most of this, so read it every time.
- The problem and the decision: what goes wrong, what solutions come up, which one they choose.
- Details: numbers, days, times, prices, names.
- Opinions: who thinks what, and whether they change their mind.
There is no penalty for a wrong answer. Paragon gives one point for each correct answer and takes nothing away for a wrong one, so a guess can only help you.

### How should you take notes on CELPIP Listening?

You get paper and a pen at the test centre, and you can ask for more paper. Nobody reads your notes and they have no effect on your score, so write whatever helps you, as messy as you like.

Set up your page before the audio starts. This is the habit that separates a 7 from a 9. The moment you read the introductory statement, draw your layout:

- Two speakers (Parts 1, 2 and 3): draw a line down the middle. Put M on one side and W on the other. Paragon says that in every two-person conversation, one speaker is a man and one is a woman, so this layout always fits.
- Three speakers (Part 5): three columns, labelled by where each person sits on screen as they are introduced.
- One speaker (Parts 4 and 6): one column, with space down the side for names. When a news report names someone, it gives the full name once and then uses only the last name, so write the last name the first time you hear it.
Write keywords, not sentences. "Mon, 3pm, $200" is faster and more useful than "the meeting is on Monday at 3 pm and the budget is $200". Use symbols you already know: an arrow for "leads to", a tick for agree, a cross for disagree, a question mark for unsure.

Keep your notes in order. The questions come in roughly the same order as the answers appeared in the audio. If your notes run top to bottom in the order you heard things, you can find the answer fast.

Keep writing when the questions start in Parts 1 to 3. In these parts you hear each question read out. Note its keywords, then look at your notes from the audio.

Practise this before test day with pen and paper, not a keyboard. Typing notes on a screen is not the same skill.

### Part 1: Listening to Problem Solving

What it is: one conversation between two people, played in three sections of about 1 to 1.5 minutes each. After each section you answer 2 or 3 questions, 8 questions in all.

What makes it tricky: the first idea is rarely the final one. Speaker A suggests one thing, speaker B pushes back, and they end up somewhere else.

- Track the problem AND the solution. Questions often ask what was decided, not what was discussed. Listen for closing phrases: "So let's go with...", "I think the best option is...", "Why don't we..."
- Note every option and cross out the losers. If they reject Saturday, put a line through it. The answer is what is left standing.
- Use the pause between sections. You answer questions after each section, so tidy your notes for the next one.

### Part 2: Listening to a Daily Life Conversation

What it is: one conversation of about 1.5 to 2 minutes between two people who know each other: neighbours, friends, coworkers. Five questions.

What makes it tricky: the language is casual, so speakers imply things instead of saying them. "I'm not sure that's the best idea" does not mean "I'm unsure". It means "I disagree."

- Listen for tone, not just words. Questions often ask how someone feels or what they mean. Hesitation, enthusiasm and politeness all carry meaning.
- Track who says what. When a question asks "What does the woman suggest?", your M and W columns answer it.

### Part 3: Listening for Information

What it is: one conversation of about 2 to 2.5 minutes between a man and a woman. One of them has information the other wants, such as a customer asking a salesperson about a new car. Six questions.

What makes it tricky: it is packed with facts. If you did not catch the detail, you cannot reason your way to it.

### Part 4: Listening to a News Item

What it is: one speaker reading a news report of about 1.5 minutes. Five questions, all on one screen, where you complete each sentence by choosing from four options.

What makes it tricky: news packs a lot into a short clip, and the people in it get mixed up easily.

- Listen for the five Ws: who, what, where, when, why. The first sentence usually gives you the main story. Write it down.
- Write the last names. A person is introduced once by full name, then only by last name. If you missed "Dr. Elena Ruiz", you will not know who "Ruiz" is.
- Catch the numbers. Figures, percentages and dates are favourite question targets.
- Keep the sources apart. "The mayor said..." and "Critics argue..." are two positions. Note which belongs to whom.

### Part 5: Listening to a Discussion

What it is: a video of three people discussing something, about 1.5 to 2 minutes, often a decision at work or in the community. Eight questions, the most of any part, all on one screen.

What makes it tricky: three people means three positions, and they shift as people agree and disagree.

- Label the speakers by screen position as they are introduced. Left, middle, right, with a name if you catch it. Then note each stance: "L = expand, M = cautious, R = agrees with L."
- Use faces to read feelings. Facial expressions and body language help you tell how each person feels. You will not be asked about visual details that have nothing to do with the conversation, like the colour of someone's shirt, so don't waste attention on them.
- Listen for agreement and disagreement. "I see your point, but...", "Exactly!", "I'm not so sure about that" tell you where each person stands.

### Part 6: Listening for Viewpoints

What it is: one speaker giving a prepared talk of about 3 minutes, presenting different views on an issue. Six questions, all on one screen. The language is harder here, with less common and more specialised words.

What makes it tricky: one voice carries several opinions, and it is easy to mix up the speaker's own view with the views they report.

- Separate reported views from the speaker's own. "Dr. Smith argues...", "Critics say...", "Supporters believe..." are other people. The speaker's own view often comes near the end: "Ultimately..." or "The evidence suggests..."
- Note who, what they think, and why. Last names again: once you hear the full name, you will usually hear only the last name after that.
- Track the turns. "However", "on the other hand", "in contrast", "while some argue" mean a new view is starting. Start a new line in your notes.
- Unknown word in a question? Work out whether it sounds positive or negative from the sentence around it. That is often enough to choose.

### How do you manage your time on CELPIP Listening?

The timing works differently in the two halves of the test, and knowing this stops the panic.

- Parts 1 to 3: you hear each question and see four answers, one question at a time. You get 30 seconds to hear and answer each one, then the test moves on by itself. Pick your best answer before the time runs out. There is no going back.
- Parts 4 to 6: all the questions for the part sit on one screen with one timer for the whole screen. You can answer them in any order, so do the easy ones first, then come back to the hard ones. Aim for about 30 seconds per question.
In both halves, if you are stuck, guess and move on. One missed question costs you one point. Getting stuck on it and losing the next two costs you three.

You get a 9 by getting enough questions right across all six parts, especially the later ones. Paragon gives one point per correct answer, and your level is then calculated from your points and the difficulty of the questions. The audio and questions get harder from Part 1 to Part 6, so Parts 5 and 6 are where most 9s are won or lost.
