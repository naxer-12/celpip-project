## Canadian Writing Conventions

### ⚠️ Read this before the rest of the lesson

> Paragon publishes free instructions about the test, and those instructions never ask for Canadian spelling, Canadian date formats, or Canadian address layout. What they ask for is readability , which means paragraphs, capital letters and punctuation, so that the writing is easy to read. Two sections below describe things you should not put in a Task 1 answer at all: the mailing address (section 4) and the professional signature (section 7) . Paragon names both as unnecessary, and together they can eat fifty words of a two hundred word limit.

So why is this lesson here? Because these conventions are real and useful. You will need them for job applications, for letters to your landlord or your bank, for work email, and for the Reading section, where Canadian formats appear in the texts you have to understand. Learn them for your life in Canada. Do not spend exam minutes on them.

## Coherence & Organization

Organization is worth more than it looks. Two of the four things inside the Readability score are about shape rather than correctness: paragraphing and connectors . And paragraphs are one of only three format items that CELPIP actually marks, alongside capital letters and punctuation.

An examiner reads a great many answers. One block of text is hard work before it has even been judged. Clear paragraphs make your ideas findable, and findable ideas score better than the same ideas buried.

## Fast Planning for Tasks

Planning feels like time you're not writing. It's the opposite: the five minutes you spend here are bought back with interest, because the thing that actually eats your writing time isn't typing, it's deciding .

Stopping mid-sentence to work out what comes next, or which word fits, or whether that paragraph was any good, is what turns eighteen minutes of writing into twenty-five. A plan front-loads every one of those decisions into a window where they're cheap.

Your window: about 5 minutes for Task 1, about 4 minutes for Task 2.

## Scoring & What “Good” Looks Like

You can write an email with no grammar mistakes at all and still get a 7.

That surprises people, and it is the most useful thing to understand before you study anything else. Your writing is scored against CELPIP's published performance standards, on four areas . Inside those four areas there are sixteen factors . Grammar is one of the sixteen.

So if grammar is the only thing you have been practising, you have been polishing one of sixteen factors.

## Task 1 Essentials: Writing an Email

Task 1 gives you one everyday situation and asks for an email of 150 to 200 words in about 27 minutes . You type it on screen with a word counter and a basic spell check.

You are scored on four areas, against CELPIP's published performance standards, and almost every prompt ends with three bullet points that an examiner checks one by one. So the job is to cover all three, develop each one, in the right voice, inside 200 words.

Most preparation time goes on the wrong parts of that. People study email layout and Canadian address formats, and skip the two decisions that actually move the score.

## Task 2 Essentials: Responding to Survey Questions

Task 2 is the survey response. You're given a short situation, your city, your building, or your workplace is deciding something, and you pick one side and explain why.

The specification


|  |  |
| --- | --- |
| Length | 150–200 words. Aim for ~180. |
| Time | About 26 minutes (Task 1 gets 27 — they're different). |
| Format | No greeting, no sign-off. This is not the email task. Start with your opinion. |
| On screen | Timer, word counter, basic spell check. |

The danger zones. Under about 130 words your answer looks thin and empty. Over about 220 you start repeating yourself and making more mistakes. Both drag your score down, so 180 is the sweet spot — long enough to develop two reasons properly, short enough to stay controlled.

How it's scored: four criteria

- Content & Coherence — do your ideas make sense and connect?
- Vocabulary — the right words, not the biggest words.
- Readability — clean grammar, easy sentences, clear layout.
- Task Fulfillment — did you actually answer the question asked?
Every mistake in this lesson is one of those four going wrong.

## Task Fulfillment & Tone Control

Task Fulfillment is one of the four scoring areas, and it holds four of the sixteen factors:


| Factor | What it checks |
| --- | --- |
| Relevance | Did you answer the question they actually asked? |
| Completeness | Did you cover every part of it? |
| Tone | Does your voice suit the person reading? |

None of these is about your English. That is what makes them dangerous. A test taker with excellent grammar can lose this entire area without writing a single incorrect sentence, and usually has no idea it happened.

## Timing Drills & Editing Routines

Most timing advice stops at "plan, then write, then check." It never tells you what to do when the clock is running down and you're starting to panic.

That is the gap this lesson fills.

The whole test: 53 minutes. Task 1 (email) — 27 minutes. Task 2 (survey) — 26 minutes. Both want 150–200 words.

The single most common way to lose easy marks is to keep typing until the clock runs out and never edit. It feels productive; you're producing words, the counter is climbing. But an unedited answer bleeds marks in the two criteria that are cheapest to protect: task fulfillment and readability . A missing word, a typo, a comma splice, one bullet you never actually answered.

So the first thing to fix is a belief:

> Editing is not something extra you do if there's time left. It is part of writing, and the time for it is booked in advance.

## Vocabulary for Precision

Vocabulary is one of the four areas your CELPIP Writing is scored on, and it is scored on Speaking too. It is also the area where hard work most often makes things worse .

Here is the mistake almost everyone makes: memorising lists of "advanced" words, believing that using them proves a high level. The opposite is true. When you force a heavy word into a simple sentence, a grader doesn't see sophistication — they see someone who is not fully in control of the language . The very thing you thought was helping is quietly costing you points.

### How is the CELPIP Writing test scored?

Here's the part most score charts get wrong: CELPIP Writing is not graded out of a fixed number of correct answers the way Reading and Listening are. There's no "28 out of 38." Writing is scored by trained raters against published performance descriptors, and it comes down to two tasks and four criteria.

You complete two writing tasks:

- Task 1 — Writing an Email: (~27 minutes, roughly 150–200 words). You respond to a situation, and the tone has to fit the person you are writing to: a landlord and a friend get different emails.
- Task 2 — Responding to Survey Questions: (~26 minutes, roughly 150–200 words). You pick a position on a survey question and defend it.
Each task is scored against CELPIP's published performance standards on the same four criteria:

- Content/Coherence : how well your ideas are organized, developed, and connected.
- Vocabulary : the range and accuracy of your word choice.
- Readability : grammar, sentence structure, spelling, punctuation, and formatting.
- Task Fulfillment : whether you addressed every part of the prompt in the right tone and format.
Your final CELPIP Writing level reflects how you did on both tasks. CELPIP does not publish a formula for combining them, so a strong survey response will not reliably make up for a weak email. The practical takeaway: you can't coast on one strong task. A weak email drags down a strong essay and vice versa, so you have to prepare for both. ### What do the CELPIP Writing level descriptors say?

CELPIP publishes level descriptors for Writing: short "At this level, I can" statements for each level, in the same four categories your two tasks are rated on (Content/Coherence, Vocabulary, Readability and Task Fulfillment). They are the closest thing to a public rubric the test has, and the official wording is in the CELPIP Level Descriptors document on celpip.ca . Below is my own summary of what each category asks for as the levels rise. It is written in my words, not quoted from the document.

Read them the way a rater reads your email: each category on its own, and the level you get is a judgement across all four. There are many ways to land on the same level. A short note follows each summary on what a 9 does that a 7 does not, because that is the gap most of my students are trying to close.

For what each level means in plain words, and what it is enough for in Canada, see what each CELPIP level means

#### Content/Coherence


| Levels | In plain words |
| --- | --- |
| 10 to 12 | Formal and informal texts with real complexity, with ideas developed through relevant facts, details and examples. |
| 9 | Short texts of some complexity, where each key idea is backed by a relevant fact, description or detail. |
| 7 to 8 | Short, moderately complex texts built around a main idea with supporting detail. |
| 5 to 6 | Short, clear texts with a main idea and some related or supporting points. |
| 3 to 4 | Simple sentences and short paragraphs, mostly about yourself. |

What a 9 does that a 7 does not. A 7 states a main idea and backs it with a detail. A 9 supports several key ideas, each with a relevant fact, description or detail, and the text has some complexity: a reason inside a reason, a comparison, a condition. In an email that means every bullet in the prompt gets its own developed paragraph, not one sentence each. In the survey task it means your position comes with two or three distinct reasons, each one explained, not the same reason said three ways.

#### Vocabulary


| Levels | In plain words |
| --- | --- |
| 10 to 12 | Specialised, formal and everyday words, chosen to say exactly what you mean. |
| 9 | Words and phrases chosen to give accurate details, descriptions and comparisons. |
| 7 to 8 | Common words plus some words specific to the situation. |
| 5 to 6 | Common words and phrases. |
| 3 to 4 | Very common, basic words. |

What a 9 does that a 7 does not. Look at the verb the descriptors use. Up to Level 8 you use words. From Level 9 you choose them. A 7 uses common words and reaches for a topic word when it has to. A 9 picks the word that gives an accurate detail or a fair comparison, and the reader can see the choice was made. You do not get there with rare words. You get there with the exact common word: "the fridge leaks" rather than "the appliance has an issue".

#### Readability


| Levels | In plain words |
| --- | --- |
| 10 to 12 | Ideas linked smoothly within and between paragraphs, with good to very good control of a wide range of complex structures. |
| 9 | Well-organised paragraphs, control of a range of complex and varied structures, and good spelling and punctuation. |
| 8 | Well-organised paragraphs, with good control of complex structures, spelling and punctuation. |
| 7 | Related ideas grouped into paragraphs, adequate control of complex structures, and good control of simple grammar. |
| 5 to 6 | Related ideas linked or grouped into paragraphs, with good control of simple grammar. |
| 3 to 4 | Some control of simple grammar, capital letters and basic punctuation. |

What a 9 does that a 7 does not. Two things. A 7 has "adequate" control of complex sentences, which in practice means the long sentences carry the errors. A 9 has control of a range of them, so the errors do not cluster in the complex sentences. And a 7 organizes related ideas into paragraphs, while a 9 writes well-organized ones: one job per paragraph, a first sentence that says what the job is. The cheapest gain here is not more grammar. It is writing the complex sentence you can control instead of the one you cannot.

#### Task Fulfillment


| Levels | In plain words |
| --- | --- |
| 10 to 12 | Tone and style that fit the situation and follow most formal and informal conventions, with your meaning clear and precise. |
| 9 | Tone and style that follow most informal and some formal conventions, with your intended meaning clear. |
| 7 to 8 | Tone and style that follow common conventions, with your main ideas or facts clear. |
| 5 to 6 | Tone that is sometimes right for the situation, and some information on familiar topics. |
| 3 to 4 | Common phrases and basic information about yourself or very familiar topics. |

What a 9 does that a 7 does not. A 7 conveys facts in a style that follows most common conventions. A 9 conveys what it meant to convey, and it follows most informal conventions and some formal ones. That last phrase is the whole email task. If the prompt is a complaint to a building manager and you open with "Hey", the tone is wrong for the situation, and it costs you in this category no matter how good the grammar is. Match the register the prompt sets, cover every bullet, and say clearly what you want the reader to do.

The descriptors also say who you are writing for at each level. At 9 and 10 it is a defined audience, in a formal or informal situation. At 7 and 8 it is a familiar or clearly defined audience. Only at 11 and 12 does the audience become undefined. Both tasks give you a defined reader (the person you are emailing, the organization running the survey), so those are the lines that apply to you.

### How does CELPIP Writing compare to IELTS Writing?

Both CELPIP and IELTS are accepted by IRCC for Canadian immigration, and both convert to the same CLB scale. So the best way to compare them is through CLB, not directly. Here's how CELPIP Writing levels line up with IELTS Writing bands, based on IRCC's official CLB equivalency:


| CELPIP Writing / CLB | IELTS Writing band |
| --- | --- |
| 10–12 | 7.5–9.0 |
| 9 | 7.0 |
| 8 | 6.5 |
| 7 | 6.0 |
| 6 | 5.5 |
| 5 | 5.0 |
| 4 | 4.0 |

So in plain terms: CELPIP Writing 8 ≈ IELTS Writing 6.5 , and CELPIP Writing 9 ≈ IELTS Writing 7.0 . These are approximate cross-references. The two tests measure writing differently (CELPIP uses two short real-world tasks; IELTS uses a graph-description task plus an essay), so treat the alignment as a planning guide, not a guarantee. For anything official, use the IRCC test equivalency charts.

### What is a good CELPIP Writing score?

A "good" score depends entirely on what you need it for:

- CLB 7 (CELPIP Writing 7) is the level most Express Entry candidates aim for. Federal Skilled Worker and Canadian Experience Class applications generally require CLB 7 across all four skills, so Writing 7 is the practical floor for most immigration streams.
- CLB 9 (CELPIP Writing 9) is where your Comprehensive Ranking System (CRS) points jump meaningfully. Language is one of the biggest levers in Express Entry, and moving from CLB 7 to CLB 9 across your skills can add dozens of CRS points. It is often the difference between an invitation and a wait.
- Citizenship is the exception: the citizenship language requirement only tests Listening and Speaking , so there is no Writing requirement for a citizenship application. If citizenship is your only goal, Writing isn't on the exam.
So before you set a target, work backwards from your program. ### How can I improve my CELPIP Writing score?

Writing is one of the most improvable CELPIP skills, because the four criteria are concrete and coachable. The catch is that you can't grade your own writing objectively, which is exactly where most self-study stalls.

A few strategies that move the needle:

1. Write to the criteria, not to a word count. Every response is judged on Content/Coherence, Vocabulary, Readability, and Task Fulfillment. Before you submit, check each one: Did I answer every part of the prompt (Task Fulfillment)? Is my tone right? Are my ideas connected with real transitions (Coherence)?

2. Nail the register. Task 1 lives or dies on tone. A formal complaint email and a friendly note to a coworker are scored differently. Read the prompt's audience carefully and match it. Register mismatch is one of the most common avoidable point losses.

3. Build a bank of precise vocabulary and collocations. Raters reward accurate, varied word choice, not big words used incorrectly. Learn natural collocations ("raise a concern," "meet a deadline") rather than memorizing rare synonyms.

4. Time yourself. You get roughly 26–27 minutes per task. Practice planning for 3–5 minutes, writing, then leaving 2–3 minutes to proofread. Running out of time means unfinished tasks, and incomplete responses hurt Task Fulfillment badly.

5. Get objective feedback fast. This is the real bottleneck. Human tutoring is slow and expensive, and you can't judge your own errors. ## CELPIP Writing Spell Check & Retake Rules: What You Must Know

The CELPIP writing test has a manual spell-checker but no auto-correct function. About retaking, administrators forbid candidates from retaking only the writing section. A $55 CAD score re-evaluation exists instead, letting candidates challenge a low writing score without booking a new four-hour test session.

### Does the CELPIP Writing Test Have Spell Check?

Yes, the CELPIP test features an automatic spelling checker. The software underlines misspelled words in real time. It shows a dropdown list of suggested corrections next to each flagged word. A candidate must click the underlined word and manually select the correct spelling from that list; the system will not fix anything on its own, which means a misspelled word left untouched at the end of the test stays wrong on the final submission.

This checker runs continuously during both writing tasks, the email-response task (task 1) and the survey-response task (task 2), without needing to be turned on or refreshed. It flags a typo the moment a candidate finishes typing a word and moves to the next one. The dropdown typically offers two to four alternative spellings ranked by similarity to what was typed. A candidate can also simply retype the word manually instead of clicking a suggestion, and either method produces the same result on the final transcript.

#### The Danger of Relying on the Spelling Checker

The spelling checker corrects spelling. It does not touch grammar. It ignores sentence structure entirely, and a test-taker who treats the tool as a full proofreader is gambling with the Vocabulary and Readability scoring categories that human raters assess separately from spelling accuracy. Consider a candidate writing about a policy change at work who means to say the announcement will "affect" morale. The spelling checker flags nothing if that candidate accidentally types "effect" instead, because both words are spelled correctly on their own. A human rater reading the finished response sees a homophone error that muddies meaning, and that error pulls down the Vocabulary score even though the on-screen tool showed zero red underlines.

The same blind spot applies to subject-verb agreement, article usage, and run-on sentences. A sentence like "The manager have already told the staffs about the new policy" contains three separate errors: a verb agreement mistake, an uncountable noun treated as plural, and no spelling mistake at all. The checker leaves every one of those errors unmarked because every word is spelled correctly. Candidates who finish typing and immediately click submit, trusting a clean screen of black text as proof of a clean response, are the ones most likely to lose points they never saw coming.

### Does CELPIP Have Auto-Correct Software?

CELPIP does not feature auto-correct. The interface will not fix capitalization, punctuation, or spelling automatically as a candidate types, unlike a smartphone keyboard or a word processor with live grammar suggestions. A candidate who types "toronto" instead of "Toronto" sees no automatic fix and no warning; the lowercase letter stays on the page exactly as typed. Raters count this kind of missed capitalization on proper nouns like "Canada" or "Toronto" against the Language Use score, and repeated instances compound the penalty across both writing tasks.

The absence of auto-correct also means punctuation errors survive untouched. A missing period between two independent clauses does not trigger any visual warning. Neither does a comma splice, and neither does a missing apostrophe in a contraction like "dont" or "cant." Candidates coming from typing environments that quietly fix these issues, such as a phone keyboard or Google Docs, need to unlearn that dependency before test day or risk submitting a response with small mechanical errors they never had to catch by hand before.

### Can I Retake the CELPIP Writing Section Only?

Candidates cannot retake only the writing section. Paragon Testing , the organization that delivers CELPIP under the Prometric testing network, requires every retake to cover all four components: Listening, Reading, Writing, and Speaking. A candidate who scores a 7 in Writing but a 9 in every other section still sits through the entire test again to generate one new writing number. Paragon's own policy also caps how quickly that retake can happen: a candidate may not register for more than one test session within five (5) calendar days of another, so last-minute back-to-back attempts are not an option.

The financial case against a full retake is direct. CELPIP-General costs $299 CAD plus tax per sitting ( see the cost page ), and that fee buys one complete attempt at all four skills, not a targeted redo of Writing alone.

#### The $55 CAD Alternative: CELPIP Score Re-evaluation

A CELPIP score re-evaluation lets a candidate challenge the Writing score without a full retake. The request must go in within exactly six (6) months of the original test date, filed directly through the candidate's CELPIP account. The fee is $55 CAD plus tax per component, rising to roughly $110 CAD plus tax if a candidate re-evaluates both Writing and Speaking together. Only Writing and Speaking qualify for this process because Listening and Reading are scored by computer rather than by a human rater, so requesting a re-evaluation of either of those two sections almost never changes the outcome.

The mechanics of the refund are what remove the risk. If the re-evaluation raises the score, Paragon refunds the full $55 fee to the candidate for that component. The score can never drop during re-evaluation, only rise or stay the same. Results typically arrive within one to two weeks of submitting the request and payment, and a different senior rater, not the original one, conducts the re-score. A candidate effectively pays $55 CAD to find out whether the first rater's judgment holds up, and gets that money back the moment it does not.

#### Is a CELPIP Score Re-evaluation Worth the Money?

Yes, for most candidates sitting just below their target CLB level, a re-evaluation is worth the $55 CAD. A raised score refunds the fee entirely, and a confirmed score costs less than one-fifth of a full retake.

A candidate who scored an 8 in Writing but needs a 9 for a specific Express Entry point threshold is a strong fit for this option, especially if the gap is a single band. A candidate whose Writing score came in two or more bands below every other section, however, has a different problem: that gap usually points to a genuine performance issue rather than a scoring error, and a re-evaluation is unlikely to close it.

Retaking the full test still makes sense in narrower cases. A candidate who suspects a system-wide technical failure during the test, one who reported a test-day issue to staff within 24 hours as required by policy, has a stronger claim through a different channel than re-evaluation. A candidate who simply was not ready on test day and knows it should treat a retake as a fresh attempt at every skill, not just Writing, and should build in real study time before booking it.

### Tactical Strategies for the CELPIP Writing Interface

A disciplined time split is better than a rushed one. Spend three to four minutes planning each response before typing a single word, then write for the most of the allotted time, and reserve the final three minutes strictly for reviewing the red-underlined words the spell-checker has flagged. This sequence forces a candidate to fix errors with time still on the clock instead of noticing them after submission.

Both CELPIP writing tasks require 150 to 200 words, and the live word counter in the corner of the screen tracks this in real time, so candidates should monitor the counter throughout the response rather than checking it only once at the end. A response that is below 150 words costs points for Task Fulfillment regardless of how strong the grammar is elsewhere.

Candidates should click every red-underlined word, read the suggested corrections carefully, and reject incorrect suggestions rather than clicking the first option out of habit. They should also scan proper nouns for missing capital letters, since the spell-checker will not catch a lowercase "canada" on its own. Reading the response once silently for grammar, separate from the spell-check pass, catches the agreement and article errors the software ignores entirely.

Typing speed and screen familiarity matter as much as review habits. CELPIP is a computer-delivered test, so a candidate who normally writes by hand loses time hunting for keys instead of composing sentences. Practicing on a standard keyboard, in the same font and screen layout the real interface uses, removes that friction before it costs any of the four to five minutes a candidate needs for planning. A candidate who has never typed a timed 200-word response before test day is spending part of the exam learning to type, not writing.

Building this habit in practice matters more than reading about it once. A candidate who rehearses the same three-minute review sequence, click flagged words, reject bad suggestions, scan capitalization, read once for grammar, during every timed practice test performs it automatically on the real exam.

Adrenaline on test day makes it easy to skip the final check and submit a response full of fixable errors instead. Treating that review window as a fixed, non-negotiable step of the writing process is what separates candidates who convert a shaky first draft into a passing score from those who do not.

| Dimension | What raters look at | Common way to lose it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Content/Coherence | how many ideas you have, how good they are, how you order them, and the examples that back them up | ideas listed with no detail, or jumping between ideas |
| Vocabulary | choosing the right words, using them naturally, having enough variety, and being exact | repeating the same words, vague words, phrases copied from the prompt |
| Readability | layout and paragraphs, linking words between ideas, grammar and sentence structure, spelling and punctuation | one long paragraph, run-on sentences, missing articles |
| Task Fulfillment | answering what was asked, covering every part, the right tone, and the right length | skipping a part of the prompt, the wrong tone for the reader |

CELPIP publishes these dimensions but not a formula for how they combine into your level. So don't bet on one strength covering a weakness. Perfect grammar will not rescue an email that skipped one of the things the prompt asked for.

### CELPIP Task 1 Strategy: Writing an Email

Task 1 gives you 27 minutes to write an email of 150 to 200 words about an everyday situation. The prompt names the person you are writing to and usually asks you to do around three things. Every one of them needs an answer.

#### Step 1: Decide who you are writing to (30 seconds)

This is the most important decision in Task 1. Get it wrong and you lose Task Fulfillment marks even if your grammar is perfect.

CELPIP's 2026 overview study pack places the Task 1 reader somewhere in the middle: usually not a close friend or a family member, and not a government official either. Its tone advice is to avoid both extremes, so the email should not sound stiff and should not sound too casual. So the real question is how well you know the reader and what you are asking for.


| If the reader is | Write | Greeting | Sign-off |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| someone you know: a neighbour, a coworker, a member of your club | polite and friendly | "Hi Sarah," or "Dear Sarah," | "Thanks again," or "Best regards," |
| someone in a role: a manager, a landlord, a company, a city office | more formal | "Dear Ms. Rivera," | "Sincerely," or "Kind regards," |

How to tell: the prompt names the reader and your relationship. Read it twice before you plan. Either way, CELPIP's Target 9+ advice applies: use a greeting and sign-off that fit the situation, and avoid rudeness, sarcasm and casual slang.

#### Step 2: Plan before you write (about 4 minutes)

Don't start typing right away.

- Note the reader and the tone.
- Read every part of the prompt and write a one-line idea for each.
- Put them in order: greeting, purpose, one paragraph per part, closing, sign-off.
This step prevents the two biggest Task 1 problems: a messy email and a part of the prompt you forgot to answer.

#### Step 3: Write with this structure (about 20 minutes)

Greeting (one line). Match the reader. "Dear Mr. Thompson," for a manager. "Hi Sarah," for a neighbour you know.

Purpose (one or two sentences). Say why you are writing. Get to the point.

Body (one short paragraph per part of the prompt). Two or three sentences each, with one specific detail. Don't just state the point. Develop it a little.

Closing (one or two sentences). A forward-looking line or a polite request that fits the tone, then your sign-off and name.

#### Step 4: Check (about 3 minutes)

Read once for grammar. Read again for flow and for every part of the prompt. The spell check catches misspelled words, but it will not catch a real word in the wrong place ("their" for "there") or a missing article.

#### Task 1 example: what a 9+ answer looks like

Prompt: Write an email to your building manager about noise from another apartment. In your email, describe the noise, say when it happens, and explain what you would like the manager to do.

> Dear Ms. Rivera, I am writing to report an ongoing noise problem from the apartment directly above mine, unit 405. For the past three weeks, loud music has been playing almost every evening between 10 p.m. and midnight, and on weekends it sometimes continues until 1 a.m. The bass is especially disruptive. It vibrates through the ceiling into my bedroom, and it makes it very difficult to sleep before my early shifts at the hospital. I have already tried to speak with the tenant directly, but no one answered the door on two separate occasions. I also left a polite note, and the situation has not improved. I would appreciate it if you could contact the tenant and remind them of the building's quiet hours, which the lease sets from 11 p.m. to 7 a.m. If the noise continues after that, I would like to meet with you to discuss what other options might be available. Thank you for your time and attention to this matter. Sincerely, Alex Chen

Why it works. The formal tone matches a building manager. All three parts of the prompt are answered, each with a concrete detail (unit 405, 10 p.m. to midnight, the bass coming through the ceiling, the lease's quiet hours). The writer shows they tried to solve it first, which makes the request reasonable. One idea per paragraph. (169 words.)

### CELPIP Writing Task 2 tips: how do you answer the survey?

Task 2 gives you 26 minutes and 150 to 200 words. You read a short survey with two options, pick one, and explain why. It is not an email, so there is no greeting and no sign-off, and it is not an academic essay either. Write to the people running the survey, clearly and politely.

#### Step 1: Choose your side quickly (30 seconds)

Don't overthink it. Pick the option you can support with better examples, not the one you would choose in real life. If you have two strong reasons for Option A and one vague reason for Option B, write about A.

#### Step 2: Plan your response (about 3 minutes)

Write down:

- your choice
- reason 1 and a specific detail
- reason 2 and a specific detail, from a different angle
- one sentence about the other option
#### Step 3: Write with this structure (about 18 minutes)

Opening (one or two sentences). State your choice in the first sentence. No warm-up.

Reason 1 (three or four sentences). The reason, then a specific example from real life: a place, a person, a number from your own week.

Reason 2 (three or four sentences). A different angle from reason 1, with its own detail.

The other option, then your conclusion (two or three sentences). Say something fair about the option you did not choose, then why yours is still better. CELPIP's own Target 9+ advice for Task 2 includes comparing your choice with the option you did not pick, so this paragraph earns its place.

Give reasons, not suggestions or descriptions, and write in paragraphs, never in point form.

#### Step 4: Check (about 5 minutes)

Same as Task 1: grammar first, then flow, then the word count on screen.

#### Task 2 example: what a 9+ answer looks like

Prompt: Your employer is planning next year's work schedule and has asked staff to choose one option. Option A: staff work from home three days a week. Option B: staff work in the office every day, and the company provides a free lunch. Choose one option and explain why.

> I would choose Option A, working from home three days a week, because it would give me back time and help me do better work. My main reason is the time I would save. My commute is about an hour each way on two buses, so three days at home would give me back six hours every week. I could use those hours to exercise before work or help my children with their homework, and I would start each day with more energy. Another reason is focus. Our office is open plan, and phone calls and conversations happen around my desk all day. At home, I can close the door and finish a report in one morning instead of two. My work would be faster and more accurate. I understand that a free lunch is attractive, and seeing colleagues every day builds team spirit. However, two office days a week are enough for meetings and social time, and the hours I would save at home are worth far more to me than a free sandwich.

Why it works. The choice is in the first sentence. Two separate reasons, each with a detail from the writer's own life (two buses, six hours a week, an open plan office). The last paragraph is fair to Option B and still explains why A is better. Polite and plain, not an essay. (175 words.)

### How should you split your time on CELPIP Writing?

CELPIP's Writing Pro: Target 9+ Study Pack suggests about 4 to 5 minutes of planning for each response and about 3 minutes of checking once you finish. Here is how we teach it. Neither split is official, and both sit close to that advice.

Task 1 (27 minutes)


| Phase | Time | What to do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Read and plan | 4 minutes | Decide the tone, note every part of the prompt, order your paragraphs |
| Write | 20 minutes | One paragraph per part of the prompt |
| Check | 3 minutes | Grammar, spelling, tone, every part answered |

Task 2 (26 minutes)


| Phase | Time | What to do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Read and plan | 3 minutes | Choose your side, note two reasons and their details |
| Write | 18 minutes | Choice, reason 1, reason 2, the other option and your conclusion |
| Check | 5 minutes | Word count, reread twice, fix the two worst problems |

The Task 2 template explains why we keep Task 2 planning short and the check long.

If you are running out of time: write one closing sentence now, so the response has an ending. In Task 2, a short note on the other option is better than none, but two developed reasons matter more than a rushed third paragraph.

### Which grammar mistakes cost the most points?

These are the errors we see most often in student answers. They all live in Readability, so fixing them pays off on both tasks.


| Grammar trap | Wrong | Right |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Missing articles | "I went to store" | "I went to the store" |
| Subject and verb agreement | "He go to work" | "He goes to work" |
| Tense shifting | "I walked in and I see the damage" | "I walked in and I saw the damage" |
| Run-on sentences | "The noise is loud I can't sleep" | "The noise is loud, and I can't sleep" |
| Wrong prepositions | "Interested for the job" | "Interested in the job" |
| Comma splices | "I tried calling, nobody answered" | "I tried calling, but nobody answered" |
| Double negatives | "I don't have no complaints" | "I don't have any complaints" |
| Wrong word form | "I am very satisfaction" | "I am very satisfied" |

A quick checking trick: read your answer from the last sentence to the first. You stop reading for meaning and start seeing each sentence on its own, so errors stand out. For the full list of grammar points and how they affect your level, see our CELPIP grammar guide .

### Does CELPIP Writing have spell check, and how many words should you write?

Yes, there is a spell check. CELPIP's Guidebook names a spell check among the tools on the Writing screen, along with a personal timer and a word counter. It is a spelling tool, not a grammar tool: it cannot tell you that "their" should be "there" or that a verb doesn't agree with its subject. CELPIP's own writing advice also points out that the spell check can suggest several words, and picking the right one is not always easy, so know your own spelling of common words. More detail, including retakes, is in our guide to CELPIP Writing spell check and retake rules .

Word count: 150 to 200 for each task. CELPIP says there is a little room to be under or over, and that your score may suffer if you are significantly outside the range. It publishes no exact buffer. We teach students to aim for 175 to 200 words: enough room to develop every idea, without running out of time. Watch the word counter on screen as you write.

### How should you practise CELPIP Writing?

Daily (15 to 20 minutes)

- Write one email or survey answer under a timer. Use the free first question in the Writing Task 1 sample test and the Writing Task 2 sample test .
- Check your answer against the four dimensions above. Be honest about where you lost marks.
- Read one article from a Canadian news site and notice how the writer opens paragraphs and moves between ideas.
- Build your word bank with the CELPIP word lists . A bigger word bank means more precise details.
Weekly

- Write both tasks back to back under full timed conditions. No pausing, no looking things up.
- Ask a friend or a teacher three questions: does the tone match the reader, is every part of the prompt answered, which mistake repeats?
- Keep a list of your repeat grammar errors and drill those.
- Keep Listening, Reading and Speaking going too. Writing points don't help if another skill falls below your target.
The week before the test

- Don't learn new grammar rules. Review the ones you know.
- Write two or three timed answers and focus on your planning, not just the writing.
- Make sure you can type comfortably for 20 minutes at a time. If you type slowly, the clock is a real problem.
- Read the test day checklist so nothing surprises you.

### Frequently Asked Questions

#### Does CELPIP Writing have a spell checker?

Yes. The test has a spell check, a word counter and a personal timer. The spell check is for spelling only. It will not catch a correctly spelled wrong word ("their" for "there"), a missing word or a grammar error, so you still need to read your answer yourself.

#### What is the ideal word count for CELPIP Writing?

The range is 150 to 200 words for each task. CELPIP allows a little room either side but says your score may suffer if you are significantly over or under, and it publishes no exact buffer. We recommend 175 to 200 words, which gives you room to develop each idea.

#### How is the CELPIP Writing section scored?

Trained raters score your writing on four dimensions: Content/Coherence, Vocabulary, Readability and Task Fulfillment. Your result is a CELPIP level from 0 to 12, and from level 3 up your CELPIP level is your CLB (Canadian Language Benchmark) level, so CELPIP 9 is CLB 9. CELPIP publishes the dimensions but not the formula that combines them. See our CELPIP scores and CLB guide for what each level means for immigration.

#### Can I use contractions in CELPIP Writing?

Match the reader. In an email to someone you know, a few contractions like "I'm" and "don't" sound natural. In an email to a manager, a company or an office, and in Task 2, full forms ("I am", "do not") are the safe choice. Whatever you choose, be consistent.

#### What happens if I don't finish CELPIP Writing in time?

Treat the timer as a hard stop and plan to finish early. An answer without an ending looks incomplete, so if time is short, write one closing sentence before anything else. A finished answer with a short ending is better than a long one that stops in the middle of an idea.

#### Should I mention the other option in Task 2?

Yes, briefly. CELPIP's Target 9+ advice for Task 2 includes comparing your choice with the option you did not pick. One or two sentences are enough, for example: "While office work offers more time with colleagues, the hours saved at home matter more to me."

#### How can I improve my CELPIP Writing score quickly?

Fix your repeat grammar errors and never skip the planning and checking steps. Most lost points come from avoidable errors (missing articles, tense shifts, run-on sentences) and from organization (a missed part of the prompt, no clear structure). About 4 minutes of planning and 3 of checking catch most of them. Practise with timed tasks on CELTESTPIP Writing practice to build the habit.
