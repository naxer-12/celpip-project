## Answer Engineering: Eliminate Like a Pro

The wrong options in CELPIP Reading are not random. Most of them are almost right. They use real words from the text, they talk about the same topic, and they sound reasonable.

That is why "it sounds right" is not enough. This lesson gives you seven ways a wrong option is built, and one quick check for each.

## Core Reading Skills

You do not need to read every word of every text in CELPIP Reading. You need to know which kind of reading each question asks for, and how to do it quickly.

CELPIP's study pack names the skills. This lesson shows how to use each one.

## Part 1 Mastery: Reading Correspondence

Part 1 gives you one message and asks you to do two different jobs with it. First you answer questions about the message. Then you complete a reply to it.

Most people practise the first job. The second job is worth almost half of the points, and it needs a different way of reading.

## Part 3 Mastery: Reading for Information

Part 3 gives you one text in four paragraphs and nine statements. For each statement you decide which paragraph says it. If no paragraph says it, the answer is E.

That sounds simple. The difficulty is that the text almost never uses the same words as the statement, and some statements are true in real life but are still not in the text.

## Pacing & Time Control (Minute-by-Minute Plans)

Most pacing advice forgets one fact: in CELPIP Reading, time never moves from one part to another. You cannot finish Part 1 early and spend the extra minutes on Part 4.

So pacing is not one big plan. It is four small plans, one for each clock.

## Paraphrase & Vocabulary Toolkit for Reading

Paraphrase means saying the same idea with different words.

And here is the fact that shapes this whole lesson: on CELPIP, the answer options are almost never written in the same words as the text . Not the right one, and not the wrong ones either.

So in every part, you are matching ideas, not words. This page gives you the word swaps to recognise, the ones to distrust, and one check that decides.

---

## Part 4 Mastery: Reading for Viewpoints

Part 4 is the longest Reading part, and it is mostly about people. Several people give their opinion on one issue. Your job is to remember who said what, why they said it, and how strongly they meant it.

Get the notes right, and most of the ten questions answer themselves.

### What are the 5 traps in CELPIP Reading Part 2?

#### The format: a picture, an email with 5 blanks, 3 questions

First, the format of CELPIP Reading Part 2. There is a picture and an email from one person to another. There are five blanks in the email, and you fill them using the data in the picture and the clues in the email. After the email there are three more questions, which are about what you understood from the email.

#### Every question is 1 point, so answer them all

CELPIP says every question in each Reading part is worth one point: a correct answer gets 1 point and a wrong answer gets 0. There are no negative points and no half points. I explain this in my video on CELPIP Reading scoring. Because there are no negative points, answer every question. Try your best to find the answer, and if you can't find it, guess. You might be lucky.

#### The one move: a clue in the email, a fact in the picture

Here is how to answer Reading Part 2: find a clue in the email, find a fact in the picture, and connect the clue to the fact. Part 2 has two pieces and you go back and forth between them, so first find the clue in the email, then the fact in the picture. The answer sits where the clue and the fact meet.

#### Your first minute: the picture, then the email

When you open a Part 2 question, check the picture for 30 seconds and read the email for 30 seconds. In the picture, look for the titles and the important parts. Here, the picture is about bike rental: four different shops across the city, with their website, location and prices. The small notes are sometimes very important.

#### You cannot mark the screen on test day

Be careful: in the exam you cannot mark the screen like I'm doing here. That is just for teaching. In the exam you write notes on the paper they give you at the test centre, so learn to write what I highlight as notes.

In the email, the receiver is Derek and Tom is sending it. It says: "Glad you're heading downtown with Lisa and Jake this Saturday and Sunday." Names like Lisa and Jake can be important when you answer.

#### Trap 1: a rule

For the first blank, read the sentence before it: "Jake is only 14", so age can be important. After the blank: "so he won't be able to rent from Wheelhouse." Before reading the options, find the fact in the picture. Under Wheelhouse Bikes it says "must be 16+ to rent, photo ID required". So the answer is the minimum age requirement. The other options (they don't carry bikes small enough for teenagers, a certified technician must approve reservations) all sound like things a bike shop could say, but none of them is in the picture. Choose only what you can find in the picture.

So the first trap is rules. Most rules are printed in the picture, and the email talks about them. They come with words like must, could and should, and the strength of the rule affects the answer.

#### Trap 2: a place

Blank 2 says: "Speaking of Pedal City, I noticed they're right beside ___, which is convenient if you are coming by boat." There is no boat in the picture. But it's about Pedal City, so the fact is under Pedal City, and the address says Harbour Road, next to the ferry terminal. Boat and ferry have the same meaning in different words. This is paraphrasing, and it happens a lot in Reading Part 2. The options are a GreenLane docking station, the seawall bike path, and the ferry terminal. The answer is the ferry terminal. So the second trap is a place: sometimes they use the exact name, and sometimes a synonym.

#### Trap 3: a price

The third trap is price, and many students get it wrong because it needs a little maths: adding, dividing, finding a discount. Question 3 says: "If you and Lisa want bikes for both days, the weekend bundle at Wheelhouse actually saves you something compared to paying for two separate full days at Pedal City." So we compare two shops. The Wheelhouse weekend package is $85. A standard bike at Pedal City is $45 a day, so two days is $90. The saving is $5 per person.

Where does the $15 option come from? If you compare the Wheelhouse weekend package with two Wheelhouse full days ($50 twice is $100, minus $85), you get $15. But that is not the comparison the email asks for. Compare the Wheelhouse weekend package with Pedal City's standard bike, as the question says. Be honest in the comments: did the $15 option get you?

#### Trap 4: a need

Blank 4 says: "Lisa mentioned she just wants to take it easy and soak in the waterfront scenery without having to figure out routes." So Lisa needs a guide. In the picture, Coastline Tours and Rentals has a bike rental plus a guided waterfront tour, and one option is a guided ride along the waterfront. Another option, the GreenLane Day Pass, is cheaper at $14, but it is not what Lisa needs. She wants to relax without planning routes, so the answer is the guided ride.

#### Trap 5: a missing thing

The fifth trap is my favourite, because the answer is something that is not in the picture. Blank 5 says: "By the way, I wanted to call Wheelhouse to confirm what time they close, but ___, so you'll have to look it up online instead." Go back to Wheelhouse Bikes: it has everything except a phone number. Pedal City has a phone number, GreenLane has an email, Coastline has a phone number, but Wheelhouse only has a website. The options are: their shop was already closed; they only accept inquiries by email; there is no phone number listed for them; their website says to visit in person. The answer is that there is no phone number listed. Why did Tom want to call? The note at the bottom of the picture says most rental shops close by 7 PM, so confirm hours before visiting.

#### The last 3 questions

We answered the first five by checking between the clue and the fact. For the last three, you sometimes go back to the email or the picture, and often they ask what you understood from the whole situation.

Question 6: what is the main purpose of Tom's email? We already know the story, so it's easy: to help Derek plan bike rentals for his family's weekend trip. Not to persuade Derek to join a guided cycle tour, not to compare all four shops, and not to recommend the single cheapest option, because he suggested different options and some were not cheap.

Question 7: what does Tom call the little freebie included with the Wheelhouse weekend deal? Go back to Wheelhouse Bikes: free water bottle with every weekend package. The other options (a complimentary helmet, a free city bike map) appear at other shops, but we only focus on this fact.

Question 8: why does Tom suggest the afternoon departure for Lisa? This is a suggestion, not in the picture, so go back to the email: "she could join the afternoon group while you and Jake ride around on your own." So the answer is that it lets Derek and Jake ride on their own while Lisa is on the tour.

The CELPIP Reading test is scored out of 38 questions. Your number of correct answers converts to a CELPIP level from 0 to 12, and that level equals your CLB level on a 1:1 basis; a CELPIP 9 is CLB 9. As a rough guide, 31–33 correct maps to Level 9, and 24–28 correct maps to Level 7, the usual Express Entry minimum. The full chart is below.

Your CELPIP Reading result looks simple. A single number from 0 to 12; but that number decides your CLB level, your CRS points, and whether you clear the bar for Express Entry, a PNP, or the program you're applying to. This page shows exactly how your raw score out of 38 becomes that level, what each level means, and the score you actually need.

### What is the CELPIP Reading score chart?

The CELPIP Reading score chart maps the number of questions you answered correctly (your raw score, out of 38) to a CELPIP Reading level from 0 to 12. That level then equals the same Canadian Language Benchmark (CLB) level with no extra conversion: CELPIP 7 is CLB 7, CELPIP 9 is CLB 9.

Here is the approximate raw-score-to-level chart for CELPIP Reading:


| Raw score (correct out of 38) | CELPIP Reading level | CLB level | What it signals |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 33–38 | 10, 11, 12 | CLB 10+ | Advanced; maximum CRS language points |
| 31–33 | 9 | CLB 9 | Strong; competitive for most Express Entry draws |
| 28–31 | 8 | CLB 8 | Good comprehension of detailed passages |
| 24–28 | 7 | CLB 7 | Meets the common FSW / CEC minimum |
| 19–25 | 6 | CLB 6 | Below the FSW minimum |
| 15–20 | 5 | CLB 5 | Basic professional reading |
| 10–16 | 4 | CLB 4 | Emerging; limited ability |
| 8–11 | 3 | CLB 3 | Below most immigration minimums |
| 0–7 | 0 to 2 | Below CLB 3 | Beginner |

Taking the Listening section too? Its scale works the same way; see the companion CELPIP Listening score chart .

Why the ranges overlap: Prometric (which owns CELPIP; it acquired the test's maker, Paragon Testing Enterprises, in 2021) "equates" every test form, meaning a slightly harder version needs a few less correct answers for the same level. So these bands are approximate; the same raw score can land one level higher or lower depending on your test version. Treat them as a close guide, not a guarantee. (Source: Paragon Testing Enterprises, official CELPIP Reading raw-score and level information.)

### How many questions can you miss in CELPIP Reading?

For Level 7 you can miss about 10 to 14 of the 38 questions, and for Level 9 about 5 to 7. Here is the same chart turned around, so you can see how many wrong answers each level allows.


| CELPIP Reading level | Correct answers (out of 38) | Questions you can miss |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 10 to 12 | 33 to 38 | 0 to 5 |
| 9 | 31 to 33 | 5 to 7 |
| 8 | 28 to 31 | 7 to 10 |
| 7 | 24 to 28 | 10 to 14 |
| 6 | 19 to 25 | 13 to 19 |
| 5 | 15 to 20 | 18 to 23 |

Because the ranges overlap, aim for the top of the range you need, not the bottom. If your target is Level 7, plan for 28 correct in practice. If it is Level 9, plan for 33. That buffer covers a harder test version and an off day.

### How is the CELPIP Reading raw score (out of 38) calculated?

Your raw score is simply the number of the 38 Reading questions you answered correctly. You get one point per correct answer, and there is no penalty for wrong answers , so you should answer every question, even when you're guessing.

The Reading test has four parts : reading correspondence (emails and messages), reading to apply a diagram, reading for information, and reading for viewpoints. All four feed into the same 38-question raw score, which is then converted to your CELPIP level using the equated chart above. Because there's no negative marking, leaving a question blank only costs you. In other words, a guess has upside and no downside. (If any one part reliably trips you up, our CELPIP Reading tips and strategies break each part down.)

### What CLB level does each CELPIP Reading score give?

Every CELPIP level equals the same CLB level, one to one. This is the reason many candidates find CELPIP simpler to interpret than IELTS : there's no separate conversion table to read.


| CELPIP Reading level | CLB level | Proficiency |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 10, 11, 12 | CLB 10+ | Advanced to expert |
| 9 | CLB 9 | Effective: interprets tone and implied meaning |
| 8 | CLB 8 | Strong: detailed and paraphrased information |
| 7 | CLB 7 | Adequate: main ideas and supporting detail |
| 6 | CLB 6 | Developing: moderately complex texts |
| 5 | CLB 5 | Emerging: familiar, straightforward passages |
| 4 | CLB 4 | Basic: simple, explicit content |
| 3 | CLB 3 | Minimal |
| 0 to 2 | Below CLB 3 | Beginner |

Note that CELPIP levels 10, 11, and 12 are grouped as a single band on the Listening and Reading scale, and all count as CLB 10+ in IRCC's equivalency tables. (Source: IRCC CLB equivalency tables; Paragon Testing.)

### What CELPIP Reading score do I need for Express Entry and PR?

For most Express Entry programs you need CELPIP Reading 7 (CLB 7) . That's the minimum for the Federal Skilled Worker Program and for Canadian Experience Class applicants in TEER 0 or 1 jobs. Some paths are lower: the Federal Skilled Trades Program accepts CLB 4 in Reading, and CEC TEER 2/3 jobs need CLB 5. (See the full minimum CELPIP scores for every immigration program .)

Remember that IRCC assesses each skill independently . For example, a high Listening score can't rescue a low Reading score. You have to clear the minimum in Reading on its own. And because language is worth up to 136 CRS points (the single heaviest factor), moving your Reading from CLB 7 to CLB 9 doesn't just clear the bar, it can add the points that turn a profile in the pool into an Invitation to Apply. ### What does each CELPIP Reading level mean?

Beyond the number, each level describes what you can actually do with written English:

- Levels 10–12: You accurately interpret complex passages, implied meaning, tone, and detailed arguments; near-native reading.
- Level 9: You interpret viewpoints, implied meaning, and complex arguments with high accuracy.
- Level 8: You understand detailed passages and paraphrased information reliably.
- Level 7: You grasp main ideas, supporting details, and common workplace or immigration texts.
- Level 6: You handle moderately complex content with reasonable accuracy.
- Level 5: You understand familiar, straightforward passages.
- Level 4: You read simple, explicit content.
- Levels 0 to 3: limited to minimal comprehension, below immigration minimums. A 2 corresponds to CLB 1 to 2; 0 or 1 means not enough to assess.
If you're sitting a level or two below your target, the gap is usually specific: misreading viewpoint questions, or running out of time on Part 3. Working through questions in the real test format in the Reading question bank shows you which of the four parts is costing you the level, instead of leaving you to guess.

### Is the CELPIP General reading score chart different?

No; there is only one Reading scoring scale, and it's the same chart whether people call it the "CELPIP General reading score chart" or just the "CELPIP reading score chart." The important distinction is which test type you take:

- CELPIP-General tests all four skills (Listening, Reading, Writing, Speaking) and is what you use for PR and Express Entry. It gives you a Reading score.
- CELPIP-General LS tests only Listening and Speaking and is used for Canadian citizenship . It has no Reading section , so it produces no Reading score or Reading CLB.
So if you need a Reading score, you need CELPIP-General. If you're only applying for citizenship, Reading doesn't apply to you at all.

### How long is a CELPIP Reading score valid?

Your CELPIP scores, Reading included, are valid for two years from your test date for immigration purposes. IRCC needs a valid score both when you create your Express Entry profile and when you submit your PR application. If your score expires while your profile is active, the profile becomes incomplete and can't be drawn. Official results are usually released within a few business days through your CELPIP account. Paragon's own pages currently quote different turnarounds, so check the figure shown when you book.

### Frequently Asked Questions

#### Is there a CELPIP Reading score chart PDF?

#### What is 33 out of 38 in CELPIP reading?

33 correct out of 38 is a Level 9 or a Level 10 result. On the approximate chart, 33 is the top of the Level 9 band (31 to 33) and the bottom of the 10 to 12 band (33 to 38), and which one you get depends on the version of the test you took, because Prometric equates every form. Either way you are at CLB 9 or above, well past the CLB 7 minimum for Express Entry, and into the levels that add real CRS points.

#### What is 31 out of 38 in CELPIP reading?

31 correct is a Level 8 or a Level 9 result, which means CLB 8 or CLB 9. On the chart, 31 sits at the top of the Level 8 band (28 to 31) and the bottom of the Level 9 band (31 to 33). The exact level depends on your test version. Both clear the CLB 7 Express Entry minimum; the difference between them is CRS points, not eligibility. If you are practising and landing on 31, one more correct answer in Part 3 or Part 4 is usually what turns it into a 9.

#### What is a good CELPIP reading score?

For immigration, a good CELPIP Reading score is 7 or higher, and 9 or higher if you want the score to work for you in the CRS. Level 7 (about 24 to 28 correct) is the minimum for the Federal Skilled Worker Program and for Canadian Experience Class in TEER 0 or 1 jobs. Level 9 (about 31 to 33 correct) is where the CRS language points rise sharply, and Level 10 or above earns the maximum. What counts as "good" is set by the program you are applying to, so check the minimum CELPIP scores for your program before you decide on a target.

#### How to calculate reading score in CELPIP?

Count your correct answers. The Reading test has 38 questions, one point each, and there is no penalty for a wrong answer, so your raw score is simply the number you got right out of 38. Prometric then converts that raw score to a CELPIP level from 0 to 12 using an equated chart, and the level is your CLB level. You cannot calculate the level exactly yourself, because the conversion shifts slightly from one test version to the next, but the chart above tells you the band you are in.

#### What is a CELPIP reading score of 6?

A CELPIP Reading score of 6 means CLB 6, which is roughly 19 to 25 correct answers out of 38. At this level you handle moderately complex texts with reasonable accuracy. It is below the CLB 7 minimum for the Federal Skilled Worker Program and for CEC applicants in TEER 0 or 1 jobs, but it meets the CLB 5 Reading minimum for CEC applicants in TEER 2 or 3 jobs and the CLB 4 Reading minimum for the Federal Skilled Trades Program. IRCC checks each skill on its own, so a 6 in Reading is not offset by a higher score elsewhere.

#### Is it hard to get CELPIP 7?

In Reading, a 7 means about 24 to 28 correct out of 38, so you need roughly two thirds of the questions right. For a test taker who already reads English at work or at school, that is a realistic target with a few weeks of timed practice. The questions that decide it are usually in Part 3 (matching statements to paragraphs) and Part 4 (viewpoints), where the answer is paraphrased rather than copied from the text. Practise those two parts under time and the 7 tends to follow.

## CELPIP Reading Time Management: Minutes per Part (2026)

CELPIP Reading gives about 11, 9, 10 and 13 minutes per part, each on its own clock, and you cannot go back. Here is how to pace all four and finish.

CELPIP Reading gives you 43 to 56 minutes for 38 scored questions in 4 parts, and each part runs on its own clock: about 11, 9, 10 and 13 minutes. When a part's time ends, the test moves you on and you cannot go back. So time management means finishing each part inside its own limit.

That one fact changes how you should practise. In my experience, most people who miss their Reading target did not run out of English; they ran out of time in one part and carried the panic into the next. Below is the official timing for each part, where the minutes actually go, and the pacing I teach for Parts 1 to 4.

Every part has a free sample test on the real clock. Start a free CELPIP Reading practice test , and time yourself before you read any further.

### How much time do you get in CELPIP Reading?

About 43 minutes of scored reading, inside a section that CELPIP times at 43 to 56 minutes. CELPIP's Reading Pro Study Pack gives this breakdown, and labels both columns "approximate":


| Part | Questions (approximate) | Time (approximate) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Part 1: Reading Correspondence | 11 | 11 minutes |
| Part 2: Reading to Apply a Diagram | 8 | 9 minutes |
| Part 3: Reading for Information | 9 | 10 minutes |
| Part 4: Reading for Viewpoints | 10 | 13 minutes |
| Sum of the four parts (our total, not printed by CELPIP) | 38 | 43 minutes |

Why does the official Guidebook say 43 to 56 minutes for the section? Because the Reading test can contain unscored questions used for test development. CELPIP's study pack says there may be one unscored part, that you will not be told which one it is, and that you should do your best on every part. An extra part is where the other 13 minutes can come from. You will not be told which part it was, so treat every part as if it counts.

Two more official details matter for pacing. Each correct answer is worth one point, and points are not deducted for wrong answers, so a guess is always better than a blank. And the passages and questions get harder from Part 1 to Part 4, which is why Part 4 gets the most time.

### Why can't you carry time from one part to the next?

Because each part has its own timer, and the test moves you forward when it runs out. CELPIP's study pack explains the rule. Inside a Reading part, you can change any answer as often as you want until the time runs out. Then the test moves you to the next part on its own, and there is no way back.

In practice that means three things:

- Finishing Part 1 in 8 minutes does not give you 3 extra minutes for Part 4. Spare time in a part is only useful for checking that part.
- A question you leave blank in Part 2 is gone once Part 3 starts. Answer everything before the clock ends, even if some answers are guesses.
- The text and all its questions sit on one screen, and you scroll between them. You get paper and a pen for notes, but you cannot highlight or write on the screen.
So the skill is not "manage 50 minutes". It is "manage 11 minutes, then 9, then 10, then 13", four times in a row, with no rollover.

### Where does the time actually go?

Usually into reading the passage twice and getting stuck on one question. In my experience, these are the four leaks:

- Reading the whole passage carefully before looking at a single question. You then read it again to find each answer.
- Re-reading a question three times because the options look alike. CELPIP distractors are built to look right.
- Spending two minutes on one hard question and rushing the last four easy ones.
- Checking answers you were already sure about instead of the two you doubted.
Every one of these is a habit, which is good news, because habits change with a few timed sets. The fixes are below, part by part.

### How should you pace Part 1: Reading Correspondence (about 11 minutes)?

Preview the first question set, read the email once, answer, then handle the reply. Part 1 is an email from one community member to another about an everyday social or work situation, with questions in two groups: questions on the email, then a short response with gaps to fill in. My split for the 11 minutes:


| Minute | What you do |
| --- | --- |
| 0 to 1 | Skim the first question set so you know what to look for |
| 1 to 4 | Read the email once, properly, noting who is writing to whom and why |
| 4 to 7 | Answer the first set, going back to the exact line for each |
| 7 to 10 | Read the reply and fill the gaps, using the original email to check facts |
| 10 to 11 | Check only the answers you were unsure about |

The reply gaps are the trap. They test whether you understood the first email, so each gap is answered by something in the original message, not by what sounds polite. Our Reading Part 1 vocabulary and tone guide covers the formal and informal wording these emails use.

### How should you pace Part 2: Reading to Apply a Diagram (about 9 minutes)?

Read the diagram first, then the message, then answer the gaps from the diagram. Part 2 gives you a diagram with point-form text and visuals (a transit schedule, an event flyer, a guide to a community service) and a short email about it: five gaps to fill in, then three more questions. Nine minutes for 8 questions sounds generous and is not, because people read the message twice.


| Minute | What you do |
| --- | --- |
| 0 to 2 | Study the diagram: headings, numbers, dates, prices, labels |
| 2 to 4 | Read the message once and underline, on your paper, every detail it takes from the diagram |
| 4 to 7 | Fill the five gaps in the email, checking each one against the diagram, not the message |
| 7 to 8 | Answer the last three questions |
| 8 to 9 | Check the two you doubted |

Most wrong answers in Part 2 come from choosing the option that fits the sentence grammatically instead of the one that matches the diagram. The diagram is the answer key.

### How should you pace Part 3: Reading for Information (about 10 minutes)?

Read the questions first, then read the text one paragraph at a time and answer as you go. Part 3 is a four-paragraph informational text about a person, place, event or process. Each question is a statement you match to the paragraph that contains it, or to the "not stated" option if no paragraph does. Ten minutes for 9 questions.


| Minute | What you do |
| --- | --- |
| 0 to 1 | Read all the statements, and circle the keywords on paper |
| 1 to 6 | Read the text one paragraph at a time; after each paragraph, match any statement it contains |
| 6 to 9 | Go back for the statements still open, scanning for their keywords; if a statement is in no paragraph, choose "not stated" |
| 9 to 10 | Check the two you doubted |

The time leak in Part 3 is searching the whole text for every statement, and searching twice for one that is not there. Give any statement one careful search, then decide.

### How should you pace Part 4: Reading for Viewpoints (about 13 minutes)?

Split the time: about 7 minutes for the article and its questions, about 6 for the comment. Part 4 is the hardest text: an article with two or more opinions about a community issue, followed by a short reader comment with gaps to fill in. Ten questions, split five and five.


| Minute | What you do |
| --- | --- |
| 0 to 1 | Skim the first five questions: they tell you whose opinion to track |
| 1 to 5 | Read the article, noting on paper which person holds which view |
| 5 to 7 | Answer the first five |
| 7 to 11 | Read the comment and fill its gaps, checking each against the article's views |
| 11 to 13 | Check the ones you doubted, and make sure nothing is blank |

Part 4 is where people arrive tired and behind, which is exactly why it has the most time. If you have paced Parts 1 to 3, you walk into Part 4 with your full 13 minutes and a clear head. If you want the strategy for the opinion-matching questions themselves, the CELPIP Reading tips and strategies guide goes deeper on each part.

### What do you do when you are running out of time?

Answer every question, then use what is left on the ones you doubted. Three rules I give every student:

- The 60-second rule. If you have spent a minute on a question and you are no closer, choose your best option, note the number on your paper, and move on. Come back only if the part's clock allows it.
- Never leave a blank. Wrong answers cost nothing, so a guess with a 25 percent chance beats a blank with none. In the last 30 seconds of any part, fill every gap.
- Watch the clock at the halfway point, not the end. In an 11-minute part, if you are not halfway through the questions at minute 6, speed up now, not at minute 10.
And one rule for after the part ends: let it go. You cannot return to it, and the next part has its own clock. Thinking about a Part 2 question during Part 3 costs you Part 3.

### What are the three CELPIP Reading question types?

Every Reading question is one of three types, and CELPIP says the same three appear on the Listening Test:

- General meaning. A big picture question. You put together ideas from several sentences, a paragraph, or the whole text. It often looks like "The article is mainly about..." or "The main idea of the third paragraph is..."
- Specific information. A close up question. You find one fact, such as a date, a name, a place or a fact, in one sentence of the text or the diagram.
- Inference. The answer is not written in the text. You use what is there to reach a conclusion. These questions often say "probably" or "most likely", or ask about the tone or attitude of the writer.
Why it helps: once you know the type, you know where to look. General meaning means skim the whole passage. Specific information means scan for one word. Inference means read the sentences around the key idea and ask what the writer would agree with.

### CELPIP Reading Strategy 1: Skim First, Then Scan

This is the single most important CELPIP Reading strategy, and it applies to every part.

Most test takers make this mistake: they read the entire passage word by word from top to bottom, then look at the questions. By the time they finish reading, they've forgotten half of what they read and have to re-read anyway.

Do this instead:

- Skim the passage first (60-90 seconds). Read the first sentence of each paragraph, glance at any headings, bold text, or key names. Your goal isn't understanding, it's building a mental map of where information lives.
- Read the first question. Identify what it's asking for: a fact, an opinion, a purpose, a detail.
- Scan back to the relevant section and find the answer. Your mental map tells you roughly where to look.
- Answer and move to the next question.
This approach is faster because you only read each section deeply when you need to, not the entire passage upfront.

> Think of it this way: When you look something up on a restaurant menu, you don't read every item from appetizers to desserts. You scan for the section you want, then read the options in that section. CELPIP Reading works the same way.

### Part 1: Reading Correspondence

What it is: You'll read an email, letter, or message exchange (usually two pieces: an original and a reply). Then answer 11 questions about the content, tone, and purpose.

Why people struggle: The correspondence looks simple, but questions often test implied meaning ; what the writer meant but didn't say directly. "I appreciate your enthusiasm, but perhaps we should consider other options" doesn't mean the writer appreciates anything, it means they disagree.

#### Strategies for Part 1

- Identify the relationship between the writers first. Are they coworkers? Friends? A customer and a business? This immediately tells you what tone to expect and helps you interpret vague language
- Pay attention to the purpose of each message. The original message might request something, and the reply might accept, decline, or propose an alternative. Questions often ask about the overall purpose
- Look for polite disagreement. In professional correspondence, people rarely say "no" directly. Phrases like "I see your point, however..." , "That's one option, but have you considered..." , and "I'm not sure that would work because..." all mean some form of disagreement
- Track specific details. Dates, times, locations, and names mentioned in the correspondence are common question targets. As you skim, mentally note these facts
#### Example Question Types


| Question Type | What They're Really Asking |
| --- | --- |
| "What is the purpose of the first email?" | Why did the writer send this? |
| "What does the writer mean by...?" | Interpret implied meaning |
| "What will the recipient likely do next?" | Infer the next action from context |
| "The word '___' is closest in meaning to..." | Vocabulary in context |

### Part 2: Reading to Apply a Diagram

What it is: You'll see a diagram, chart, map, schedule, or visual organizer alongside a text passage. You need to connect information between the visual and the text to answer 8 questions.

Why people struggle: Most test takers read the text and ignore the diagram (or vice versa). The questions require you to cross-reference both , because the answer might be in the text, the diagram, or a combination.

#### Strategies for Part 2

- Study the diagram first (30-45 seconds). Understand what it shows: categories, flow, hierarchy, schedule, map layout. Read all labels, headings, and any legend
- Then skim the text. The text usually explains or expands on what the diagram shows. Note where the text adds information that the diagram alone doesn't provide
- For each question, check BOTH sources. Some answers are only in the diagram. Some are only in the text. Some require connecting a fact from the text to a position in the diagram
- Pay attention to conditions and exceptions. Diagrams often show a general process, while the text mentions special cases: "Except for employees in department C..." or "This applies only during peak hours." Questions love testing these exceptions

| Diagram Type | What to Focus On |

| Schedule/Timetable | Days, times, overlapping events |
| Map or floor plan | Locations, directions, relative positions |

### Part 3: Reading for Information

What it is: A single informational text divided into 4 labeled paragraphs (usually A through D). You'll answer 9 questions, many of which ask you to identify which paragraph contains a specific piece of information .

Why people struggle: The "which paragraph" format is unique to this part. You can't just find the right answer. You need to find the right answer AND know which paragraph it came from. If you didn't build a mental map during your skim, you'll waste time re-reading everything.

#### Strategies for Part 3

- Label each paragraph during your skim. As you read the first 1-2 sentences of paragraphs A through D, jot a 2-3 word summary on your noteboard: "A = history, B = benefits, C = challenges, D = future plans." This takes 60 seconds and saves you minutes later
- The questions do not follow the passage order. Any statement can belong to any paragraph, so match each one by its content, not by its position in the list
- Watch for paraphrasing. The passage might say "the initiative was launched in 2019" while the question says "when the program began." CELPIP deliberately rephrases, so look for meaning matches , not exact word matches
- "All of the following are mentioned EXCEPT" questions require you to verify three true statements and find the one that's false or not mentioned. Check each option against the text and don't rely on memory

| In the Passage | In the Question |
| --- | --- |

### Part 4: Reading for Viewpoints

What it is: You'll read a passage (usually an article or opinion piece) that presents multiple viewpoints on a topic, sometimes followed by a reader comment or editorial response. 10 questions: the most in any Reading part, and typically the hardest.

Why people struggle: Keeping track of who believes what. When three different people express opinions in the same passage, and the questions ask "Who would most likely agree with the statement...?", you need to have tracked each person's position accurately.

#### Strategies for Part 4

- Identify each person/source and their stance immediately. As you read, note on your noteboard: "Expert A = supports, Expert B = against, Reader = mixed." This is the single most useful thing you can do for Part 4
- Pay attention to the strength of opinions. There's a difference between "strongly disagrees" and "has reservations." Questions often test whether you caught the nuance, not just the direction of the opinion
- The reader comment (if included) often contrasts with the article. If the article presents a positive view, the reader comment frequently offers criticism or a different perspective. This contrast is a question magnet
- "Who would most likely agree/disagree" questions are about matching a new statement to an existing viewpoint. Ask yourself: "Is this consistent with what Person X said?" Don't look for exact quotes. Look for alignment in logic and values
- Don't confuse facts with opinions. The passage might state a fact ("Sales increased by 20%") and then present opinions about that fact ("The CEO attributed this to the new marketing strategy" vs. "Analysts believe it was due to market conditions"). Questions will test whether you can separate the two
#### Part 4 Time Warning

Part 4 has the most questions (10) and the most complex passage. Many test takers rush through it because they spent too much time on earlier parts. If you followed the time budget above, you should have a full 13 minutes here. Don't rush this part . It's worth the same points as the earlier parts and deserves your full attention.

Even with the right strategy, you'll hit questions where you're torn between two options. Here's how to handle them:

#### The Elimination Method

- Cross out obviously wrong answers first. In most questions, 1-2 options are clearly wrong. They contradict the passage or mention things that weren't discussed
- Compare the remaining options against the text. Go back to the specific section and re-read it carefully. One option will usually be a closer match

### How do I improve my CELPIP Reading score?

Three changes, in the order that pays.

First, fix your pacing before you fix anything else. Time the four parts separately on a full section and write down where the minutes actually went. Almost everyone finds the same thing: the first two parts eat time that Part 4 needed. Until that is fixed, more vocabulary will not show up in your score.

Second, practise the part you avoid. Most people rehearse Part 1 because it feels comfortable and finishes quickly. Part 4 is the one that decides the section, and it is the one people do least. Reverse that ratio.

Third, review your wrong answers properly. Not "I should have read more carefully". Write down which distractor you fell for and why it looked right. CELPIP Reading distractors are built to be plausible, and once you can name the trick you stop falling for it. This is slower than doing another test, and it is worth more.

What does not move the needle: reading news articles for general practice, memorising word lists, and doing test after test without reviewing any of them.

### What do I do if I keep running out of time on CELPIP Reading?

Stop finishing the questions you are stuck on. That is almost always the whole problem.

Running out of time is rarely a reading-speed problem. It is a decision problem, and the decision is when to leave a question. Set yourself a hard ceiling of about 90 seconds on any single question. When you hit it, mark your best guess and move on. You can come back within the same part if there is time at the end, and usually there is not, which is exactly the point.

Then rebuild your pacing from the back. Give Part 4 the time it needs first, and let Parts 1 to 3 have what is left. Most people do the opposite without noticing, which is why the hardest part gets whatever is left over.

If it is happening in practice as well as under exam conditions, the problem is upstream: you are reading the whole passage before looking at the questions. Skim the questions first so you know what you are hunting for, then read.

More than people want to hear, and less than they fear.

An honest answer is that we cannot give you a number of hours, and anyone who does is guessing. What we can tell you is the shape of it. People who move up a band usually do it on consistent short sessions over several weeks, not on a heavy weekend. Two or three focused sessions a week, each one a single timed part plus a proper review of the wrong answers, does more than one long session where nothing gets reviewed.

The people who stall are almost always doing volume without review. They finish a practice test, see the score, feel bad or relieved, and start another one. The review is the part that changes the score.

If your test is next week, do not try to build reading skill. Spend the time on pacing and on the Part 4 question types, and accept the level you have.

### CELPIP Reading Practice Plan

#### Daily (15-20 minutes)

- Read one news article from a Canadian source (CBC, Globe and Mail, Toronto Star) and summarize each paragraph in 2-3 words. This builds your skimming speed
- Practice one CELPIP Reading part under timed conditions on CELTESTPIP's Reading section . Alternate between parts throughout the week
- After answering, review every wrong answer and identify the exact line in the passage that contains the correct answer. Train yourself to find evidence
- Build your vocabulary with the CELPIP word lists . Stronger vocabulary means faster reading comprehension
#### Weekly

- Complete a full Reading practice test (all 4 parts) under timed conditions. Try a full mock test to simulate the real experience
- Track your scores by part. If Part 3 is consistently your weakest, give it extra practice
- Time yourself per question. If you're consistently going over 90 seconds on any question, practice the 60-second rule
- Balance your study. Don't neglect Listening , Writing , and Speaking . Your immigration score depends on all four skills.
#### The Week Before the Test

- Do 1-2 full practice tests but don't introduce new strategies. Stick with what you've practiced
- Focus on time management , not perfection. Your goal is to answer every question, not to get every question right
- Rest your eyes. If you've been reading on screens for hours, take breaks. Tired eyes slow you down more than you think
- Read through the test day checklist so you know exactly what to expect
Reading strategy only becomes a score when you run it against the clock. Practise the four parts one at a time, with the timer on, at CELPIP Reading practice , then come back to the review method above and work through what you got wrong.

### Frequently Asked Questions

#### Can I go back to previous questions in the CELPIP Reading section?

Yes, but only within the same part. You can move back and forth between questions in Part 1 while Part 1 is active. Once Part 1's time expires, the test moves to Part 2 and you cannot return to any Part 1 questions.

#### How long are the CELPIP Reading passages?

Passage length ranges from 200 to 500 words depending on the part. Correspondence (Part 1) is typically 200-400 words across two messages. The informational text (Part 3) and viewpoints passage (Part 4) are usually 300-500 words each. Part 2 combines a diagram with shorter text.

#### How is the CELPIP Reading section scored?

Your raw score out of 38 converts to a CELPIP level, and that level maps to a Canadian Language Benchmark (CLB). The conversion is not a simple percentage, and the number you need depends on what you are applying for. We keep the full table, including what each raw score band works out to, on our CELPIP Reading score chart .

#### What is the hardest CELPIP Reading part?

Most test takers find Part 4 (Reading for Viewpoints) the hardest because it requires tracking multiple opinions and answering nuanced questions about who believes what. Part 3 is a close second because of the "which paragraph" question format. That said, difficulty is personal. Some people struggle more with Part 2's diagram interpretation.

#### Should I read the CELPIP Reading questions before the passage?

Yes, scan the questions first when possible. In Parts 1 and 2, quickly looking at the first few questions before reading the passage tells you what to focus on. In Parts 3 and 4, skim the passage first to build a mental map, then read questions one at a time and scan back for answers.

#### I always run out of time on CELPIP Reading Part 4. What should I do?

Two fixes: stick to the time budget on earlier parts, and practice Part 4 with a timer. Most people lose time on Part 4 because they either spent too long on Parts 1-3, or they keep re-reading the viewpoints passage. Using your noteboard to label each person's stance during your first read saves significant time when answering questions. See our common CELPIP mistakes guide for more time management fixes.

#### Is the CELPIP Reading vocabulary difficult?

No, CELPIP uses everyday Canadian English, not academic or technical language. You'll encounter workplace, community, and daily life vocabulary. The real challenge is paraphrasing : the test restates ideas using different words, so you need to recognize meaning matches rather than looking for exact word matches.

### What does "Reading Correspondence" mean?

Start with the word, because the word is the problem. Correspondence is a formal English word for something very ordinary: the letters, emails and messages that two people send each other. The Cambridge Dictionary gives two everyday senses: "letters, especially official or business letters" and "the action of writing, receiving, and reading letters, especially between two people." So "reading correspondence" just means reading somebody's messages.

Most of my students have never used the word in their life. You do not say "I checked my correspondence this morning." You say "I checked my email." That is why the phrase stops people when they first see it on a test format page. It sounds like a special kind of reading. It is not. It is the most everyday reading on the whole test.

In CELPIP the phrase is a label. Reading Part 1 is called "Reading Correspondence" because the text you read is one person's email to another. The three parts after it have their own labels for the same reason: Part 2 is Reading to Apply a Diagram, Part 3 is Reading for Information, Part 4 is Reading for Viewpoints. The name tells you what kind of text is coming, nothing more.

### What is CELPIP Reading Part 1?

Part 1 gives you two short texts. The first is the main text: an email between two people in the same community about an ordinary social or work matter. The second is a short reply to that email, with blanks in it, and you choose the missing words from the options offered. That matches how Prometric, the company that runs CELPIP, describes the two texts in its Reading Pro Study Pack.

The situations are deliberately ordinary. Prometric's own examples are baseball team members discussing an upcoming competition, a customer asking a bakery for a graduation cake, a community member emailing an animal shelter about volunteering, and colleagues making travel arrangements for a conference. Our free practice set is an email about returning a camping trailer. Nobody is writing about economics or philosophy here.

The tone is ordinary too. Prometric describes the Part 1 texts as relaxed and friendly, the way people who know each other write, but never extremely informal, so no slang. If you have been told that this part is about formal business letters, that is not what the test maker says. Read the email the way you would read a message from a neighbour or a colleague.

### How many questions is Reading Part 1, and how long is it?

Eleven questions in about eleven minutes, and the split matters more than the total.


|  | Questions | What they test |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The main email | 6 | Purpose, details, tone and inference about the message you read |
| The reply with blanks | 5 | Choosing the words that fit the reply, so it matches the situation in the email |
| Total | 11 | about 11 minutes |

Every question is a choice from four options. Each correct answer is worth one point, and points are not deducted for a wrong answer, so you answer every single question even when you are guessing. The Reading test has 38 scored questions in total across the four parts, and Prometric says the test may contain unscored items anywhere within Reading and that you will not be able to tell them from scored items, which is one more reason to treat every question as real.

Two details from the same source that people miss. First, the reading passages and questions increase in difficulty from Part 1 to Part 4, so Part 1 is where the test is most generous and where careless points hurt most. Second, that "11 minutes" is approximate and the test moves you forward automatically when time is up. You cannot go back to a part you have left.

If you want the scoring side of this, how 38 questions become a level from 0 to 12, it is on our Reading score chart .

### Is "Reading Correspondence" the same as Writing Task 1?

No, and the two get mixed up because both are about emails. Writing Task 1 asks you to write an email of 150 to 200 words in 27 minutes. Reading Part 1 asks you to understand an email someone else wrote, then finish a reply by choosing the missing words. Same kind of text, opposite skill.

The confusion is useful, though. The email in Reading Part 1 is a model of the everyday, friendly register the Writing test also rewards, so reading it closely teaches you something about your own Task 1 emails. If Writing is your weaker skill, the Writing Task 1 email template is the page you want after this one.

### Frequently Asked Questions

#### What does "correspondence" mean in simple terms?

Messages that people send to each other in writing: emails, letters, notes. If you have ever replied to a neighbour's email about a parking spot, you have handled correspondence.

#### What are correspondence examples?

In CELPIP Part 1, an email between two community members about an everyday matter: a team competition, a cake order, a volunteering request, travel plans for a conference. Those are Prometric's own published examples.

#### How many questions are in CELPIP Reading Part 1?

Eleven. Six are about the main email and five are about the short reply with blanks. The time is about 11 minutes.

#### Is Reading Correspondence hard?

It is the first of four parts and Prometric says difficulty increases from Part 1 to Part 4, so it is the most approachable part of the Reading test. The mistake that costs points is not the reading. It is not scrolling down to the reply, or running out of time before the five reply questions.
